# Sending money home

How people working abroad send money to Bangladesh, what it costs, the official incentive, the hundi trade and its risks, and what customs allows on the way home, as of November 2026. Each country's own doc gives what is specific to sending money from that country. A raised number such as 3 is an official source; N3 is a news, NGO (non-governmental organisation), research or guide source, listed under "Needs an official source"; a year, like N3 (2022), marks a source older than 2025. "Need to know" marks a point no source has yet confirmed.

## In one minute

A new worker usually needs six months to a year abroad before sending money home, experts told Prothom Alo in 2024 [GM0005]. Money then comes one of two ways. Through a bank, an exchange house or a mobile wallet such as bKash, it earns the family the government's 2.5% cash incentive; in early 2024 banks added another 2.5% of their own, until they stopped in May 2024, and in September 2024 the government's 2.5% was the only incentive paid [GM0001, GM0039, GM0059, GM0122]. Through hundi, the informal and illegal money-transfer trade, an agent abroad takes the worker's riyals or dirhams and a partner in Bangladesh pays the family in taka, often at a better rate, through networks that reach remote villages, and sometimes on credit [GM0007, GM0006, GM0010, GM0011]. Workers who borrowed heavily to go are pulled to whichever channel pays more, because they want to clear the debt fast [GM0009]. The foreign currency hundi collects is used abroad to move money out of Bangladesh [GM0050]. In 2024 a former finance minister put the legal share at only 51% of the money migrants send [GM0045]. The gap between the bank rate and the hundi rate decides much of this, and it moves: in December 2023 a migrant got Tk 109.50 a dollar through a bank, while hundi paid more; in May 2024, after Bangladesh Bank changed how the rate is set, a remittance dollar fetched about Tk 120 with the government incentive, and from late August 2024 banks agreed to pay exchange houses no more than Tk 120 a dollar [GM0001, GM0007, GM0059, GM0112]. When the official rate was allowed to rise in 2024, legal remittances grew and the gap with hundi narrowed a lot, economic analyst Mamun Rashid wrote [GM0108]. In September 2024 Bangladesh Bank's governor said that, for the first time, a remittance dollar fetched more at a bank than on the open market; by December 2024 banks were paying up to Tk 127.50, and that month migrants sent USD 2.639 billion, the most ever [GM0150, GM0164, GM0169]. In March 2025, before Eid-ul-Fitr, USD 3.29 billion came, a new monthly record; bankers put the rise in legal remittances mainly down to less money laundering [GM0206, GM0199]. In May 2025 Bangladesh Bank left the dollar rate to the market to meet the conditions of its IMF (International Monetary Fund) loan; by late June 2025 banks were paying exchange houses Tk 122.70 to Tk 122.80 a dollar (see How the rate is set) [GM0208, GM0219]. Fiscal year 2024-25 brought a record of about USD 30.33 billion, and calendar 2025 USD 32.82 billion [GM0234, GM0257]. In January 2026 the dollar traded at Tk 122 to Tk 123, and people in banking said the government's 2.5% incentive together with the market-based rate had narrowed the gap between the hundi and bank rates [GM0262, GM0260]. In March 2026 migrants sent USD 3.75 billion, a new monthly record, during Eid-ul-Fitr and the war of the United States and Israel with Iran, and in fiscal year 2025-26 USD 35.59 billion, the most ever [GM0288, GM0289, GM0352]. In the 2026-27 budget of 11 June 2026 the finance minister said the 2.5% incentive would continue [GM0307]. From June to October 2026 monthly remittances stayed below USD 3 billion [GM0358, GM0342]. In late October 2026 banks traded dollars among themselves at up to Tk 123.99 and bought cash dollars from returnees at Tk 123 to Tk 125.50, while Motijheel money changers paid up to Tk 128 (see What a remittance dollar fetched) [GM0360, GM0361, GM0362, GM0363]. A government "Probashi Card" for migrants, starting as a Janata Bank debit card, was due to be piloted from September 2026; Prothom Alo reported on 5 September 2026 that it had not yet been introduced and was still being prepared, and in October 2026 the state minister for expatriates' welfare said it was being piloted in four to five countries and that a 3% incentive for cardholders was planned (see The Probashi Card) [GM0329, GM0351, GM0425, GM0426, GM0381, GM0379]. Since 1 December 2024 a migrant can put any amount into the Wage Earners' Development Bond, a government savings bond paying 9 to 12% free of income tax (see Wage Earners' Development Bond) [GM0130, GM0142, GM0137].

## Ways to send money and what each costs

### Banks and exchange houses

Legal remittances arrive through banks in Bangladesh; in 1 to 23 February 2024 private banks brought USD 1.404 billion of USD 1.646 billion, state-owned banks USD 175.5 million, one specialised bank USD 63 million and foreign banks USD 3.5 million [GM0018]. Through a bank or licensed exchange house the family gets the government incentive (see The government cash incentive) [GM0001]. In the first week of June 2024 Islami Bank brought in the most among private banks (USD 159.1 million), then BRAC Bank (USD 52.9 million) and Bank Asia (USD 33 million); among state-owned banks Janata Bank brought USD 60 million and Agrani Bank USD 57.5 million [GM0071, GM0127]. Of about USD 730 million that week, private banks carried USD 540 million, state-owned banks USD 170 million, specialised banks USD 10 million and foreign banks USD 2 million [GM0072]. In January 2025 five banks brought 42% of all remittances: Islami Bank (USD 282.2 million), Sonali Bank (about USD 180 million), BRAC Bank (USD 162.7 million), Trust Bank (USD 161.5 million) and Agrani Bank (USD 135 million) [GM0177, GM0178, GM0179, GM0180, GM0181, GM0182]. People in banking said Islami Bank, a Shariah-based bank, gets the most response from migrants because it has more presence than any other bank in the Middle East and the other countries where most Bangladeshi workers are [GM0183]. In July 2025 Bangladesh Bank data showed that banks which used to compete to buy remittance dollars were bringing in less, and banks with wide networks more: Islami Bank USD 530 million that month, Agrani Bank USD 240 million, Krishi Bank USD 230 million, BRAC Bank USD 170 million, Janata Bank USD 130 million, Trust Bank USD 120 million and Dhaka Bank USD 100 million [GM0239, GM0240]. In January-August 2025 five banks brought 49% of all remittances, in order Islami Bank, Agrani Bank, Krishi Bank, Janata Bank and BRAC Bank [GM0250, GM0228]. The state-owned Bangladesh Krishi Bank rose from 15th in 2023 to seventh in 2024 and third in 2025, collecting USD 1.98 billion in January-August 2025, about 9.25% of the total [GM0263, GM0264]; its general manager Mostafizur Rahman put this down to more than 1,000 branches in remote areas and agreements with bKash, Nagad and other mobile wallets and with exchange houses, so families receive the money at home instead of coming to the bank [GM0249]. For all of 2025: Islami Bank USD 6.20 billion, Krishi Bank USD 3.13 billion and Agrani Bank USD 2.78 billion, together 36.91% of the year's remittances; then Janata Bank USD 2.26 billion, BRAC Bank USD 2.17 billion, Trust Bank USD 1.82 billion, Sonali Bank USD 1.51 billion, Rupali Bank USD 1.22 billion, City Bank USD 951 million and Bank Asia USD 848 million [GM0231, GM0259]. In May 2026 Islami Bank brought in about USD 592.1 million and BRAC Bank about USD 410 million, the most among private banks; the six state-owned banks together about USD 566.5 million, led by Agrani Bank (about USD 242.5 million); and Krishi Bank about USD 470 million [GM0302, GM0303]. In the first 13 days of June 2026 Krishi Bank moved to first place with USD 234 million, ahead of Islami Bank with USD 213 million [GM0310]; for what happened at Islami Bank, see When the receiving bank is in trouble. In fiscal year 2025-26 ten banks brought 71% of all remittances, in this order: Islami Bank, Krishi Bank, BRAC Bank (USD 2.97 billion), Trust Bank, Agrani Bank, Janata Bank, City Bank, Dutch-Bangla Bank, Eastern Bank and Pubali Bank [GM0373, GM0377, GM0378, GM0322]. In July-September 2026 the top ten brought about USD 5.88 billion of USD 8.59 billion (68%): Islami Bank, Krishi Bank, BRAC Bank, Agrani Bank, Trust Bank, Dutch-Bangla Bank, City Bank, Dhaka Bank, Mutual Trust Bank and Eastern Bank [GM0371, GM0375, GM0376, GM0372]. BRAC Bank brought more than USD 2.5 billion in January-September 2026, against USD 2.2 billion in all of 2025 [GM0367]. The nine foreign banks in Bangladesh bring in only 0.3% of remittances, according to a Bangladesh Bank report of August 2026 [GM0345].

A family with no bank account: Bangladesh Bank promotes "no-frills" accounts opened with Tk 10, 50 or 100 at bank branches, sub-branches, agent outlets and digitally, for farmers, workers, the very poor, migrants and other marginal groups, banker M M Mahbub Hasan wrote in April 2026 [GM0293]. Citing Bangladesh Bank's October-December 2025 report, he wrote that there were about 29.23 million such accounts and that about Tk 827 crore of remittances came into them in that quarter [GM0294].

How the family receives it: remittance used to mean cash over the counter, with the family travelling to a bank branch or NGO office to collect it, at more time and cost, BRAC Bank official Shahriar Jamil said in September 2025; he said about 70% of remittances now come digitally, though demand for cash pickup has not gone away [GM0245, GM0246]. BRAC Bank's managing director said the bank was making cash pickup easier through its branches, sub-branches and agent banking outlets [GM0229]. People in banking said in January 2026 that links between commercial banks and mobile wallets such as bKash, Nagad and Rocket get the money to families within a short time [GM0261]. According to bKash, 92% of remittance-dependent families use smartphones and 77% of them are online [GM0247]. BRAC Bank said in July 2026 that recipients can collect at its 315 branches and sub-branches and 1,117 agent banking outlets, and it holds "uthan baithak" (courtyard meetings) with families who receive remittances to promote legal channels and banking [GM0323, GM0369].

An account of the migrant's own: BRAC Bank said in 2026 that a migrant can open an account with it from abroad within minutes through its digital e-KYC (electronic know-your-customer) platform, then use its "Astha" app to transfer money, pay bills and open a DPS (deposit pension scheme, a monthly savings plan) or FDR (fixed deposit) [GM0324, GM0368].

Banks sometimes reward families who receive through them: Bangladesh Krishi Bank ran a remittance festival from 19 May to 15 June 2024 in which recipients were entered in a weekly lottery for 41 prizes in all [GM0173]. The top prize, a gold necklace and earrings, went to a recipient on Tk 1.47 lakh her brother sent from Sweden; the bank said she was drawn from 1 million customers [GM0174, GM0175]. BRAC Bank ran a three-month remittance campaign in 2025 with weekly prizes (mobile phones, Bluetooth speakers, power banks, earbuds, gift coupons) and fortnightly prizes (refrigerators, air conditioners, microwave ovens, water purifiers); the grand prize, a Royal Enfield motorcycle, went to the top remittance customer of its Muradpur branch in Chattogram [GM0252, GM0251]. Krishi Bank ran a 40-day festival for Eid-ul-Fitr from 19 February to 30 March 2026, picking 39 winners by lottery among people who received remittances through it; the top prize, a motorcycle, went to Rabeya, whose husband in Malaysia sent Tk 2.05 lakh to her account at the bank's Bajitpur branch in Shibchar, Madaripur [GM0295, GM0296]. Embassies reward senders too: the Bangladesh Embassy in Rome started a "Remittance Award" in 2019 to encourage sending through legal channels, and on 2 April 2024 gave it to five Bangladeshis in Italy, one of them a woman, as the top senders from July 2022 to June 2023 [GM0395, GM0396].

How long it takes: Bangladesh Bank officials said in July 2024 that a bank must pay out a remittance within 24 hours of receiving it; if the exchange house abroad holds the money, it can take longer, usually no more than two or three days [GM0096]. Exchange houses have held money back on purpose: bank treasury heads said in August 2024 that exchange houses were holding migrants' remittances in hope of a better price from banks in Bangladesh [GM0113].

> **Need to know**
> - What does a bank or exchange house charge a worker in the Gulf or Malaysia to send USD 200 home in 2026?

### When the receiving bank is in trouble

- **Islami Bank, June 2026:** after a customer movement, heated debate in parliament and customer panic, Bangladesh Bank dissolved Islami Bank's board on a Sunday night in mid-June 2026 and took charge of the bank [GM0308]. Many people closed their accounts in panic, and many migrants stopped sending through it [GM0309]. Withdrawal pressure then eased, and with the bank waiving charges for old customers, some who had closed their accounts came back; its cheque clearing and ATMs were not yet fully working in mid-June [GM0311, GM0312]. Its acting managing director Altaf Hussain called on migrants in a video message to keep sending through the bank as before, without fear [GM0313]. In July-September 2026 Islami Bank was again the bank bringing in the most remittances [GM0371].
- **Five merged banks:** five troubled commercial banks were dissolved or merged into a new government-managed bank, Sammilito Islami Bank, on Bangladesh Bank's recommendation, a columnist wrote in February 2026; before that the governor had said no customer would lose out and advised people not to withdraw money in panic [GM0279, GM0280]. The columnist wrote that the five banks had 9.2 million customers, and that pensions, housewives' savings and migrant workers' remittances deposited in them had been devoured under the state's protection [GM0281, GM0282].

> **Need to know**
> - Can a family with savings in one of the five merged banks withdraw them in full from Sammilito Islami Bank in 2026, and what is the process?

### Foreign-currency accounts in Bangladeshi banks

Since the end of 2023 a Bangladeshi living abroad can hold a foreign-currency account in a bank in Bangladesh, called an offshore or international banking account [GM0088, GM0087]. As reported in July 2024 [GM0087, GM0089, GM0090, GM0091, GM0092, GM0093, GM0094, GM0095, GM0128]:
- **Who opens it:** the migrant, from abroad, on the bank's website; or a close relative in Bangladesh on his behalf.
- **What to submit:** proof of Bangladeshi citizenship, proof of living abroad, a work ID card and a photo; if someone in Bangladesh operates the account, that person's details too; when a relative opens it, both give ID, a photo and other documents. Each bank's website explains its account under "offshore banking account" or "international banking account".
- **What it pays:** up to 8.75% a year, a limit set by Bangladesh Bank.
- **Tax:** profit is not taxed, while profit on a taka deposit is taxed at 15% without a TIN (tax identification number) or 10% with one.
- **Taking it out:** under the Offshore Banking Act 2024 no agency asks the source of the deposit, and the whole amount with profit can be taken out at any time.
- **Banks offering it:** The City Bank, BRAC Bank, Dutch-Bangla Bank, Pubali Bank, Eastern Bank, Islami Bank and several others; more than USD 50 million had been deposited, USD 24 million of it at City Bank.

> **Need to know**
> - What is the minimum deposit for an offshore account in 2026, and can a worker on a labour visa in the Gulf open one with his iqama or work permit as the proof of living abroad?

### Mobile wallets: bKash

Money sent into a bKash account earns the family the government incentive of Tk 25 per Tk 1,000, bKash officials said in April 2024 [GM0039]. According to bKash, about 1 million migrants sent a little over Tk 965 crore through it from 12 March to 10 April 2024, and Tk 6,252 crore came through bKash in 2023 [GM0038]. In July 2024 the money-transfer company TapTap Send let migrants in 15 countries, among them the US, UK, Italy, France, Canada, the UAE, Portugal, Germany, Spain, the Netherlands, Belgium, Ireland and Austria, send any amount to a bKash account with no charge; the money arrived at once, with the government incentive [GM0082, GM0083]. According to bKash, in 2025 migrants sent Tk 20,000 crore into 4.1 million bKash accounts, nearly double the year before; migrants in more than 170 countries can send to bKash through 135 international money transfer operators, and the money is settled through 27 banks and reaches the account with the Tk 25 per Tk 1,000 incentive [GM0273, GM0274]. Remittances through mobile wallets rose more than 90% in 2025, against 22% through banks [GM0275]. The bKash app has a remittance statement, which relatives can use when filing an income tax return; from the app they can also open a DPS at four banks and one financial institution, and pay utility bills and school fees [GM0276, GM0278].

Taking the money out, as of April 2024 [GM0034, GM0035, GM0036, GM0037]:
- **At a bank ATM:** Tk 7 per Tk 1,000 for remittance money, still the charge in January 2026, when bKash put the number of partner-bank ATMs at more than 2,500 [GM0277]; in April 2024 it was about 2,700 ATMs of 19 banks, started from the bKash app or by dialling *247#. The banks: The City Bank, BRAC Bank, Jamuna Bank, Trust Bank, First Security Bank, Social Islami Bank, Mercantile Bank, Community Bank, NCC Bank, Shahjalal Islami Bank, NRB Commercial Bank, Union Bank, Midland Bank, Rupali Bank, BASIC Bank, Shimanto Bank, Bangladesh Krishi Bank, ICB Islamic Bank and Citizens Bank.
- **At a bKash agent:** normally Tk 18.60 per Tk 1,000.
- **At one of two "priyo" (favourite) agents:** Tk 14.90 per Tk 1,000, for up to Tk 50,000 a month at that rate.

> **Need to know**
> - Are the bKash agent cash-out charges (Tk 18.60, or Tk 14.90 at a "priyo" agent, per Tk 1,000) still the same in 2026, which banks' ATMs take part now, and what do Nagad and Rocket charge to cash out remittance money?

### Sending to a charity or institution at home

Sending money from abroad for charitable work is often very hard, because the receiving organisation in Bangladesh is told to register with the NGO Affairs Bureau (the government office that approves foreign funding for non-governmental organisations), and that registration is slow and hard to get, wrote Ziauddin Ahmed, president of the Bangladesh USA Chamber of Commerce and Industries in New York, in September 2026 [GM0406]. He wrote that a friend in New York wanted to give USD 50,000 to the Sylhet kidney hospital's foundation during Covid, but the money could not be sent because the foundation was not registered with the bureau, and he asked Bangladesh Bank and the finance authorities not to limit migrants' foreign currency to individuals and bureau-registered organisations [GM0407, GM0408].

> **Need to know**
> - Can a migrant send a donation from abroad to a school, mosque or hospital in Bangladesh that is not registered with the NGO Affairs Bureau, for example to the institution's own bank account, and what papers does the bank ask for?

### Paying for a language course abroad

Since 22 May 2025 a bank can send the fees for a language course abroad without Bangladesh Bank's approval, and the student no longer needs a bachelor's degree; before, only bachelor's-degree holders could send such costs, and only with Bangladesh Bank's permission [GM0413, GM0414]. The student sends the course's admission fee through the bank [GM0415]. People in the sector said a language-course certificate helps in getting a job abroad, especially in Japan, by a route that needs no recruiting agency; a source said the change followed a letter from the Japanese embassy to Bangladesh Bank about Japan's interest in taking workers [GM0416, GM0417]. PKB's loans for language study abroad: see "The PKB loan for language study and skills abroad" in the Services for migrants and their families doc [GM0413].

> **Need to know**
> - Which papers does a bank ask for before sending a language school's fee abroad (offer letter, passport, visa), and is there a limit on the amount [GM0415]?

### What keeps people off the legal channels

A parliamentary standing committee on the Ministry of Expatriates' Welfare and Overseas Employment listed 10 obstacles to sending money legally, as reported in April 2024 [GM0046, GM0047, GM0048]:
- the gap between the legal and the illegal exchange rate;
- workers without valid papers;
- no Bangladeshi bank branches abroad, or too few;
- no Bangladeshi money exchange firms or exchange points, or too few;
- high fees or service charges, and fixed sending limits;
- the power of hundi traders;
- migrants or their close relatives at home often have no bank account;
- money that does not match the sender's income is obstructed;
- income from unauthorised business or work has no legal channel;
- too little awareness and encouragement to send legally.

The committee on the finance ministry advised in April 2024 that migrants be given national ID cards and birth registration certificates so they can send money without obstacles, and that a smart app be used [GM0044].

> **Need to know**
> - Which papers does a bank or exchange house abroad ask for before sending money, and can a worker whose permit has expired still send through them?

## The government incentive

### The government cash incentive

The government pays a 2.5% cash incentive on money sent through legal channels, credited to the family with the remittance; bKash described it in April 2024 as Tk 25 per Tk 1,000 [GM0001, GM0039]. It began at 2% on 1 July 2019 and rose to 2.5% from 1 January 2022 [GM0123, GM0124]. As of September 2024 it was 2.5%, and banks paid nothing on top [GM0122]; in March 2025 it was still 2.5% [GM0185], and people in banking described the government's 2.5% cash incentive as in place in January 2026 [GM0260]. Remittances sent through legal channels are also free of income tax: the chairman of NBR (the National Board of Revenue, Bangladesh's tax authority), Md Abdur Rahman Khan, said in March 2025 that a law was made for this [GM0185, GM0186]. When banks stopped their own incentive in May 2024, Bangladesh Bank's spokesperson said the government incentive would remain and that the government would decide on it [GM0063]. Former Bangladesh Bank governor Atiur Rahman said in May 2024 that cash support on remittances should continue for several more years and even be increased [GM0064]. In January 2026 bKash still described it as Tk 25 per Tk 1,000 [GM0274]. In the 2026-27 budget speech of 11 June 2026 the finance minister, Amir Khasru Mahmud Chowdhury, announced that the existing 2.5% cash incentive would continue [GM0307]; Prothom Alo reported in July 2026 that the incentive was being paid on remittances sent through banking channels [GM0340], and on 13 October 2026 the State Minister for Expatriates' Welfare and Overseas Employment, Md Nurul Haque, said migrants sending legally through banks get 2.5% [GM0380]. For the 3% planned for Probashi Card holders, see The Probashi Card.

### Compensation money

Since 12 September 2024, compensation paid by an employer or insurance company for a migrant who died or was physically harmed while working abroad earns the incentive at the normal rate when it comes through legal channels; Bangladesh Bank made the change on the instruction of the Ministry of Expatriates' Welfare [GM0118, GM0121]. Two conditions: the source of the money must be confirmed, and it must be converted to taka as soon as it arrives [GM0119]. Compensation received before that date does not get the incentive [GM0120].

### Tax on money sent to other relatives

The 2024-25 budget decided to tax remittances sent to anyone other than a spouse, children or parents, banker Faruque Mainuddin wrote in June 2024; he said a significant share of remittances comes through other relatives, so the tax risks cutting legal remittances [GM0075, GM0076].

> **Need to know**
> - Is the tax on remittances sent to relatives other than a spouse, children or parents in force in 2026, at what rate, and who deducts it?

### Abuse of the tax exemption and incentive

In March 2025 the NBR chairman said a businessman had used the tax exemption and cash incentive to bring Tk 730 crore into the country as remittance [GM0187]. Prothom Alo reported that the case was four years old, that Tk 721 crore had come from China, and that the person was Syed Mohammad Faruki Hasan, chairman of Pratik Group [GM0188, GM0190]. According to Prothom Alo [GM0191, GM0189, GM0197, GM0196, GM0192]:
- the money came as remittance over nine years from three Chinese companies, Norinco International Corporation, China Shipbuilding and Offshore International Company;
- it was shown as wage earners' income in his tax file, no tax was paid on it, and the cash incentive was taken;
- tax officials believed it could be laundered or commission money, and tax intelligence suspected it could belong to influential people linked to the then ruling Awami League; his and his companies' bank accounts had been frozen;
- NBR's tax intelligence unit was investigating why it had been left tax-free as remittance.

Faruki Hasan told Prothom Alo the money was legal income: fees equal to Tk 730 crore over 13 years as agent and consultant for Chinese state shipbuilders, which sold 15 ships to the Bangladesh Navy from 2011 to 2024; he said NBR certified it every year he brought money in, and that he did not take the incentive [GM0198, GM0195, GM0194]. Prothom Alo explained that money laundered out through trade, or sent abroad by hundi, can be brought back shown as remittance, pay no tax and become legal in the tax file [GM0193].

### The banks' own incentive, October 2023 to May 2024

In October 2023 BAFEDA (the Bangladesh Foreign Exchange Dealers' Association) and ABB (the Association of Bankers, Bangladesh), both bodies of commercial banks, decided that banks would pay their own 2.5% on top of the government's [GM0061, GM0023]. In May 2024, when Bangladesh Bank introduced a new way of setting the dollar rate (see How the rate is set), banks stopped it: a remittance dollar then fetched Tk 120, and would have fetched Tk 123 with it [GM0059]. BAFEDA's chairman, Sonali Bank's managing director Afzal Karim, said they stopped because the government incentive alone takes the dollar close to Tk 120, and other banks would decide for themselves; Bangladesh Bank's spokesperson Mezbaul Haque said banks had been advised to stop [GM0062, GM0063].

### The Probashi Card

In the 2026-27 budget speech of 11 June 2026 the finance minister announced a special "Probashi Card" for migrant workers, linked to expatriate welfare services, insurance, banking and emergency help, through which migrants would get government benefits and legal protection [GM0305, GM0306]. At a meeting chaired by Prime Minister Tarique Rahman on 18 July 2026 the government decided to pilot it, starting with a migrant debit card [GM0325]. As announced that day by the Prime Minister's deputy press secretary Shahadat Hossain [GM0329, GM0330, GM0326, GM0328]:
- **Who issues it:** Janata Bank, a state-owned bank, issues the debit card in the first phase; in the second phase all card work moves to Probashi Kallyan Bank (PKB, the state bank for migrants).
- **Targets:** 50,000 cards by December 2026 and 200,000 by June 2027.
- **The card:** a "dual currency card" for international payments and banking.

As of 31 August 2026 the Janata Bank pilot was due to start in September [GM0351]. Prothom Alo reported on 5 September 2026 that the card had not yet been introduced and was still at the preparation stage, although the expatriates' welfare minister had announced in February 2026 that it would come soon [GM0425, GM0426]. On 13 October 2026 the State Minister for Expatriates' Welfare and Overseas Employment, Md Nurul Haque, said the card was being piloted in four to five countries, including Jordan, South Korea and some Middle Eastern countries, and that the government plans a 3% incentive on remittances cardholders send through legal channels, against the 2.5% paid now [GM0381, GM0379, GM0380]. The benefits announced [GM0332, GM0333, GM0331, GM0327]:
- **Travel:** airport lounges at home and abroad, special immigration booths, meet-and-greet at international airports, discounts on air tickets and hotels.
- **Health and death:** a migrant service booth at government hospitals and discounts at private ones; the body of a cardholder who dies is carried home free.
- **Returnees:** rehabilitation and insurance.
- **Services at home:** priority in land registration and mutation (transfer of title), utilities, licences, investment, NID (national ID), passport and consular services.
- **Money:** remittance reward points, credit scoring and loans, and sending money directly through the card.

> **Need to know**
> - Is the pilot running in four to five countries the Janata Bank debit card; in which Middle Eastern countries is it running; and in those countries who qualifies, where does a migrant apply, with which papers, and at what cost [GM0381]?
> - Which of the announced benefits actually work at the airport and in hospitals by late 2026?
> - Has the 3% incentive for Probashi Card holders been approved, and from when does it apply?

### Who gets the incentive

Former Bangladesh Bank governor Mohammed Farashuddin said in March 2024 that a large part of the incentive was going to big players and middlemen, who pass part of it to the people sending money, keep the rest, and bring the money in through illicit routes [GM0024].

### Proposals not known to be in force

- **A card for remittance senders:** in April 2024 the parliamentary standing committee on the finance ministry recommended a special smart card for remittance senders and their families, giving migrants' parents, spouses and children priority at government hospitals, government offices and with law enforcement, and smooth immigration and customs clearance at airports [GM0042, GM0043]. On 23 May 2024 the Finance Division told the committee that such non-financial benefits were being seriously considered, with no final decision [GM0410]. Its list: priority at government offices for senders who come home for good, special airport desks for fast reception, immigration and customs, a smart card for senders and their families, priority at government hospitals, quotas for senders' children in schools, colleges and universities, lower work-permit renewal fees, and priority for cardholders for passports and other embassy services [GM0421, GM0422, GM0411]. The committee also asked for more publicity so that migrants actually get the government's low-interest loans and other benefits [GM0412]. For the card the government is now introducing, see The Probashi Card.
- **A cheaper remittance system:** the BNP (Bangladesh Nationalist Party) promised in its 2026 election manifesto to modernise the remittance system for faster, safer and cheaper sending, Selim Reza of North South University's Centre for Migration Studies wrote in May 2026 [GM0298].
- **VIP treatment:** the chief adviser of the interim government said in 2024 that remittance senders should be treated as VIPs, Unilever Consumer Care chairman Masud Khan wrote in September 2024 [GM0126].
- **A tax on all remittances:** in March 2024 the executive director of PRI (the Policy Research Institute, a private research body), Ahsan H Mansur, said the IMF (International Monetary Fund) was talking of taxing remittances; he said he supported it but that it is a political question with different effects on society [GM0027]. He became Bangladesh Bank's governor on 14 August 2024 [GM0111].


## Exchange rates and timing

### How the rate is set

BAFEDA and ABB have set the dollar rate from time to time by supply and demand, on Bangladesh Bank's advice [GM0023]. On 8 May 2024 Bangladesh Bank introduced the "crawling peg", a managed rate, with a mid-rate of Tk 117 a dollar against the earlier official Tk 110; exchange houses abroad then set the remittance dollar at Tk 117 to Tk 117.50 [GM0060]. Bank sources said in mid-2024 that Bangladesh Bank had also eased its oversight of the dollar price and relaxed the rules on bringing money in legally [GM0080]. Its monetary policy of 18 July 2024 kept the dollar at Tk 117 and signalled a market-based rate later [GM0086]. In August 2024 the band for interbank dealing was widened from 1% to 2.5%, letting banks raise the mid-rate from Tk 117 to as much as Tk 120 [GM0110]. On 29 August 2024 the treasury heads of the banks agreed that none would pay more than Tk 120 a dollar for remittances bought from exchange houses abroad, to end the exchange houses' practice of holding money back for the highest bidder [GM0112, GM0113]. Bank treasury officials said in September 2024 that the race to outbid each other had eased [GM0117]. In March 2025 banks were buying remittances within a maximum of Tk 123 set by the central bank, and competition among banks had largely eased [GM0200, GM0201]. In May 2025 Bangladesh Bank left the rate fully to the market to meet the conditions of its IMF loan; at first the banks' treasury officials decided none would buy remittances above Tk 122.50, and the central bank reportedly gave the same advice, banker Mamun Rashid wrote [GM0208, GM0209]. Over fiscal year 2024-25 the taka lost 3.89% against the dollar, from Tk 118 to Tk 122.77, according to MCCI (the Metropolitan Chamber of Commerce and Industry, a Dhaka business body) [GM0243]. By July 2025 remittance and export inflows were high and imports low, the dollar began to fall against the taka, and Bangladesh Bank bought dollars from commercial banks by auction to hold its price; its view then was that the dollar would stay between Tk 120 and Tk 125 for the time being [GM0237, GM0236]. In 2026 the Middle East crisis weakened the taka somewhat against the dollar, so a remittance dollar fetched more taka, people in banking said [GM0290, GM0304]. In May 2026, with remittances rising, banks had a surplus of dollars, and Bangladesh Bank kept buying them to build reserves [GM0299]. The Middle East crisis pushed up the remittance dollar's price; after banks bought remittance dollars at higher prices, Bangladesh Bank stepped up its oversight, and bank officials said in August 2026 that this could reduce remittances [GM0344, GM0343]. Economists Jyoti Rahman and Asif Khan wrote in September 2026 that the system is in practice a loosely managed float within an unannounced band, with Bangladesh Bank reportedly telling commercial banks the acceptable rate limits through informal verbal instructions; when banks cannot legally pay above a fixed rate, they wrote, remittances easily move back to hundi and a black market in foreign currency grows [GM0364, GM0365].

Aggregators, large money-transfer companies that collect remittances from small exchange houses in many countries, had in recent years become the controllers of the remittance market, and banks were reportedly forced to buy at the rate they set, Mamun Rashid wrote in May 2025 [GM0210, GM0211]. When the banks jointly refused a higher rate, he wrote, aggregators cut their dollar sales, and it was thought they might hold dollars to create an artificial shortage [GM0212].

### When money moves

- **The first money home:** usually six months to a year after a new worker goes abroad, experts told Prothom Alo in January 2024 [GM0005]; in March 2025 people in the sector said a large share of those who went in 2023 and 2024 had now started sending money [GM0203].
- **Before Eid:** every year, towards the end of Ramadan before Eid-ul-Fitr, migrants send more than usual; in April 2024 the usual Eid rise did not show [GM0032, GM0033].
- **Before Eid-ul-Azha:** in June 2024, the month of Eid-ul-Azha (17 June), USD 2.542 billion came, the most in any month for three years [GM0081, GM0077, GM0078].
- **Before the national election:** in January 2026 several bank officials said a large part of the extra remittance arriving was being sent to meet candidates' election costs, with funds raised abroad coming in as remittance, and that this would continue until the election [GM0271, GM0272]; people in the sector also linked the December 2025 rise to the election [GM0257]. In Sunamganj, candidates' expense statements showed Tk 4.09 crore of their planned Tk 8.43 crore election spending to come from relatives and well-wishers abroad [GM0232].
- **Eid-ul-Fitr and the Iran war, 2026:** migrants sent more in March and April 2026 because of Eid-ul-Fitr and the war of the United States and Israel with Iran, and kept it up in May before Eid-ul-Azha, people in banking said [GM0321]. Remittances then fell below USD 3 billion a month from June; in October 2026 people in the sector said the war-related instability in the Middle East had not had a big effect, and that sending through banks was easy and the official dollar rate stable [GM0358, GM0359, GM0355].
- **Month-end:** a bank managing director said money that arrived earlier is often counted only when accounts are updated at month-end [GM0056].

### When the system shuts down

During the quota-reform protests of July 2024, a curfew was imposed from the night of 19 July; banks were closed from 19 to 23 July, broadband internet was off for 5 days and mobile internet for 10, and no remittance could arrive through banks in those days [GM0084, GM0105]. Mutual Trust Bank's managing director Syed Mahbubur Rahman said money sent in that time should arrive once banks reopened [GM0085]. On 24 July, the day banks reopened, the SWIFT system (the network banks use to move money between countries) was cut off and many banks got no remittances; they were back to normal the next day [GM0097]. Some migrants campaigned to send money outside banks, a bank managing director said; in early August many announced they would send nothing, then, after the government fell on 5 August, campaigned to send more [GM0099, GM0115]. Remittances fell to about USD 1.91 billion in July and rose to USD 2.22 billion in August [GM0104, GM0114].

Around the national election in February 2026 banks were closed for four days in a row: families could not withdraw remittances from bank branches, though those with an ATM card could take out up to Tk 2 lakh, depending on the bank [GM0285]. Transfers from one bank account to another were stopped, and mobile financial services were limited, so remittances could not be withdrawn through bKash, Rocket or Nagad [GM0286].

## Hundi: how it works and the risks

### How it works

Hundi rings abroad buy dollars from migrants and pay the family in taka through partners in Bangladesh, people in the sector told Prothom Alo in January 2024; Bangladeshi-owned money exchange and remittance companies are also alleged to be involved in laundering [GM0006]. Banker Mamun Rashid wrote in April 2024 that the family gets the same amount in taka, or even 5 to 10% more, and relatives are even given loans in advance [GM0049]. The Saudi riyals, UAE dirhams and other currency stay abroad, where they are used to under-invoice imports, evade tax or move assets out of Bangladesh [GM0050]. PRI's Ahsan H Mansur said in January 2024 that money launderers were buying migrants' dollars abroad [GM0004]. Recruiting-agency owners told Prothom Alo that agency owners also use hundi for visa trading, because there is no legal way to send money abroad [GM0008]. Licensed firms take part too: economic analyst Mamun Rashid wrote in August 2024 that many exchange houses under common ownership had worked with hundi, and, with banks among their owners, gave migrants advance loans and delivered more taka to families door to door [GM0107]. A Prothom Alo editorial of October 2024 said rings in several countries lure migrants with a better price, take their earnings and pay the families through relatives and business partners in Bangladesh [GM0153]. Mohammad Jalal Uddin Sikder of North South University's Centre for Migration Studies wrote in December 2024 that USD 10-12 billion leaves Bangladesh every year through hundi and funds drugs, human trafficking, gold smuggling and false information in India and Myanmar [GM0166].

### Gold bars instead of remittance

In July 2024 many migrants in the Middle East were reported to be buying gold bars instead of sending money, because a bar brought home fetches a little more taka and can cover the return air ticket and even the fare from the airport to the village [GM0109, GM0116]. For how much gold may be brought in, see "How much gold and cash can I bring?" in the Frequently asked questions about going abroad doc.

### Why people use it

- **Rate:** the gap between the formal and informal dollar rates pushes many migrants to hundi, said Zahid Hussain, former lead economist at the World Bank's Dhaka office, in January 2024 [GM0012]; if hundi pays more, money comes that way, said Mustafa K Mujeri, executive director of InM (the Institute for Inclusive Finance and Development) and former chief economist of Bangladesh Bank [GM0030].
- **Debt:** a study by North South University's Centre for Migration Studies found the pull of hundi is tied to migration costs: workers who borrowed to go want to repay fast and use the channel that pays most [GM0009]. Mujeri said most recent migrants went on loans and are sending money, but not through legal channels [GM0031].
- **Reach and credit:** the same study found hundi networks reach remote areas and promise faster, cheaper delivery, and if a worker cannot send one month, the hundi trader sends the money on credit, to be repaid the next month; banks and exchange houses have not reached migrants' doorsteps [GM0010, GM0011].
- **Incentive alone:** a Prothom Alo editorial of November 2024 said the incentive announced when the dollar crisis grew did little, because nothing effective was done to stop hundi [GM0157].
- **Ease:** an unskilled worker in Dubai, rather than go through an exchange house's complicated process, makes one phone call to a hundi operator, agrees a rate and the family is paid, Unilever Consumer Care chairman Masud Khan wrote in September 2024 [GM0125]. Selim Reza of North South University's Centre for Migration Studies wrote in May 2026 that hundi is still popular in many cases because it is fast and often seems more profitable, and that the complexity and cost of legal channels add to this [GM0297].

### After the change of government

The chief adviser of the interim government, Muhammad Yunus, called on migrants in his first address to the nation to send more through legal channels [GM0167]. Bankers said in October 2024 that the campaign of July-August not to send money legally had turned into a campaign to send it legally, and many were using banks [GM0152]. In January 2025 Bangladesh Bank's spokesperson Husne Ara Shikha said hundi had come somewhat under control because money laundering had fallen, and that banks were working harder to collect remittances to settle overdue foreign debts; Mutual Trust Bank's managing director Syed Mahbubur Rahman said less careless bank lending had cut laundering and so brought hundi under control [GM0171, GM0172]. In March 2025 bankers said migrants were choosing legal channels mainly because laundering had fallen [GM0199]; CPD (the Centre for Policy Dialogue, a private research body) research director Khondaker Golam Moazzem said in March 2025 that lower demand for laundering and hundi may have raised legal remittances [GM0204], and people in the sector credited the government's tough action against illegal channels together with the incentive [GM0217]. Mamun Rashid wrote in May 2025 that six banks owned by S Alam had acted almost as a cartel in the Middle East and Far East, collecting migrants' foreign currency and keeping it abroad for laundering or under-invoicing, giving workers advance loans before they went abroad and buying their currency at a higher price; he said this had stopped, so remittances were rising [GM0221, GM0222]. In January 2026 people in banking said the 2.5% incentive and the market-based exchange rate had narrowed the gap between the hundi and bank rates, so migrants were sending through banks instead of taking the risk of hundi [GM0260]. In February 2026 people in banking said hundi had shrunk because money laundering had fallen, and that a bank exchange rate stable for several months had drawn more money into legal channels [GM0284]. In September 2026 economists Jyoti Rahman and Asif Khan wrote that after the political change of 2024 the taka was stable and the reason to use hundi had effectively gone, so formal remittances reached record levels [GM0366]. In July 2026 former Bangladesh Bank chief economist Mustafa K Mujeri said a main reason for the rise in remittances was that less money came through hundi, through which money used to be laundered; the gap between the legal and illegal dollar price had narrowed, and sending and receiving money legally had become easier [GM0314, GM0315].

For the law on hundi, see "Is hundi illegal?" in the Frequently asked questions about going abroad doc; for its use in scams, see "Hundi and money mules" in the Dalal scams doc.

> **Need to know**

## Bringing cash home, and saving it

### Cash at the airport

Bangladesh Bank's governor Abdur Rouf Talukder said in March 2024 that anyone arriving from abroad who declares the dollars they carry will not be questioned, and the dollars can be deposited in an RFCD (Resident Foreign Currency Deposit) account at a bank; he said banks were taking about USD 500,000 in cash deposits a day without asking questions [GM0025, GM0026]. For the amount you may carry without declaring, see "How much gold and cash can I bring?" in the Frequently asked questions about going abroad doc.

### Changing money

In Dhaka's open market in mid-December 2024 a dollar was bought at Tk 126.70 and sold at Tk 127.50, up from Tk 122.50 and Tk 123 ten days earlier, mainly because people travelling abroad in December wanted dollars [GM0161, GM0163]; in early November 2024 banks sold cash dollars at Tk 120 [GM0156]. In 2025 the open-market dollar sold at up to Tk 124.30 in early May and Tk 125 in early June; after the rate was left to the market in May 2025 it traded at most Tk 126 on the kerb (street) market, against at most Tk 123 at banks [GM0207, GM0218, GM0215]. In August 2026 the open-market dollar rose to Tk 128; Bangladesh Bank tightened its oversight of money changers, and on 24 August the Money Changers Association of Bangladesh (MCAB) set the rate at Tk 125.50 to buy and Tk 126.50 to sell, published to all money changers on its website [GM0347, GM0346, GM0348]. On 29 October 2026 money changers in Motijheel, Dhaka, bought dollars at up to Tk 128 and sold at Tk 128.50, while banks bought cash dollars from returnees at Tk 123 to Tk 125.50 and sold at Tk 124 to Tk 126.50 [GM0363, GM0361, GM0362]. MCAB's president M S Zaman said money changers deal only in cash foreign currency and cannot bring in remittances, and that cash brought home by returning travellers and migrant workers is their main business [GM0349]. Bangladesh has about 250 authorised money changers but more than 700 firms doing the business, former ICAB (Institute of Chartered Accountants of Bangladesh) president Jamaluddin Ahmed said in January 2024 [GM0065].

> **Need to know**
> - How can a returnee check that a money changer is one of those authorised by Bangladesh Bank?

### Wage Earners' Development Bond

The Wage Earners' Development Bond is a five-year government savings bond for Bangladeshis earning abroad, introduced in 1981, with profit paid at simple interest [GM0134]. It once paid compound interest; the profit is now paid every six months (Tk 6,000 each half-year on Tk 1 lakh at 12%), and the government can raise or lower the rate [GM0224, GM0241]. From 3 December 2020 it and the two US dollar bonds together were limited to foreign currency worth Tk 1 crore, and because the money could not be reinvested automatically, migrants were forced to withdraw it [GM0135, GM0136]. A notification of the IRD (Internal Resources Division, the government division that sets these rules) removed the limit from 1 December 2024 [GM0130, GM0144]. As reported in November 2024:
- **Who can buy:** the worker himself, or in the name of a person he nominates; government employees at Bangladesh embassies abroad; and, again from 1 December 2024, non-resident Bangladeshi mariners, pilots and cabin crew working in the offices abroad of foreign-owned shipping and airline companies [GM0138, GM0133].
- **What to bring:** national ID card, passport, a copy of a valid visa, proof of employment and a savings account in Bangladesh; a businessman also a trade licence, a professional a professional certificate (as reported in August 2025) [GM0226].
- **Where:** Bangladesh Bank, exchange houses, exchange companies, and the foreign and AD (authorised dealer) branches of scheduled banks; no foreign-currency account is needed [GM0137].
- **What it pays:** 12% on foreign currency worth up to Tk 15 lakh, 11% from Tk 15 lakh to Tk 30 lakh, 10% from Tk 30 lakh to Tk 50 lakh and 9% from Tk 50 lakh to Tk 1 crore, still the rates in August 2025, when there was no upper limit on investment; the profit is free of income tax [GM0142, GM0242, GM0227, GM0137].
- **How long:** five years, reinvested for two more terms if wanted, 15 years in all [GM0134, GM0132].
- **Loans:** a bank loan of up to 75% of the investment can be taken against the bond, but not abroad, and the bond cannot be pledged for someone else's loan [GM0137, GM0225].
- **If the holder dies:** a death-risk benefit of 40 to 50% is paid to the nominee if the holder dies before the bond matures, up to Tk 20 lakh, only if the holder was under 55, and the nominee must apply within three months of the death; the heirs get the principal and profit at maturity [GM0139, GM0140, GM0141].

Bonds come in denominations of Tk 25,000, Tk 50,000, Tk 1 lakh, Tk 2 lakh, Tk 5 lakh, Tk 10 lakh and Tk 50 lakh; profit can be drawn every six months; a nominee can be named, changed or cancelled; a lost, burnt or damaged bond is replaced with a duplicate; and the principal can be taken in US dollars (as reported in August 2025) [GM0418, GM0423, GM0420]. Officials of government and state bodies working abroad on lien can also buy it, on conditions [GM0419].

The US Dollar Premium Bond and the US Dollar Investment Bond already had no limit; under the November 2024 rules they allow one term of investment and four of reinvestment [GM0131, GM0143]. Both are three-year bonds, paying 7.50% and 6.50% at maturity respectively as reported in August 2025, less if cashed early, and their profit is also tax-free [GM0424].

Seafarers, pilots and cabin crew had been barred from the bond by IRD and Bangladesh Bank notifications of August and September 2020 [GM0145]. They send about USD 600 million a year through foreign banks and money exchanges; the Bangladesh Merchant Marine Officers' Association (BMMOA) says more than 12,000 seafarers have no pension or provident fund and work on 4- to 9-month contracts [GM0146, GM0147]. BMMOA's vice-president feared the word "non-resident" in the 2024 notification would cause trouble for seafarers; the IRD secretary said the professions are named and IRD would clarify if needed [GM0148, GM0149].

> **Need to know**

### A fund for when remittances stop

A Prothom Alo personal-finance piece of 4 March 2026, written as the Middle East crisis put remittances in doubt, advised families living on remittances to build a separate emergency fund of three to six months' costs, or, if that is not possible at once, to put 10 to 15% of each remittance into a separate bank account or a short-term savings certificate, where it is less likely to be spent [GM0390]. Its example: a family receiving Tk 50,000 a month with minimum costs of Tk 35,000 would aim for at least Tk 1.05 lakh, three months' costs, reachable in a year by setting aside Tk 7,000 a month [GM0391].

### Land and houses at home

A large part of the money migrants send goes into buying land or building houses, and because the owner is usually abroad, this often leads to land grabbing, forged papers, boundary and inheritance disputes and court cases, often against close relatives, Selim Reza of North South University's Centre for Migration Studies wrote in July 2026 [GM0319, GM0318, GM0320]. On 13 October 2026 the State Minister for Expatriates' Welfare and Overseas Employment, Md Nurul Haque, said the government planned, after talks with the land and law ministries, to resolve migrants' land disputes on priority within seven days [GM0382].

One case, as reported in September 2025:
- **The purchase:** Liton Howlader, in Italy for 17 years, bought 5.75 decimals of land in Keraniganj, Dhaka, four years before, holds the papers, pays the land tax and built a six-room house on it, he told a press conference [GM0383].
- **The claim:** he alleged that after the change of government on 5 August 2024 Alamgir Hossain, known as a land grabber, and associates claimed the land; his family filed a written complaint with Keraniganj Model police station and then a court case [GM0384, GM0385]. Alamgir Hossain said he had bought land in the same plot and that Liton had none there [GM0388].
- **The seizure:** on 31 August 2025, with the case pending, 30 to 35 people with local weapons forced their way in, evicted the tenants and began pulling the house down, Liton said; he came home from Italy to save it and was threatened with death if he went near [GM0386, GM0387].
- **The police:** the station's officer-in-charge said they had his written complaint and would act after looking into it [GM0389].

> **Need to know**
> - How can a worker abroad register land in his own name and give a relative a power of attorney safely, and where can he get legal help with a property dispute from abroad?

### Money left with relatives

In June 2025 Halim Khan, 45, an Italy expatriate from Rajoir, Madaripur, died after asking his in-laws to return money he had sent them, as reported by Prothom Alo [GM0398, GM0399]:
- **The money:** police and relatives said he had given his second wife's father about Tk 60 lakh from abroad over time, and a motorcycle bought with money he sent for himself was put in his brother-in-law's name; his brother said all of Halim's nine years' earnings were with his wife [GM0398, GM0400].
- **The quarrel:** back home, he asked for the money and the motorcycle back [GM0398].
- **The death:** relatives alleged he was called to his in-laws' house at night, beaten and strangled; his wife denied that anyone beat him and said he was brought to hospital ill, and police called the death suspicious and began an inquiry [GM0399, GM0401, GM0402].

### Pension

In January 2024 Bangladesh's ambassador in Geneva, Md Sufiur Rahman, spoke of including expatriates in Bangladesh's universal pension scheme with special priority, and Bangladeshis in Switzerland asked the government to act on the obstacles to sending money into the pension scheme [GM0067, GM0068]. The Bangladesh Embassy in Rome's remittance award event of 2 April 2024 included a session on the universal pension scheme's benefits and simplified registration [GM0397].

## Remittance numbers, kept short

### By year

- **Calendar years:** USD 32.82 billion in 2025 [GM0257]; about USD 27 billion in 2024, 23% more than 2023; USD 21.90 billion in 2023, against USD 21.30 billion in 2022 [GM0170, GM0003]. The World Bank and KNOMAD had forecast USD 23 billion for 2023 [GM0013].
- **Fiscal years:** USD 24.78 billion in 2020-21, the highest so far as of April 2024; USD 21.03 billion in 2021-22; USD 21.61 billion in 2022-23; USD 23.91 billion in 2023-24; about USD 30.33 billion in 2024-25; USD 35.59 billion in 2025-26, the most in any fiscal year, with six of its months above USD 3 billion [GM0014, GM0015, GM0079, GM0234, GM0352].
- **Workers against money:** in 2023 more than 1.3 million workers went abroad, the most in any year and 13% more than in 2022, while remittances rose 2.88%, RMMRU (the Refugee and Migratory Movements Research Unit) said [GM0016]. In 2024 a little over 1 million went abroad for work, Canada-based analyst Mohiuddin Ahmed wrote [GM0409]. Experts linked the gap to hundi and money laundering [GM0006, GM0012]. A bKash remittance head said in September 2025 that about 1.5 crore Bangladeshis live in 176 countries, more than 50% of workers are unskilled, and so remittances are not rising as expected [GM0248].
- **The hidden part:** former finance minister AHM Mustafa Kamal said only 51% of the money migrants send comes through legal channels, putting the total at USD 40-42 billion [GM0045]; research by RMMRU, cited in August 2026, puts the share coming through hundi at 40-45% [GM0350]. Bank officials believed some money laundered out of Bangladesh was coming back disguised as remittance [GM0022]; in June 2024 a bank managing director said many were bringing laundered money back, encouraged by the budget's offer to whiten black money and by offshore banking [GM0074].

### Months in 2024

USD 2.101 billion in January, USD 2.166 billion in February (38.77% more than February 2023), USD 1.997 billion in March, USD 2.043 billion in April (21% more than April 2023), USD 2.25 billion in May (the most in 46 months), USD 2.542 billion in June, about USD 1.91 billion in July (the lowest in 10 months), USD 2.22 billion in August, USD 2.40 billion in September (80% more than September 2023), USD 2.39 billion in October, USD 2.20 billion in November and USD 2.639 billion in December, the most in any month so far [GM0017, GM0019, GM0020, GM0029, GM0055, GM0069, GM0077, GM0104, GM0114, GM0151, GM0155, GM0158, GM0169]. ### Where it goes

- **Districts receiving it, July 2023 to February 2024:** Dhaka USD 5.23 billion, Chattogram USD 1.42 billion, Sylhet USD 870 million, Cumilla USD 810 million, Noakhali USD 460 million [GM0040]. Bank officials said districts with many migrants receive less than expected because many migrants have settled abroad and are selling assets at home to take the money out [GM0041].
- **Districts sending workers, fiscal year 2023-24:** Cumilla 80,572, Brahmanbaria 49,391, Chattogram 43,008, Tangail 38,712, Chandpur 34,956, Kishoreganj 32,907 [GM0051].
- **Poverty:** research by Dhaka University economist Mahtab Uddin, presented in April 2026, found that families with members working abroad are much less likely to fall below the poverty line than families who moved within Bangladesh, because their income is higher [GM0292].
- **What it is spent on:** because most Bangladeshi migrant workers are unskilled, their income per head is much lower than that of workers from competing countries, and most of it goes on family consumption and on land and flats, economic analyst Mamun Rashid wrote in November 2026 [GM0374].
- **Rural effect:** former state minister for planning Shamsul Alam said in January 2024 that remittances through formal and informal channels were softening inflation in rural areas [GM0066].

### Where it comes from

CPD's Khondaker Golam Moazzem said in March 2025 that after the change of government the US and UK, rather than the Middle East, had become the top source countries, and that this big change should be examined closely [GM0202]. Mamun Rashid wrote in May 2025 that money coming through an aggregator is counted as from the country that owns it; many aggregators are US-based, and it is alleged that this is largely why the US shows as the top source, though much of that money is collected in the Middle East and elsewhere [GM0214, GM0213]. In December 2025 bank officials confirmed that money sent from other countries through some US-owned transfer firms had been shown as from the US, and said this had been corrected [GM0268]. With the correction, Saudi Arabia was back on top and the US fifth [GM0230]: in July-November 2025, Saudi Arabia USD 2.05 billion, the UK USD 1.67 billion, the UAE USD 1.58 billion, Malaysia USD 1.43 billion, the US USD 1.03 billion, then Italy USD 830 million, Oman USD 770 million, Kuwait USD 640 million, Qatar USD 590 million and Singapore USD 550 million [GM0266, GM0267]. A Bangladesh Bank report published by April 2026 showed that half of all remittances come from six Gulf countries: Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Oman, Bahrain and Kuwait [GM0291]. In July 2025-May 2026 five countries sent about 62%: Saudi Arabia USD 5.28 billion (16%), the UK USD 4.69 billion, the UAE USD 4.28 billion, Malaysia USD 3.22 billion and the US USD 2.79 billion; Oman, Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain, Singapore and Italy sent another 27%, so 11 countries gave about 90% [GM0334, GM0335, GM0336, GM0337]. Saudi Arabia was the top source in 10 of those 11 months, but in May 2026 the UK sent the most (USD 650.9 million against Saudi Arabia's USD 546.5 million) [GM0338, GM0339]. For the full fiscal year 2025-26 about 60% came from the Middle East, with Saudi Arabia at USD 5.85 billion and the UAE at USD 4.58 billion [GM0370]. People in the financial sector said in July 2026 that Middle East remittances are less stable because more of the workers there are unskilled or semi-skilled, while UK and US remittances come more steadily [GM0341]. Jin Liangxiang of the Shanghai Institute of International Studies said in Dhaka on 24 July 2026 that the many Bangladeshi workers in the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries make Bangladesh especially exposed to the Iran-war crisis, and that if those economies fall into serious crisis over time the workers could lose their jobs [GM0428, GM0427].

### Worldwide

IOM (the International Organization for Migration) put global remittances at USD 128 billion in 2000 and USD 831 billion in 2022, of which USD 647 billion went to low- and middle-income countries [GM0057, GM0058]. Global remittances reached USD 857.3 billion in 2023; India received the most (USD 115 billion), then Mexico, China and the Philippines, and Bangladesh ranked seventh [GM0102, GM0103, GM0101]. In 2024, by the World Bank's count, India again received the most among low- and middle-income countries (USD 129 billion), and Bangladesh was sixth with USD 26.6 billion [GM0405]; by the World Bank's count, as cited in July 2026, it is the eighth largest receiving country [GM0317]. Under the UN Sustainable Development Goals, sending USD 200 should cost no more than 3%; receiving money in India cost 5.01% on average in January-March 2024 [GM0100, GM0129].
