    Migrant Workers in Romania: Dreams of Freedom Exploited for Profit

    Illustration: Igor Vujcic/BIRN.

    Migrant Workers in Romania: Dreams of Freedom Exploited for Profit

    Iulia Hau, Sebastian Viskanic and Filippo Poltronieri
    Bucharest, Dhaka, Milan, Rome
    BIRN
    February 2, 202608:02
    Iulia Hau, Sebastian Viskanic and Filippo Poltronieri
    Bucharest, Dhaka, Milan, Rome
    BIRN
    February 2, 202608:02
    Work visas provide little protection for migrant workers in Romania; many end up moving on or returning home to escape exploitation.

    As a teenager, Rafi* dreamed of studying and starting a business in an English-speaking country, escaping what he felt was a stifling religious conservatism in his native Bangladesh.

    By the time he was 24, however, he had settled for migrant work in Romania, selling land and borrowing from relatives to raise the more than 10,000 euros he needed to secure a job with a transport company called Soaca Expedition, including 8,000 euros commission charged by the Bangladeshi recruitment agency and the 700-800 euros he spent on travelling to India to apply for a visa at the Romanian embassy.

    Rafi landed in Bucharest in July 2024 as part of a group of some 20 migrant workers.

    There was no sign of Soaca Expedition, however. Instead, Rafi was told he would be working for a cement manufacturer called Prebet and put in touch with another recruitment agency, this time Romanian.

    “They told us we had to work more than 12 hours a day, seven days a week, with only half a day off,” he said. “And they demanded our passports.”

    Every year, thousands of migrant workers arriving in European Union member Romania face a similar fate – systematic exploitation under a legal framework that gives almost all power to employers and provides little or no protection for foreign workers.

    Romania is set to amend the law regulating foreign labour; rights groups and labour experts have welcomed some of the proposed changes, but have criticised a lack of transparency in the way the text was drafted.

    ‘Do things like that happen?’


    The Prebet factory where Rafi worked. Photo: Sebastian Viskanic and Filippo Poltronieri.

    “Our country is Muslim,” said Rafi. “I believe in God, but I’m moderate. I’m a moderate Muslim, not a fervent believer or an extremist. But many things happen, there are many conservatives. And that was the main reason I left Bangladesh.”

    “Young people today tend to go abroad in search of a better life, for freedom.”

    However, in the case of Rafi and two other men interviewed by BIRN, they arrived in Romania saddled with debt and were quickly deceived.

    At the factory he ended up at in Aiud, some 400 kilometres northwest of Bucharest, Rafi said there were several dozen other migrant workers. Some told him they had been there for more than a year without any legal status, earning 400 euros a month. Rafi said their passports had been taken away.

    Having refused to hand over his passport, Rafi went to the state Immigration Office to seek help.

    According to Romanian regulations, Rafi was required to work for Soaca Expedition for a year or secure the company’s approval to change jobs. Rafi said the staff at the Immigration Office were of little help.

    Less than two weeks after arriving in Bucharest, he fled for Italy, a common destination for migrant workers from Asia who first enter the EU in Romania. BIRN spoke to him in Rome.

    Prebet, a large construction firm that in 2024 secured more than 1.2 million euros in financing under the EU’s Recovery and Resilience Facility, PNRR, for industrial energy-efficiency improvements, did not respond to repeated requests for comment.

    The Bangladeshi recruitment agency in Rafi’s case, GEIS, said its role was only to process the visa request and that its responsibilities ended when the workers arrived in Romania.

    “I don’t even have any communication with the workers after they reach Romania and get in touch with SAS Enterprise, because they are the main recruiter,” said GEIS CEO Shahadat Hossainn, though this is contradicted by messages exchanged between Hossainn, SAS Enterprise, Rafi and other migrant workers in a WhatsApp group and seen by BIRN.

    Hossainn said he had stopped collaborating with SAS Enterprise.

    Simina Sas, a representative of SAS Enterprise, said work permits had been secured for all workers that the agency placed with Prebet and that she had not received any complaints.

    When asked specifically about the case of Rafi, Sas replied: “Do things like that happen? I’m sorry to hear that.”

    Ioana Cimpan, an administrator at Soaca Expedition, the company listed on Rafi’s work permit as his Romanian employer, initially said the company does not hire workers from Asia.

    However, when confronted with Rafi’s work permit, Cimpan threatened to call the police and hung up. Soaca Expedition was officially registered as having only one employee in 2024.

    In Italy, Rafi applied for asylum, a process that can drag on for years. At the time of publication, he was working without a contract in a restaurant in Rome.

    “If I had found the job they promised me, I would have stayed in Romania,” he said. “But I had only two months left when I decided to move to Italy. At that point, if I didn’t find work, I would become illegal. Being illegal in Romania is terrible; you don’t even have the option to apply for asylum. Romania had just entered Schengen, and I didn’t even know how asylum worked or anything like that. That’s why I left.”

    Professor Silvia Tabusca, director of the Centre for Migration and Human Rights at the Romanian-American University, said such exploitation “would not be possible without some degree of political involvement, at least at the local level”.

    For decades, Tabusca told BIRN, “politically connected and savvy individuals have made enormous amounts of money at the expense of people who, tragically, lose their homes, their lives, and face psychological distress because of promises made by recruitment agencies that are never fulfilled”.

    New legal framework

    In mid-November, Ciprian Vacaru, secretary of state in Romania’s labour ministry, said draft amendments to the law on migrant workers would make “easier” the process of “bringing people in” and identifying those companies hiring non-EU workers, noting that of an annual quota of 100,000 work permits for non-EU employees, only between 50,000 and 70,000 are ever issued.

    Later that month, however, some 30 rights organisations sent a joint letter calling for greater transparency on how the amendments were being drafted and listing issues they should address.

    “We have serious doubts that the new provisions will genuinely reflect the needs and urgent issues of migrant workers in our country,” they wrote. The draft was eventually published on December 23, leaving civil society groups just 10 calendar days during the holiday season to propose changes.

    According to the draft, the recruitment process will be digitalised and conducted via a government platform, though it is unclear whether the workers themselves will have access previously enjoyed only by the hiring companies; recruitment agencies will need to deposit 200,000 euros with the State Treasury as a guarantee for eventual repatriation costs or fines in order to obtain operating licences; such agencies will be required to cover the cost of repatriation if a worker loses their right to work; companies will be suspended from recruiting via the government platform if, during any six-month period, 20 per cent or more of their non-EU employees lose their right to residence, whether because they were fired or for some other reason; and state agencies will only be able to levy fees on employers, not the workers themselves, as per the EU Directive on Temporary Agency Work.

    A Romanian lawyer specialising in migration said the changes contained “some very good regulations” that should make migrant workers safer. However, speaking on condition of anonymity, the lawyer said contradictions in the text “could cause a lot of problems”.

    He said he remained sceptical that non-EU workers would be able to track their own cases via the government platform but that the proposed measures would mean “an agency will no longer have an interest in bringing in 1,000 people who then leave and generate losses”.

    “It will have an interest in placing them properly on the labour market.”

    ‘In the back of a truck’

    Vinith, from Sri Lanka, travelled to Romania in 2020 to work for the fast-food chain McDonald’s.

    After encountering issues with his colleagues, he quit and found a new job in a pizzeria, saying he was promised a new work permit and a contract.

    “This guy promised he would pay my full salary, everything, but he wasn’t paying me, you know? He only paid half, saying things like, ‘I’ll give you the rest next month.’ But I didn’t get a good feeling from him, so I quit the job,” said Vinith, 28.

    By the time he left the pizzeria, after just a month, Vinith had used up half of the 90-day window he had by law to find a new job and obtain a new work permit after leaving the original employer he arrived in Romania to work for. He was hired for a trial period at an airport café, again with the promise of a work permit and a contract.

    Only employers can file requests for work permits from the Immigration Office, a process that requires correctly filling out 16 separate documents.

    Throughout this entire process, the workers have no access to their own file online nor insight into whether their documents were submitted on time or correctly. They aren’t even personally notified if the request is rejected.

    When his trial period was over, Vinith said his boss told him the request was rejected; Vinith suspected it wasn’t submitted on time.

    “That’s when the real problems began for me,” he said.

    The fine for employers who hire undocumented workers is up to 2,000 euros per employee. The penalty for a migrant caught working illegally is deportation under escort.

    His 90 days up, Vinith was effectively illegal; he approached a lawyer, who asked for a fee of 2,000 euros, a sum he could ill afford.

    Instead, Vinith borrowed 2,000 euros from a friend of his father in Italy, topped it up with his own savings and sold some jewellery in Sri Lanka, raising 4,400 euros for a trafficker to smuggle him out of Romania. In 2022, the country had yet to join the EU’s borderless Schengen zone.

    “Emotionally and mentally, it was the first time I felt like I was living a nightmare,” he said. “I had never done anything so risky in my life. If they caught me, I would be deported.”

    He crossed into Hungary, hidden under a mattress in the back of a truck, and made his way to Milan, where he spoke to BIRN. After two years barely scraping together enough money to live on, Vinith decided to return to his wife in Sri Lanka.

    Asylum in Romania not an option

    Also from Sri Lanka, 46-year-old Chaminda arrived in Romania to work in a warehouse, but the pay was not what he had been promised, so he switched jobs as soon as he secured a residence permit.

    Six months later, however, the company was behind on wages and ceased operating, leaving Chaminda jobless and on a 90-day deadline to find new work and obtain a new permit.

    Unable to afford a trafficker, Chaminda found his own way into Hungary and eventually Italy. As in the case of Vinith, it was 2022.

    From pillar to post

    According to Eurostat data, between 2021 – when Romania first opened its doors to non-EU workers – and 2024, Italy requested Romania to process 4,415 asylum claims as per the Dublin Regulation, which requires the EU country where a migrant or refugee first enters the bloc to process their asylum claim.

    In the period 2015-2020 there were just 211 such requests; between 2021 and 2024, France sent Romania 5,294 such requests, Germany 6,412 and Austria 5,409.

    “My last job in Romania was in a city near the border, so I started researching how to cross into Hungary,” said Chaminda. “I found a river and checked where it went. It flowed through Hungary and several other countries. I followed the river until I reached a big road, like a highway, and I got on a bus. That’s how I got here, to Italy.”

    Madalina Petre, a legal expert specialising in recruitment and migration-related bureaucracy, told BIRN: “Many times, the bundle of papers sent to secure a visa interview at the embassy includes a work contract that is different from the one that migrants sign upon arrival in Romania. And often that contract mentions a much higher salary. On top of that, agencies in their home countries tell them the gross wage but never mention anything about Romania’s taxes.”

    For many migrant workers, applying for asylum in Romania is not an option: almost two-thirds of all applications in 2024 were rejected. Those who did secure some form of protection were from Syria, Iraq, Palestine, and Sudan.

    In the first 10 months of 2025, immigration authorities escorted 590 people out of the country; a further 1,907 ‘voluntary return’ decisions were taken.

    Georgiana Badescu of the NGO Legal Resources Centre, which specialises in offering legal advice, took to Facebook in November to ask Romania’s General Inspectorate for Immigration, GII, what it was doing for migrants.

    “Sometimes I seriously wonder how GII thinks these abuses are solved through deportations,” Badescu wrote. “Migrants kept in the shadow economy are ‘solved’ with deportations. Abused migrants are ‘solved’ with deportations. Migrants scammed and defrauded are ‘solved’ with deportations. But are you doing anything else for them? Yes – for them, not for employers, not for yourselves. For the migrants.”

    The GII says returns are a last resort and “strictly administrative”.

    Badescu disputed this, saying: “In fact, it’s practically the only thing they do once someone falls into illegality.”

    Chaminda ended up in the coastal town of Sorrento, southwestern Italy, where he had a friend.

    “There are many restaurants there and some people work illegally there,” he said. “In big hotels they only hire documented workers, but in smaller, less-known areas, you can easily find illegal work.”

    Italy’s National Institute of Statistics estimates that in 2023, more than three million people, migrants as well as Italian nationals, were working illegally in Italy.

    Eventually, Chaminda was able to apply for asylum and, with his papers in order, find legal work in a restaurant.

    “I work here with a contract, and I now have everything,” he said.

    With a mortgage to pay in Sri Lanka, he said he has no intention of returning home regardless of whether or not he is granted asylum, knowing that he still has to earn the money to pay back the bank.

    “The EU cannot stop this because the EU is the one inviting illegal people to the country,” said Sirajul Amin, administrator of HCM Infinity, a major Bangladeshi recruitment agency with branches in Albania, Romania, Serbia and Montenegro. “Why? First of all – cheap labour, so they can exploit the workers; secondly – no need to pay taxes; thirdly – the government doesn’t need to take care of these people.”

    “If they are sick or jobless, the government doesn’t need to pay anything because they are not registered in the system. Unofficially, everybody is inviting illegal people.”

    High-level collusion

    In 2024, Romanian investigative media outlet Rise Project revealed the involvement of former interior ministry employees, businessmen with criminal records and politicians in the exploitation of thousands of non-EU who arrived in Romania only to then be abandoned by the recruitment agencies that brought them there.

    In October 2025, investigative outlet Snoop reported that trafficking and fraud networks disguised as recruitment agencies are charging migrants for their ‘services’, promising work permits but then sabotaging their applications, knowing that an undocumented migrant will find it nearly impossible to secure justice in court.

    Hiring a lawyer costs upwards of 1,500 euros and many simply take the money to open a case and then abandon it. The court process can take years, during which the migrant must live in hiding to avoid deportation and can only work in the grey economy.

    *The names of the migrant workers quoted in this article have been changed to protect their identities.
