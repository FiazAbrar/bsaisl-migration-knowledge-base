# Strengthen labour migration oversight

Protect our migrant workers from fraudulent recruitment and trafficking

Published : 6 November 2026

•

Updated : 6 November 2026, 19:48

• [EDITORIAL](/opinion/editorial)

VISUAL: STAR

The government's decision to make [ministry approval mandatory](https://www.thedailystar.net/news/bangladesh/news/44000-clearances-later-govt-tightens-migration-rules-4316861) for issuing immigration clearance to workers travelling to 12 countries, including Cambodia and Russia, is a necessary response to the growing concerns of recruitment fraud and human trafficking. However, according to a report in this daily, before this decision came, 44,122 Bangladeshis had already received immigration clearance for these 12 countries between January 2025 and October 30 this year. Of them, 27,647 received clearance for Cambodia, Russia, Laos and Belarus, all classified as Tier 3 countries in the US Department of State's 2026 Trafficking in Persons Report. This indicates that these countries don't fully meet minimum anti-trafficking standards and are not making significant efforts to do so. Our authorities should have scrutinised the recruitment arrangements much more closely.

In recent times, our workers promised legitimate jobs in Cambodia have been handed over to cyber scam networks and confined and forced to participate in online fraud, their passports confiscated. Some have also faced torture and demands for money for their release. In Russia, workers promised civilian jobs have allegedly been deceived or coerced into military service and sent to fight in the Ukraine war. The return of the bodies of three Bangladeshis from Cambodia in October, including a 23-year-old who travelled there with a BMET clearance, is a stark reminder of the authorities’ failure.

As this daily reported before, traffickers lured aspiring migrants to Libya with promises of well-paid jobs in Greece, only to subject them to torture, extortion and dangerous sea crossings. In one case, a young man from Chandpur paid Tk 15 lakh to traffickers, and his family was forced to pay another Tk 11 lakh in ransom before he could be rescued. Such cases expose how trafficking networks operate across borders, often with local intermediaries and international collaborators. A recent BRAC study found that 98 percent of cyber scam victims did not receive the jobs they had been promised, while 90 percent were forced or deceived into online scams. Concerningly, 66 percent were recruited through middlemen. These findings show how easily recruiters exploit aspiring migrants through fraudulent job offers.

The latest directive, therefore, should be treated as a temporary safeguard while the government establishes a reliable monitoring and verification system. Before granting clearance, BMET must independently verify employers, job offers, contracts, visa categories and recruitment arrangements, rather than relying solely on information provided by the recruiting agencies. Our diplomatic missions in destination and transit countries should also be involved in checking the legitimacy of employers and ensuring that workers are not exposed to forced labour or other forms of exploitation.

At the same time, the authorities must hold recruiting agencies and intermediaries accountable for deception and trafficking. This requires investigating complaints, tracing financial transactions, and prosecuting those involved. Workers and their families must also have access to clear information about legitimate recruitment channels, employment contracts, and the risks of travelling on inappropriate visas.
