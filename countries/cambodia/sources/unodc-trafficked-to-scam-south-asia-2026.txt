RECOMMENDATIONS

The recommendations offered in this report do not address the full scope of the problem, which must cut across
economic, social, cultural, and political responses. Instead, the recommendations are offered under three pillars of
prosecution, protection, and prevention and are targeted to criminal justice and other stakeholders with a mandate
to address trafficking in persons.

Without urgent course correction
to identify and mitigate threats,
countries in the region are at risk
of the economic, social, political
and reputational fallout that has
been seen elsewhere.

The scope, scale and gravity of the challenge the region
faces highlights the need for a pragmatic response.
Scam operations involve a host of serious crimes with
global impacts, including money laundering, corruption,
transnational trafficking and torture. It cannot be pre-
vented by counter-trafficking responses alone. Therefore,
it is incumbent upon States to determine what responses
can disrupt and dismantle the organised crime groups
perpetrating this crime. At the same time, where exist-
ing counter-trafficking responses are not adequately
identifying, protecting, and assisting victims, there is
a need to strengthen, adapt, and complement those
responses. Given the scale of exploitation associated
with this criminal phenomenon, addressing trafficking
into forced criminality dimensions must remain an
integral component of a comprehensive response.

Countries in South Asia have the distinct advantage of
learning from the experiences and lessons learned of
destination countries in South-East Asia to proactively
identify and acknowledge the risks and vulnerabilities
to trafficking for the purpose of forced criminality in
scam operations, and to urgently adopt a preventative
posture in responding to it. Delays in aligning its justice
and governance structures to respond only heightens
the risk that their countries will succumb to a similar
scale and severity of criminality. Ongoing impunity of
high-level offenders will embolden organized criminals
to continue to expand and entrench their activities across
the region. In short, without urgent course correction to
identify and mitigate threats, countries in the region are
at risk of the economic, social, political and reputational
fallout that has been seen elsewhere.

PROFILE OF VICTIMS OF TRAFFICKING INTO SCAM OPERATIONS

The profile of victims of trafficking for the purpose of forced criminality in scam operations and
the concept of vulnerability to them are different to those relating to other forms of exploitation.
Perpetrators behind the crime type specifically target people with specialized skills, including
computer and language skills, most of whom have been males.

Consequently, most South Asian victims of trafficking into scam operations are young adult males,
ranging from 18 to 40 years old. Job losses during the COVID-19 pandemic, and of the resultant other
economic stresses, have contributed to heightened vulnerability to recruitment into scam operations,
including among skilled, semi-skilled and even tertiary educated people.24

By way of example, a Sri Lankan victim with a degree in human resources told OHCHR that after he lost
his job as a travel agent during the pandemic, he was surviving on manual labour jobs in construction
at the time he was recruited.25 Increased vulnerability owing to the geopolitical instabilities in the
Middle East, which impacts foreign worker remittances, also serve to increase the vulnerability of
many more people to falling prey to fraudulent job offers that lead to situations of trafficking or leave
them with few choices but to participate in criminality in the scam industry.

CASINOS, GAMBLING AND GAMING

The casino, online gambling and gaming sectors are particularly vulnerable to use by criminals for
scam operations.

The experience of the Philippines offers insights on effective State response. The Philippines Offshore
Gaming Operations (POGOs) ­ online gambling companies located in the Philippines but catering to
customers based elsewhere ­ were initially viewed as a potential source of revenue for the country.
However, its operations were quickly harnessed by organized crime to commit scams and human
trafficking at scale.54 The large-scale organized criminality that entered the country through the POGO
infrastructure eventually culminated in the ban on POGOs in 2023 and the prosecution of some high-
level criminals, including high-profile politicians, who were involved in trafficking thousands of people
into them.

The 2023 ban, 2025 legislation, and a set of SOPs in 2026 to address law enforcement gaps and
the experiences leading up to those measures offer significant lessons for countries in South Asia
looking to regulate their own gaming industries to mitigate risks of organized crime.55

As in South-East Asia, local law enforcement in the South
Asian countries can exercise enforcement jurisdiction in
the SEZs by law. However, they tend not to in practice and
may even be under a misapprehension that they need
to seek permission from SEZ governance bodies before
entering those zones as they are governed by separate
regulations. Similarly, labour inspectors may not operate
in SEZs, which may be governed by separate legislation.
In both regions, there is an assumption that oversight
falls to SEZ authorities and that they would report any
concerns about criminality or labour law infractions to
police. There is scope for South Asian governments to
remain vigilant to the possibility that such incidents
would not be reported as well as to the overall risks
of misuse by criminal actors, as has unfolded in many
SEZs across South-East Asia.

Economic challenges precipitated by political upheavals,
environmental and natural disasters as well as geopo-
litical instabilities around the globe all exacerbate the
existing vulnerability to trafficking while also impacting
State capacity to respond to it. On the one hand, eco-
nomic, cultural and social push factors increase the risk
that more people will be lured into scam operations both
as victims and as perpetrators. At the same time, the
diplomatic relationships that determine the country's
economic and political interests in the context of global
instability, including to secure both remittances from
migrant workers in foreign jurisdictions and foreign
investment in their country, provides ample opportu-
nities for organized criminals to exploit and grow their
operations in the region.

The vital relationships that South Asian countries have
with States outside the region whose support is integral
to national security as well as to economic growth have
an impact on how organised crimes, including trafficking
in persons for the purpose of forced criminality into scam
operations, are prioritized and responded to. This is a
double-edged sword, contributing to both the challenges
in confronting the phenomena and opportunities for part-
nerships in doing so. If the scam operations landscape
and the potential trafficking dimensions of it continue
unchallenged, reputational and relationship risks will be
exacerbated, particularly with those countries whose
citizens are targeted for both scam operations and
for trafficking.

A key challenge raised in this respect was that of private
sector partnerships to provide critical evidence. A wide
range of well-known social media and messaging
platforms are regularly used to both target people for
trafficking and for scam operations. Challenges remain
in gathering evidence from companies whose platforms
or products are used to perpetrate crimes but may not
be registered in the country and/or may not share
information because of legislation in other jurisdictions.

Alternative charges to trafficking charges: These
very real challenges mean that many cases are not
investigated as potential cases of trafficking in persons
perpetrated by serious transnational organized crime
groups. Instead, to avoid the complexities and resource
implications of applying complex legislation related to
organized crime and human trafficking, law enforcement
may instead opt to investigate under `simpler' crimes.
As a result, people encountered in scam operations may
be penalized for immigration violations or charged for
fraud-related offences rather than being screened as
potential trafficking, and investigations to uncover con-
nections with powerful criminals may not be mounted.
Where cases are pursued under trafficking laws, it is
very often low-level actors who are prosecuted, such
as recruiters, brokers, transporters, and money mules
who provide bank accounts or property.

High level targets not pursued: In short, the targeted,
proactive approach required to get at the relatively small
number of often inter-connected perpetrators involved
in trafficking a very large number of victims is so far
lacking. As a result, the investigations that take place
of simpler offences or of low-level criminals have little
deterrent or disruptive effect on high-level perpetrators.

  LEGAL FRAMEWORK

THE NON-PUNISHMENT PRINCIPLE

The non-punishment principle acknowledges that where a person lacks criminal intent to commit a
crime ­ including because means have been used to traffic them ­ they cannot be held responsible for
their unlawful actions. Trafficking in persons is a unique and complex crime in the way it operates on a
victim. Traffickers use means against a victim ­ including force, fraud, coercion and the abuse of their
vulnerability ­ to limit a victim's decision-making power and capacity to act. A person cannot be held
liable for their involvement in unlawful activities, where they lack the mens rea. In the context of scam
operations, victims of trafficking into forced criminality in scam operations are not culpable to the extent
that traffickers have damaged their free will to participate.

The non-punishment principle is upheld in both international and regional law.68 The article 14 savings
clause of the Trafficking in Persons Protocol provides a solid basis for the non-punishment principle,
reminding States that they must implement that instrument in accordance with their international human
rights obligations. Article 2 (b) of the Protocol further indicates that its purpose is to protect and assist
the victims of trafficking, with full respect for their human rights. Furthermore, article 25 of the UNTOC
concerning assistance and protection of victims requires States to safeguard the rights of victims. This
cannot be done if victims are being punished for unlawful activities they were involved in as a direct
result of being trafficked. Article 4(2) of the 2014 ILO Protocol to the Forced Labour Convention explicitly
provides for the non-punishment of victims of forced or compulsory labour for involvement in unlawful
activities they were compelled to commit.

Even in countries that have not included a specific non-punishment provision in their domestic counter-
trafficking legislation, there are opportunities to apply the principle in practice. However, its application
depends on victims of trafficking being effectively identified as victims of trafficking. If a person is not
identified as a victim, he or she is at risk of being arrested, detained, deported, prosecuted or convicted for
their role in scam operations, contrary to State obligations to protect victims of trafficking.

COUNTER-TRAFFICKING STAKEHOLDERS IN THE MALDIVES

When the new administration assumed office in November 2023, key institutions were brought within
the purview of the Ministry of Homeland Security and Technology (MoHST), unifying the Maldives Police
Service (MPS), Maldives Immigration and Maldives Customs Service within a security framework, and
placing the Labour Relations Authority also under its oversight.116 The Anti-Trafficking in Persons Office
(ATO), previously under the Ministry of Defence, now falls within the MoHST.

The National Anti-Human Trafficking Coordination Committee (NAHTCC) under the MoHST is mandated
by the Prevention of Human Trafficking Act (Law No. 12/2013) to develop annual plans. Criminal justice
response is implemented by the Maldives Police Service (MPS), which is the agency responsible for
investigating trafficking and bringing cases to the Prosecutor General's Office (PGO). The national steering
committee on counter-trafficking convenes on a regular basis.

In July 2024, a Police Anti-Scam Centre of the Cyber Crime Department was established within the
Cybercrime Department of MPS. Its focus has been on asset recovery and investigation of scams, not
human trafficking per se.

COUNTER-TRAFFICKING STAKEHOLDERS IN NEPAL

The Ministry of Women, Children and Senior Citizens (MoWCS) is the lead agency responsible for
coordination on counter-trafficking. The Central Investigation Bureau (CIB) under the Ministry of
Home Affairs hosts weekly coordination meetings.

The criminal justice response to trafficking in persons is led by the Nepal Police, in particular through
the Anti-Human Trafficking Bureau (AHTB) under the Central Investigation Bureau (CIB). The CIB
is responsible for addressing complex and serious crimes, including cybercrime, and regularly
coordinates with the AHTB on complicated investigations. The Central Cyber Bureau of Nepal Police
investigates cases involving cybercrime.

The National Investigation Department (NID) under the Office of the Prime Minister and the Council
of Ministers is responsible for gathering information on serious crimes, including human trafficking.
The Ministry of Labour, Employment and Social Security (MoLESS) works with the Department of
Immigration on protection of migrant worker rights.

COUNTER-TRAFFICKING STAKEHOLDERS IN SRI LANKA

The National Anti-Human Trafficking Task Force (NAHTTF) under the Ministry of Defence is the
lead agency that convenes its 23 members on a monthly basis (or more often as needed). NAHTTF
was transferred from the Ministry of Justice to the Ministry of Defence in July 2021, with the aim of
improving coordination between investigation and intelligence services.

Criminal justice response involves the Sri Lankan Police, operating under the Inspector General of
Police (IGP) and the Attorney General's Department. A Human Trafficking, Human Smuggling and
Maritime Crimes Division under the Criminal Investigation Department (CID) was established in 2021.
The Human Trafficking, Human Smuggling and Maritime Crimes Division of Sri Lankan Police, which
is a part of the Criminal Investigation Department, is the counter-trafficking focal point. The Organized
Crime and Human Trafficking Unit within the Attorney General's Department handles trafficking cases,
in close cooperation with the CID and the NAHTTF.

In February of 2025, the new government established a Central Crime Investigation Bureau (CCIB) to
operate six divisions, all of which are of relevance to responding to trafficking into forced criminality
in scam operations.

The Ministry of Labour, through its Human Trafficking Unit at the Sri Lanka Bureau of Foreign
Employment (SLBFE), refers cases where trafficking indicators are present to the Human Trafficking,
Human Smuggling and Maritime Crimes Division at CID.

NATIONAL STRATEGY AGAINST ORGANIZED CRIME IN SRI LANKA

Sri Lanka's forthcoming National Strategy against Organized Crime refers to fraud as a form of organized
crime in Sri Lanka. It refers to the use of fraud to recruit victims of trafficking into forced criminality. The
threat is described as follows:

Fraud as a form of organized crime in Sri Lanka is a growing and multifaceted issue, encompassing financial
crimes, cyber fraud, and relationship fraud used to facilitate trafficking in persons. The rise of online banking
and e-commerce has increased online financial fraud, with foreign nationals involved in sophisticated cyber
scams such as investment fraud, romance baiting, phishing, and identity theft. Organized crime groups
exploit informal value transfer systems to bypass foreign exchange regulations, facilitating illicit payments
for goods, drugs, and contract killings, while also enabling tax evasion and money laundering. Fraud is also
used to recruit victims of trafficking through false job offers and relationship scams, ensnaring victims into
forced criminality and sexual exploitation. These highly coordinated, transnational criminal networks operate
across multiple countries, highlighting the complexity and seriousness of fraud-related organized crime in
Sri Lanka.195

  REGIONAL VALIDATION WORKSHOP

A Regional Validation Workshop was convened from 2 to 4 June 2026 in Colombo, Sri Lanka for the purpose of
reviewing and validating the key findings and recommendations put forward in the initial draft of this report. To
facilitate meaningful review and inputs, the draft report, together with their country snapshot, was shared with
national stakeholders in advance of the workshop.

The workshop was attended by a total of 28 representatives from five countries; Bangladesh (n=6), Bhutan (n=6),
Maldives (n=6), Nepal (n=4) and Sri Lanka (6).

Each of the country delegations sent representatives from their counter-trafficking agencies (n=3), law enforcement
or intelligence agencies (n=12), prosecutorial services, being either from Ministries of Justice or Attorney General
Offices (n=5) and from other government agencies of relevance to the issue (n=8).

Across the three days of the workshop, participants provided an update of their domestic situation and response
to trafficking into forced criminality in scam operations. They were also presented with the key findings and
recommendations provided in the draft regional report. Participants were given time to discuss and formulate their
detailed feedback to those findings and recommendations in both single- and multi-country discussion groups,
which were later shared in the plenary sessions.

Participants were also invited to provide further written comments to the report in addition to their oral responses
presented during the Regional Validation Workshop, with a particular focus on their country-specific snapshots.
The inputs received both at the workshop and in written form were integrated into the revised version of this report.

  LIMITATIONS

There are several limitations to this report. The desk-based review was constrained by the limited availability of
official data about this specific crime type across the South Asian region. Additionally, only open-source and English
language information was reviewed.

The number and profile of participants in stakeholder consultations was uneven across the countries, potentially
resulting in slanted perspectives. In each country, the number of people consulted varied drastically from 44 in
Bangladesh (accounting for almost 43% of total participants) to only 12 in the Maldives (less than 12%). Relatively
few individuals (10 people, being less than 10% of participants) from non-government entities were consulted. While
it is appropriate for an exercise of this nature that the majority of participants were criminal justice practitioners,
additional consultations with non-government actors would have offered helpful insights. That said, this limitation is
arguably balanced out by the fact that most of the available relevant literature (on which the open-source research
is based) is from non-governmental rather than government sources.

Finally, only four of the five countries included in this report were visited for in-country consultations, meaning that
country insights are unevenly represented. This balance was remedied in part by the participation of delegates from
the Royal Government of Bhutan at the Regional Validation Workshop and their subsequent provision of written
comments on their country report.
