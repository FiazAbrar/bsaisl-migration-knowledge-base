# 2026 Trafficking in Persons Report: Cambodia

## Cambodia (Tier 3)

The Royal Government of Cambodia does not fully meet the minimum standards for the elimination of trafficking and is not making significant efforts to do so; therefore, Cambodia remained on Tier 3. While there was no explicit government policy of human trafficking, during the reporting period there was a government pattern of human trafficking involving scam centers. Some senior government officials and advisors and law enforcement officers financially benefitted from the trafficking of victims in scam centers and interfered to protect scam center operations and intimidate victims and civil society. As part of a prime minister-initiated nationwide scam crackdown, the government increased law enforcement activity against scam centers through raids and arrests, including of some high-profile Cambodian and foreign transnational criminal organization (TCO) leaders. However, Cambodia enforced laws selectively and rarely held high-level perpetrators or complicit officials criminally accountable. The government did not prosecute any traffickers related to scam center activity, and detained tens of thousands of former scam workers in immigration detention facilities, reportedly with minimal or no screening to determine their potential status as trafficking victims or to refer them to adequate services. The government also prevented civil society organizations from screening or providing victim services to foreign national potential victims in immigration detention facilities, or from providing shelter services without prior government approval, which was rarely granted. Victim services were inadequate to handle the significant increase in potential victims fleeing scam centers, and authorities reportedly inappropriately penalized victims solely for unlawful acts, particularly immigration violations, committed as a direct result of being trafficked. The government reported identifying fewer trafficking victims than in the previous reporting period. Despite this, the government took steps to address trafficking, including large-scale law enforcement operations leading to the closure of many scam centers, high-profile arrests of TCO leaders who owned or operated large scam centers where trafficking occurred, tightening inspections and surveillance of residential locations that may house trafficking victims, cooperating with foreign governments on trafficking investigations, including for child sex trafficking cases, and reporting increased investigations and prosecutions of traffickers. The government also enacted new regulations on property owners and managers to report hosting foreign nationals and drafted a law to increase scrutiny, legal action, and penalties against scam perpetrators and scam enablers.

## Prosecution  

The government maintained inadequate anti-trafficking law enforcement efforts; official complicity remained a significant concern and impediment.

The 2008 law on the suppression of human trafficking and commercial sexual exploitation criminalized sex trafficking and labor trafficking and prescribed penalties of seven to 15 years’ imprisonment for offenses involving an adult victim and 15 to 20 years’ imprisonment for those involving a child victim; these penalties were sufficiently stringent and, with respect to sex trafficking, commensurate with those prescribed for other grave crimes, such as rape. Sex trafficking crimes were often prosecuted under lesser statutes, incurring lower penalties. NGOs reported that in practice the government did not issue criminal penalties under the anti-trafficking law for labor traffickers; instead, it utilized the labor law to issue fines and/or short jail sentences of six days to one month, which did not represent sufficient punishment to deter future crimes or provide justice for victims. The government drafted a law on combating online scams to criminalize recruitment to conduct scams and set increased criminal penalties of up to 20 years in prison and fines of up to \$500,000 for scam perpetrators who engaged in human trafficking, detentions, or where violence occurs.

The government did not maintain a centralized record or database of investigations and judicial proceedings that clearly distinguished trafficking crimes; however, the government made improvements to data disaggregation compared with prior years. Reported law enforcement data, including prosecutions and convictions, included non-trafficking crimes such as kidnapping; therefore, the actual number of trafficking prosecutions and convictions was lower than reported. During the reporting period, the government reported investigating 272 trafficking cases involving 410 suspects (144 sex trafficking cases and 128 forced labor cases), compared with 196 investigations involving 261 suspects (145 sex trafficking cases and 51 forced labor cases) during the previous reporting period. The government reported prosecuting 1,042 new suspects in 506 new cases (223 for sex trafficking cases and 283 for forced labor cases), compared with initiating prosecutions for approximately 380 cases (318 alleged traffickers) during the previous reporting period. The government did not report ongoing prosecutions initiated in previous years, compared with 20 ongoing prosecutions during the previous reporting period. The government reported convicting 569 traffickers (244 for sex trafficking and 325 for forced labor) under trafficking laws, compared with convicting 658 traffickers during the previous reporting period; the government did not disaggregate prior year conviction data by form of trafficking or under which laws convictions were made. The government did not provide sentencing data for convicted traffickers.

The government demoted eight officials allegedly complicit in scam crimes — including a three-star general within the ministry of interior’s (MOI) general department of immigration — but did not report any investigations, prosecutions, or convictions of government employees during the reporting period. Corruption and official complicity in trafficking crimes at many levels of government remained significant concerns, inhibiting law enforcement action and perpetuating impunity during the year. The government maintained the ad hoc commission to combat online scams — led by the prime minister — which had the authority to deploy armed forces and law enforcement officers to combat online scams. In July 2025, the prime minister issued a directive ordering national and provincial officials to eliminate scam activities within their respective jurisdictions or face dismissal. Observers previously reported prosecutors and judges accepted bribes in return for dismissal of charges, acquittals, and reduced sentencing, and there were examples of courts and prosecutors making irregular decisions in trafficking-related appeals cases, including filing of appeals past statutory deadlines. Nationwide, law enforcement authorities often did not effectively investigate or prosecute suspected traffickers, despite credible allegations because of resource constraints and official complicity.

Institutionalized corruption and official complicity in labor trafficking and forced criminality persisted. Senior government officials, family members of senior officials, and business elites with close relationships with senior officials, including two senators sanctioned by the United States, reportedly were involved with and benefited from scam centers or owned properties on which scam centers operated. Involvement of such officials and elites resulted in selective enforcement of laws, which inhibited effective law enforcement action. Observers reported scam centers often received advanced warning of impending law enforcement actions, were not investigated, or required local law enforcement to receive permission from senior government officials to enter, giving compound operators time to adjust operations and move victims. The government intimidated victims and civil society working to identify and support victims in scam centers and raise awareness of the issue, as well as prevented local officials from investigating or holding high-level government officials or individuals with close ties to high-level senior government officials criminally accountable. Government officials actively stymied countervailing efforts, with officials publicly and privately denouncing victim accounts, victim credibility, reporting by international organizations, journalists, and civil society reporting on the prevalence and severity of scam centers. Despite widespread reporting of forced criminality in scam centers, the government has not prosecuted or convicted a suspected scam center operator or owner, including high-level officials. The government took no action to hold criminally accountable the two U.S.-sanctioned senators, despite evidence connecting scam centers to several corporate entities they owned or controlled, including hotels and resorts that remained in operation.

The ad hoc commission to combat online scams announced the national campaign, “Operation XXL,” cracking down on scam centers in July 2025 and escalated activities starting in January 2026. Following the arrest and deportation of the head of the Prince Group TCO, the raids initially primarily targeted scam centers with ties to the Prince Group, but expanded to other scam centers and to smaller buildings where suspected scammers relocated their operations. These campaigns resulted in the closure of hundreds of suspected scam centers and the release of thousands of potential trafficking victims, though local residents and many survivors who fled compounds during these crackdowns reported compounds started emptying prior to police or military arrival — and that other, nearby scam centers remained in operation. Government officials reported referring approximately 80 cases involving 750 scam operators and perpetrators to court during the reporting period. Observers critiqued the government for taking only limited steps to effectively disrupt or shut down such operations and noted that some scam centers tied to powerful elites were not targeted for raids. In January 2026, the government issued official licenses to casinos with a documented history of hosting scam centers.

An observer reported the government conducted law enforcement patrols in locations of suspected sex trafficking, including guesthouses, massage parlors, and karaoke bars, although other observers noted police complicity sometimes impeded law enforcement actions. Authorities sometimes overlooked or denied labor abuses committed against children and adults — including forced labor.

The government cooperated with other governments on law enforcement operations. The government reported the Cambodian national police (CNP) cooperated with foreign governments — including China, Indonesia, Japan, Malaysia, Republic of Korea, Singapore, and the United States — on trafficking investigations and signed a new MOU with Thailand on combating human trafficking and assisting victims. In January 2026, Cambodian authorities arrested U.S. and U.K sanctioned chair of the Prince Group conglomerate — a former advisor to the prime minister — for wire fraud crimes related to the Prince Group’s involvement in the scam center industry and removed him to China. The government continued to cooperate with a foreign government through a law enforcement task force dedicated to combating online child sexual exploitation and other child sex crimes, but did not report if victims of child sex trafficking were identified through this effort. The government conducted some anti-trafficking training for law enforcement officials but did not report which officials or what topics were covered. Some trainings were in partnership with NGOs, and the government relied heavily on financial support from NGOs to conduct anti-trafficking trainings. Most police did not receive training on anti-trafficking law enforcement techniques. The government required the funding of all anti-trafficking investigative work to be conducted through reimbursement, resulting in individual police units having to personally cover trafficking investigation expenses prior to government reimbursement. Some officers waited months for reimbursement — which was sometimes not repaid in full — and the ensuing financial hardships made some police units more susceptible to corruption.

## Protection  

The government maintained inadequate victim protection efforts, especially among those exploited in scam operations.

During the reporting period, the government identified 331 victims or “suspected” victims of unspecified forms of trafficking (239 women, 12 men, 69 girls, and 11 boys) and referred 355 victims to care (252 women, 19 men, 71 girls, and 13 boys). This compared with 405 victims or “suspected” victims identified and 351 referred during the previous reporting period. However, this data included victims subjected to forced marriage, which may not involve human trafficking. This data also reflects that the government did not identify the vast majority of individuals removed from scam centers as trafficking victims, precluding their access to shelters. In some cases, the government prevented civil society from providing referrals for NGO services to victims held in detention centers. Authorities reported 110,095 foreign nationals from Bangladesh, China, India, Indonesia, Pakistan, and Vietnam left Cambodia during this period. Despite credible reporting that many workers in scam centers faced conditions indicative of trafficking, the government did not report any efforts to screen these individuals after leaving scam centers for indicators of trafficking.

The CNP and the ministry of social affairs, veterans, and youth rehabilitation (MoSVY) had victim identification guidelines. However, law enforcement agencies’ victim identification, referral, and repatriation efforts remained burdensome for victims and victim support service providers, particularly for foreign victims who frequently opted to forego the process rather than wait months for victim identification interviews with different government offices. In July 2025, the government launched a new guidebook on SOPs for victim protection on referring victims and “suspected” victims to protection services. However, the new SOPs were not piloted, disseminated to front-line officials, or formally implemented. Front-line officials were frequently unaware of trafficking protocols and did not systematically use these protocols across all populations, especially among foreign nationals; in cases involving Cambodian victims, officials were more likely to follow referral protocols for child victims than for adults. NGOs reported improved CNP and MoSVY cooperation in sex trafficking case referrals. Observers stated police were often unaware that detection of crimes at brick kilns fell under their investigative purview or feared reprisals for conducting investigations or making any arrests; as previously reported, the anti-human trafficking juvenile police (AHTJP) viewed brick kiln inspections as under the ministry of labor and vocational training’s (MOLVT) purview and would only investigate kilns if the MOLVT referred a case to them. The AHTJP did not report any such referrals. The government’s screening of and referral procedures for workers in scam centers was inadequate; officials were reluctant to identify victims exploited in compounds connected to influential officials or advisors, and police were less likely to refer foreign national victims to victim support services if an NGO or foreign embassy did not intervene in the case. Officials often did not follow established victim identification procedures for foreign victims recovered from scam centers and instead often detained and/or deported them without screening for trafficking or providing services. Observers reported that when police did encounter scam center workers, they only conducted screening to establish if workers were suspected perpetrators or were in violation of immigration laws; this often resulted in the inappropriate penalization of victims. Immigration officials imposed fines of \$10 per day on foreign nationals in immigration detention who lacked passports or valid visas, including unidentified trafficking victims. Foreign missions reported numerous obstacles to their ability to assist victims, including lack of clarity about visa overstay fee waivers and accessing repatriation support services. When the government did approve visa overstay fee waivers, it did so only as part of formal deportation proceedings, designating the foreign national as a deportee and barring their access to repatriation assistance available from an international organization. Foreign missions reported receiving hundreds of leads from potential victims in online scam operations in Cambodia; however, Cambodian authorities would often only take action and remove the specific victims if a foreign government or NGO made a direct request.

The government continued to implement a regulation barring NGOs from representing individuals seeking formal recognition as trafficking victims. Under this arrangement, authorities required victims to seek formal identification from the MOI to access protection services.

Although the law specified individuals could not be confirmed as trafficking victims until a criminal trial ended with a conviction, the government could formally designate “suspected victims” to allow potential victims to receive services, including legal services. The government did not report providing direct assistance to victims. The government did not provide trafficking-specific shelter, health care, counseling, legal services, and repatriation assistance directly but provided shelter and some other victim services to Cambodian victims via non-trafficking victim care centers and transit centers. The government continued to operate one victim support center to provide services, including temporary shelter, health care, and vocational training, for trafficking victims. MoSVY also managed long-term care and other assistance for child trafficking victims who could not reintegrate into their communities, but it did not operate any trafficking shelters for child victims. The government could refer trafficking victims to shelter and services provided by civil society organizations but did not fund civil society organization services.

As in the previous reporting period, MoSVY did not allocate sufficient funding to provide all forms of assistance outlined in its guidelines for minimum standards of care for trafficking victims and remained without the capacity available to provide adequate protection services. The government authorized public health facilities to provide free medical services to all migrant workers in Cambodia, including foreign trafficking victims; this policy relieved NGOs of the financial burden of providing medical care to this vulnerable population. The government had only limited services, including shelters, for male labor trafficking victims. It continued to cooperate with an NGO to provide services to Cambodian male victims exploited in the Thai commercial fishing industry. NCCT partnered with a foreign government and an NGO to launch an initiative to train caregivers, social workers, and officials on providing trauma-informed care in release, rehabilitation, and reintegration efforts; the initiative included a report featuring survivor testimonies and a trauma-informed care training manual for caregivers supporting child survivors of online sexual exploitation and abuse. The government continued to provide basic care or assisted in the repatriation of Cambodian victims exploited abroad, but it relied on donor organizations to finance their repatriation. Cambodian diplomatic missions overseas lacked adequate funding and capacity to provide basic assistance to or repatriate victims; some victims were reportedly unable to secure assistance from Cambodian consular services overseas because of unattended hotlines and unresponsive staff.

In January 2026, the government issued a new sub-decree on the management of security and safety in concentrated residential areas which required building owners and managers to report the presence and immigration documentation of foreign nationals staying in their properties within 24 hours of residence, under threat of fines. This decree was designed to prevent trafficking in persons, increase accountability for scam center facilitators and property owners benefitting from hosting scams on their properties, and has been used to uncover scam centers, especially in residential areas. However, the decree’s threat of fines and penalties prevented NGOs from providing shelter to foreign victims, who often lacked passports and valid immigration documents as a result of their trafficking. As a result, some NGOs turned away potential victims requesting help, which prevented foreign victims from accessing basic humanitarian support services — including shelter and food — and placed them at increased risk of being re-victimized in scam centers. The government did not require Cambodian trafficking victims to participate in trafficking investigations or prosecutions to receive protection services; however, the government sometimes presumed foreign national victims willingly participated in criminal activities and forced these victims to undergo police screening to access shelter and victim services. The government did not report if any identified victims participated in investigations or prosecutions.

The government did not have formal policies to provide foreign trafficking victims legal alternatives to their removal to countries where they might face retribution or hardship. There were no legal provisions to offer work permits or temporary residency to foreign victims wishing to remain in Cambodia to participate in civil or criminal proceedings. The government did not provide victim-witness assistance to support victim participation in criminal justice proceedings, and corruption in Cambodia’s judicial system and the lack of any victim and witness protection, exacerbated by a lengthy trial process and fear of retaliation by traffickers, made victims unwilling to cooperate in many cases. NGOs also reported judges had inadequate knowledge of victim-centered and trauma-informed approaches in courtroom settings; this led to the re-traumatization of victims. Victims were entitled to seek restitution from defendants in criminal cases and to file civil suits against traffickers for damages, but the government did not issue restitution for victims as part of criminal sentences during the reporting period, and NGOs reported the slow judicial process for civil suits discouraged victims from seeking civil damages. Victims could only receive compensation from the trafficker through court-mandated actions. Due to inadequate and inconsistent screening and assumptions among officials that foreign nationals willingly worked in scam centers, the government did not effectively prevent inappropriate penalization of potential victims solely for unlawful acts committed as a direct result of being trafficked. Officials also held potential victims of scam operations in detention, and, in some cases, re-victimized them by demanding bribes or ransom for release, food, or access to phones, or reselling them to other compounds.

## Prevention  

The government maintained insufficient prevention efforts, and official complicity exacerbated human trafficking by discouraging reporting of trafficking crimes and preventing civil society from spreading awareness of forced criminality in scam centers.

The national committee for counter trafficking (NCCT) and its secretariat coordinated anti-trafficking activities and met on a quarterly basis. The secretariat maintained six working groups to monitor the efforts of the interagency committee, as well as those of its provincial subcommittees to counter trafficking (PCCT), which coordinated efforts at the local level to mirror the activities of the NAP. The government implemented an anti-trafficking NAP. The government has not reported its anti-trafficking budget since 2020, when it was approximately 2.2 billion riels (\$549,860). As in previous years, NGOs reported the government’s inadequate funding for anti-trafficking activities led some NGOs to cover the expenses of government activities. The government conducted public awareness activities targeting vulnerable populations, including migrant workers and child laborers. The government conducted a country-wide awareness campaign about scam centers, including the possibility of forced criminality. It operated hotlines that could assist potential trafficking victims. The government made efforts to reduce the demand for commercial sex acts, including by producing public-facing materials targeting potential consumers of commercial sex with children. The CNP maintained teams of anti-trafficking and cybercrime specialists in each province to investigate cases involving online child sexual exploitation (OCSE). In 2025, CNP arrested 10 suspects involved in producing child sexual abuse material. The government maintained cooperation focused on deterring foreign perpetrators of child sex trafficking in the tourism industry. It also increased operations targeting the local population that constituted the main source of demand for commercial sex with children in Cambodia.

The AHTJP and MOI continued to operate a hotline and a social media page to report human trafficking crimes. Observers reported hotline staff had limited training and understanding of trafficking and were unable to provide resource referrals for victims. Despite observers’ continuous reports of victims exploited in scam compounds calling the hotline for assistance, hotline staff reported the majority of calls to be sex trafficking and labor disputes. The CNP referred all online scam-related trafficking victim requests to a separate hotline operated by the central security department instead of the AHTJP; some foreign missions reported test calls to this hotline went unanswered. The government did not report identifying any trafficking victims through the hotline. The ministries of labor and foreign affairs operated hotlines for Cambodians working abroad to seek assistance and report cases of human trafficking.

The government did not effectively regulate labor recruiters and did not hold them civilly and criminally liable for fraudulent recruitment. The government reported prohibiting worker-paid recruitment or placement fees; however, it did not report how it enforced these prohibitions. Observers noted the high costs, complex administrative requirements, and restrictive provisions inherent to formal migration drove most Cambodian labor migrants to pursue informal pathways to working abroad. The government conducted labor inspections but did not effectively enforce screening for labor trafficking victims and failed to identify forced labor offenses. The MOLVT maintained offices at the provincial level to monitor recruitment agencies and address complaints from workers, including potential incidents of trafficking. MOLVT monitored and inspected recruitment agencies allegedly involved in human trafficking crimes, but because MOLVT maintained its authority covered labor disputes, not human trafficking, it did not handle trafficking cases or referred them to the CNP or the anti-human trafficking and juvenile protection department. The government did not report providing anti-trafficking training to its diplomatic personnel or its troops prior to their deployment as peacekeepers. The government took some measures to prevent the use of forced labor or child labor, including in supply chains, in violation of international standards. In October 2025, Cambodia signed an agreement on reciprocal trade with the United States that included the obligation to prohibit the importation of goods produced with forced labor.

The MOLVT maintained an action plan aimed at reducing child labor and debt bondage in the service, agricultural, mining, and energy sectors by 2025 through awareness raising, legal action, and collaboration with civil society, funded in part through the national budget. In 2025, MOLVT labor inspectors conducted 889 worksite inspections, identified 21 child labor violations, and imposed 18 penalties against employers. The government also conducted 32 criminal investigations into suspected worst forms of child labor crimes, initiating 92 prosecutions and convicting 157 perpetrators for these crimes. The MOLVT conducted 229 inspections of brick kilns in 2025.

## Trafficking Profile  

This section describes the nature and scope of trafficking based on recent government and civil society reporting. Human traffickers exploit domestic and foreign victims in Cambodia, and traffickers exploit victims from Cambodia abroad. Traffickers exploit Cambodians and foreign nationals in sex trafficking, forced labor, and forced criminality in scam centers run by primarily ethnic Chinese organized crime syndicates. These traffickers use the internet and social media to fraudulently recruit workers from Cambodia, China, and other countries in Asia, Africa, Europe, the Middle East, and the Western Hemisphere for high-paying technical and other jobs abroad and subsequently force them to engage in a wide variety of online schemes, including illegal online gambling, cryptocurrency investment schemes, police impersonation, and romance scams. Brokers and other traffickers move victims through airports, overland, or by sea into Cambodia. Workers sometimes understand at the outset they will be engaging in illegal activity, whereas traffickers falsely lure others to Cambodia and coerce them to conduct scams to pay back travel costs. Once inside the scam centers, traffickers subject even previously willing scam workers to punishment for alleged poor performance and disobedience, including, but not limited to, physical abuse, sexual abuse, pay docking, and debt bondage, and may “resell” those who cannot repay recruitment debts or meet sales quotas to other criminal networks for forced labor in similar fraud schemes, domestic servitude, or sex trafficking. NGOs estimate 150,000 workers are exploited in forced labor in approximately 350 compounds in Cambodia in 2025, although the actual numbers of workers has reduced significantly since the “Operation XXL” crackdown. Large scam centers were traditionally clustered in the port city of Sihanoukville, along the borders with Thailand, Vietnam, and Laos, and in Phnom Penh, and increasingly in more rural areas, where they encounter less scrutiny by the government and NGOs and victims have less chance of escape. Since “Operation XXL”, many criminal scam groups relocated to neighboring countries or dispersed to smaller operations, including in homes, apartment buildings, and shops in urban areas.

Pervasive official complicity, including at senior levels, inhibits effective law enforcement action against trafficking crimes. Senior government officials and advisors financially benefit from the forced criminality and forced labor of trafficking victims, including through their private ownership of facilities and properties that house scam operations. Observers report MOI immigration officials at ports of entry accept bribes to allow entry of foreign workers headed to work in scam centers, and there are cases of immigration detention authorities “selling” detained migrants back into scam centers.

Cambodian adults and children migrate to other countries in the region and increasingly to the Middle East for work; traffickers force many — often through debt-based coercion — to work on fishing vessels, in agriculture, in construction, in factories, and in domestic work or exploit them in sex trafficking. Migrant workers utilizing the assistance of unlicensed brokers are at an increased risk of trafficking, although those using licensed recruiting agents can also become victims of forced labor or sex trafficking. Children from impoverished families are vulnerable to forced labor, often with the complicity of their families, including in domestic service and forced begging or street vending in Thailand and Vietnam. Many Cambodians left Thailand during the 2025 Cambodia-Thailand border conflict. Observers report that traffickers are re-recruiting significant numbers of Cambodian men and boys to work on construction sites, or in fishing boats and exploit them in forced labor on Thai-owned and -operated vessels in international waters. Cambodian men working on Thai-owned and -operated fishing vessels report deceptive recruitment tactics, severe physical abuse, underpayment or nonpayment of wages, restricted access to medical care, and confinement at sea for years at a time without permission to come ashore.

Traffickers recruit women and some girls from rural areas under false pretenses to travel to China to enter into marriages with Chinese national men. Brokers charge thousands of dollars to facilitate these transactions, and the Chinese national men seeking marriages force some of these women to work in factories or exploit them in sex trafficking to repay this debt. Some parents reportedly receive between 6.08-12.15 million riel (\$1,520 and \$3,040) from marriage brokers to send their daughters to China for marriage. Cambodian women serving willingly as illegal surrogates for Chinese national families are vulnerable to domestic servitude. Stateless persons in Cambodia, namely in ethnic Vietnamese communities, are at a higher risk of trafficking. Foreign labor brokers fraudulently recruit foreign migrants, including from Bangladesh, China, and Nepal, to work in construction sites in Cambodia — some of which are affiliated with Chinese national-owned companies — where some are indebted to recruitment firms and experience passport confiscation. There was an unknown number of Cuban regime-affiliated workers in Cambodia, including healthcare professionals, sports coaches, and athletes, whom the Cuban regime may have forced to work.

Proprietors of brick kilns subject many of the more than 10,000 Cambodians living at such kilns, including nearly 4,000 children, to multigenerational debt-based coercion, either by buying off their pre-existing loans or by requiring them to take out new loans as a condition of employment or to cover medical expenses resulting from injuries incurred while working. Observers reported child labor — including forced child labor — in brick kilns still exists, but the number of children employed in the industry has declined due to increased labor inspections and the modernization of brick making, which increasingly demands adult operators. An extensive, largely unregulated network of predatory micro-finance organizations and private creditors contributes to this arrangement by proactively advertising loans to families in vulnerable communities.

Cambodian and ethnic Vietnamese women and girls move from rural areas to cities and tourist destinations, where criminals exploit them in sex trafficking in brothels and, more frequently, clandestine sex establishments at beer gardens, massage parlors, salons, karaoke bars, retail spaces, and other non-commercial sites. Cambodian and foreign national women and girls are exploited in sex trafficking in online scam compounds. Cambodian men and men from elsewhere in Asia, Australia, Europe, South Africa, and the United States engage in child sexual exploitation and abuse in the tourism industry, increasingly facilitated through social media. Traffickers in Cambodia are most commonly family, community members, or small networks of independent brokers. North Korean nationals working in Cambodia may be operating under exploitative working conditions and display multiple indicators of forced labor.
