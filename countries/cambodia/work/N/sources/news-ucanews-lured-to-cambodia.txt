Lured to Cambodia and trapped in scam compounds

                Weak oversight, unscrupulous brokers leave vulnerable Bangladeshi workers facing ransom demands, trauma and crushing debt

Major General Bun Sosekha, deputy commissioner in charge of the Security Unit Commissariat of Phnom Penh, shows fake law enforcement items confiscated from online scam operation centers to members of the media at the Phnom Penh Municipal Police Commissariat on Sept. 22. In recent years thousands of Bangladeshi migrants have been trafficked into such centers to find themslves facing ransom demands, and torture. (Photo: AFP)

By Porimol Palma

Published: October 26, 2026 06:40 AM GMT

Updated: October 26, 2026 06:40 AM GMT

Share

Add as a preferred source on Google

When Talat Mahmud flew from Bangladesh’s capital, Dhaka, to Phnom Penh, Cambodia, last November, he believed he was taking a step toward lifting his family out of poverty.
The 28-year-old Muslim man’s dream was shattered soon after he arrived in the Buddhist-majority Southeast Asian country.
Instead of starting a job as a computer operator, Mahmud was “sold” to a notorious Chinese-run cyber-scam compound, joining hundreds of Bangladeshis and nationals of other countries who have fallen victim to similar schemes.
A part-time electrician and farmer, he had relied on his meager income to support his family of five.
“For months I was tortured and forced to do illegal work. I wanted to return home, but they demanded US$3,000 for my release and threatened to kill me if I didn’t,” he told UCA News.
Mahmud said he trusted a broker who promised him a well-paying job and referred him to a travel agency near Dhaka’s international airport. He borrowed money to pay 700,000 taka (US$5,667) in migration fees.
At the last minute, however, he received a tourist visa instead of a work visa. Having already paid a substantial amount, he felt he had little choice but to proceed.
His arrival in Cambodia marked the beginning of a nightmare. Mahmud said his handlers took him first to a hotel and then to a scam compound, where he was ordered to participate in online fraud targeting people in the United States.
“When I refused, they rebuked and beat me,” he said.
His handlers later demanded $3,000 for his release. They recorded a video of him being tortured and sent it to his family in Bangladesh to extort the ransom. He was freed after the money was paid through a Chinese-run online payment system.

He returned home on June 3 along with 45 other Bangladeshi migrants.

“I am managing somehow, but I am worried about the loans. I don’t know when I will be able to repay them,” he told UCA News.

He said a police complaint he filed had made little progress, leaving him fearful about his future.

Others not so fortunate
Mahmud considers himself fortunate to have survived the ordeal and returned home alive. Others have not been so lucky.

On Oct. 8, the bodies of four Bangladeshi migrants were returned from Cambodia. All four had traveled there through official channels and paid substantial sums, but died under mysterious circumstances.

According to the state-run Bureau of Manpower, Employment and Training (BMET), which issues clearance certificates to migrant workers, more than 16,000 Bangladeshis traveled to Cambodia for work over the past two years.

Media reports indicate that many of these workers were trafficked into cyber-scam compounds.

False promises
Despite crackdowns on the notorious scam industry across Southeast Asia in recent years, impoverished and unemployed Bangladeshis continue to fall victim to traffickers and fraudulent recruiters, according to a Bangladeshi Foreign Ministry official.
A network of unscrupulous brokers has lured job seekers with promises of work as computer operators, hotel staff, construction workers, data-entry clerks and customer service representatives. The jobs often come with attractive salaries and promises of quick departures, but the offers are fraudulent, the official told UCA News, speaking on condition of anonymity.
About 600 Bangladeshis have returned home in recent months, while another 1,800 are awaiting repatriation, the official said.
According to sources, many job seekers paid brokers and recruitment agencies between 600,000 and 900,000 taka ($5,000-$7,000). Some had also obtained BMET clearance certificates.

Returnees have reported that they were instead handed over to scam compounds after arriving in Cambodia.
The Foreign Ministry official said Cambodia was not a legitimate destination for Bangladeshi migrant workers and argued that the BMET (Bangladesh Bureau of Manpower, Employment and Training) should not have issued emigration clearances for jobs there.

Cambodia was also being used as a transit point to move migrants onward to Thailand and Malaysia, the official added.

Law enforcement officials investigating trafficking cases said most victims had traveled with BMET documentation, although some had entered Cambodia on tourist visas.

“Immigration officials in Bangladesh and Cambodia might also be involved in the process,” a police officer investigating the cases told UCA News, speaking anonymously.

“We are investigating how the BMET clearance was issued for those who traveled to Cambodia,” the officer said.

A faulty system
Bangladesh has the world’s sixth-largest migrant population, according to the International Organization for Migration. An estimated 10 million Bangladeshis live and work abroad, sending home billions of dollars in remittances that make a vital contribution to the national economy.
Labor advocates say weaknesses in the migration system and inadequate oversight leave workers vulnerable to fraudulent recruitment, excessive fees, misleading job contracts and exploitation.

Shariful Hasan, head of the Migration Program at BRAC, a leading Bangladeshi nongovernmental organization, said the group had been warning the government about trafficking into cyber-scam operations for more than a year, but its warnings had produced little action.

“There is negligence from both sides — the government and the desperate job seekers. The Bangladesh government wants to send people in whatever ways and wherever it can. On the other hand, some job seekers also are desperate to migrate abroad,” Hasan told UCA News.

Brokers exploit that desperation, leaving migrants burdened with debt and their families facing financial distress, he said.

“The end result is the loss of lives, indebtedness of the migrants and the tragedy of their families,” he said.

A regional criminal industry
Industrial-scale cyber-scam operations across Southeast Asia, including major centers in Cambodia, Myanmar, Laos and the Philippines, have trapped hundreds of thousands of people from dozens of countries, according to a February 2026 report by the U.N. human rights office.

Survivors described torture, sexual abuse, food deprivation, confinement and other serious human rights violations, the report said.
