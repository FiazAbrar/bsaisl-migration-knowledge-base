44,000 clearances later, govt tightens migration rules

Ministry approval made mandatory for 12 countries amid trafficking concerns

              Published : 6 November 2026

•

                Updated : 6 November 2026, 20:54

•

					BANGLADESH

							Mohammad Jamil Khan

Mohammad Jamil Khan

The government has made prior ministry approval mandatory for issuing immigration clearance to workers bound for 12 countries, including Cambodia and Russia, after more than 44,000 Bangladeshis received clearance for these destinations over nearly 22 months. 
The directive comes amid concerns over recruitment fraud, human trafficking, and Bangladeshis being deceived into forced labour abroad.

An analysis of the Bureau of Manpower, Employment, and Training (BMET) clearance portal shows that 44,122 Bangladeshis were granted clearance for the 12 countries between January 1, 2025, and October 30 this year.
Of them, 27,647 received clearance for Cambodia, Russia, Laos, and Belarus. The four countries are placed in Tier 3, the lowest category of the US State Department’s 2026 Trafficking in Persons (TIP) Report. 
In a letter to the BMET director general on Wednesday, the Ministry of Expatriates’ Welfare and Overseas Employment instructed the bureau not to issue clearance without ministry recruitment permission or approval for Serbia, Moldova, Belarus, Kosovo, Russia, Japan, Iraq, Laos, Vietnam, Cambodia, Ukraine, and Libya. 
The Bangladesh Association of International Recruiting Agencies (Baira) has already informed its members of the instruction in a circular signed by Secretary Md Nazrul Islam.
BMET data show Cambodia recorded the most clearances, at 15,235, followed by Iraq with 8,743, Russia with 5,776, Laos with 5,374, and Serbia with 4,255. 
The others were Japan (1,876), Belarus (1,262), Vietnam (1,220), Moldova (241), Libya (113), Kosovo (20), and Ukraine (seven). 
These figures represent clearances, not independently verified departures. 
The October 8 TIP report placed these four countries in Tier 3, indicating their governments that do not meet minimum anti-trafficking standards and are not making significant efforts to do so. The classification assesses government responses, not the direct risk faced by every migrant. 
Bangladesh remained in Tier 2. The report acknowledged increased anti-trafficking efforts but cited shortcomings in convictions, victim protection, and recruitment practices. It also warned that worker-paid recruitment fees left many migrants indebted and vulnerable to trafficking. 
Speaking to The Daily Star, Gazi Md Shahed Anwar, deputy secretary of employment-3 branch of the expatriates’ welfare ministry, said Japan was included to ensure agencies followed its recruitment regulations, including workers’ training and documentation requirements, to prevent complications or misunderstandings. 
For the other destinations, he cited concerns about suspected trafficking, deception, and workers ending up in detention.
WORKERS TRAPPED 
The directive follows repeated reports of Bangladeshis being deceived into jobs, particularly in Cambodia and Russia. 
Bangladeshis promised legitimate jobs in Cambodia were found handed over to cyber-scam networks. Victims described having their passports confiscated, being confined and forced into online fraud. Some alleged torture, intimidation, and demands for money for release. 
Survivor Al Amin said he was promised a data-entry job but given a tourist visa and sold to a scam compound for $1,500, where he was forced into online fraud. 
The bodies of three Bangladeshis, including 23-year-old Sohag Ahmed, were brought home from Cambodia on October 8. Sohag had travelled there in April 2025 with BMET clearance. 
In Russia, Bangladeshis promised civilian jobs were allegedly forced into military service and sent to fight in Ukraine. 
The UN Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine identified Bangladesh among 29 countries whose nationals were recruited into Russian forces through deception or coercion -- showing how seemingly legitimate arrangements could expose workers to exploitation.

A Brac study found that 98 percent of cyber-scam victims did not get promised jobs, 90 percent were forced or deceived into online scams, and 66 percent were recruited through middlemen.
MEASURES 
Migration expert Asif Munier said the directive appeared to respond to trafficking and deception concerns involving Cambodia and Russia, but questioned why regular monitoring had failed to prevent such incidents. 
“This should be a temporary measure, not a permanent solution,” he told this newspaper, calling for proper verification of job offers and recruitment arrangements before clearance. 
“Ultimately, it is BMET that puts the final seal of approval and issues the clearance. Those issuing it must also take responsibility,” he said, adding that officials should not rely solely on recruiting agencies’ information. 
Munier urged verification through Bangladesh’s diplomatic missions, including those in neighbouring countries where necessary. 
He warned that repeatedly permitting and restricting recruitment could confuse workers, push unscrupulous recruiters elsewhere and hurt legitimate jobseekers unless a permanent monitoring and verification system was established.
