Cambodia’s 2026 Minimum Wage Increase: What Foreign Investors Should Decide Now

October 13, 2025

Posted by 
ASEAN Briefing

Written by 

Ayman Falak Medina

Reading Time: 

 4

minutes

Cambodia has enacted Prakas 214/25, setting a new minimum wage for the garment, textile, footwear, and travel goods sectors effective 1 January 2026. Regular employees will earn US$210 per month, and probationary workers US$208, a modest two-dollar increase from the 2025 rate.

The decision reflects the government’s commitment to gradual, predictable wage adjustments, balancing industrial competitiveness with worker welfare. 

For foreign investors, the increase is fully manageable but signals the need to review how wage policies interact with cost structure, incentives, and compliance obligations within Cambodia’s regulatory framework.

Understanding the 2026 wage adjustment

The new rates were determined through Cambodia’s tripartite consultation system involving the Ministry of Labor and Vocational Training, the Garment Manufacturers Association in Cambodia (GMAC), and labor unions under the National Council on Minimum Wage (NCMW). Unions had proposed US$232, while employers sought a smaller rise; the government’s final compromise at US$210 balances economic competitiveness with social protection.

Under Prakas 214/25, the minimum wage covers all piece-rate workers and includes mandatory allowances such as a US$7 transportation bonus, US$10 attendance bonus, and US$0.50 daily meal subsidy or free overtime meal. Seniority bonuses of US$2 to US$11 per month remain in place.

The system operates under the Minimum Wage Law (2018), which requires annual review based on inflation, productivity, and living costs. This legal structure ensures predictability and transparency — critical factors for long-term investment planning.

Cambodia’s industrial context

The garment, textile, and footwear (GTF) sector anchors Cambodia’s manufacturing base, contributing nearly half of total exports and employing around 900,000 workers, three-quarters of whom are women. Most factories operate under Cut-Make-Trim (CMT) contracts, with an increasing share transitioning to Free-on-Board (FOB) models as capabilities mature.

Although productivity trails regional peers such as Vietnam and Thailand, Cambodia’s dollarized economy, stable inflation, and incentive-driven investment regime continue to attract manufacturers seeking predictable operating conditions.

The 2026 wage adjustment, therefore, represents fine-tuning, not disruption, in a sector central to national growth.

What investors must decide

For boards and senior decision-makers, Cambodia’s wage revision should prompt an integrated review across five dimensions: operational impact, fiscal incentives, regional competitiveness, modernization, and compliance credibility. Together, these determine whether operations remain optimized for cost and performance.

Assessing operational impact

Boards should quantify how the wage increase affects their cost base and margins. In labor-intensive operations, even a minor increase can pressure profitability unless matched by greater efficiency. Scenario modeling at different wage levels — US$210, 220, and 232 — helps identify the required productivity improvement to maintain margins. This diagnostic process grounds every subsequent strategic response.

Optimizing incentives and tax relief

Cambodia’s Qualified Investment Project (QIP) framework remains a key cost-management mechanism. Approved investors enjoy tax holidays of three to nine years, import-duty exemptions, and enhanced deductions for training and capital equipment. The Law on Investment (2021) also enables existing companies to expand under Enhanced QIP (EQIP) status.

A board-level review should confirm that registrations with the Council for the Development of Cambodia (CDC) are current and that all deductions are being claimed. Many firms in the GTF sector leave potential savings unutilized. When properly structured, these incentives can offset the entire financial effect of the 2026 wage increase.

Benchmarking regional competitiveness

Even after the adjustment, Cambodia’s wage levels remain below Vietnam’s and significantly under Thailand’s, while comparable to Laos and Myanmar. Combined with a USD-pegged currency, stable inflation, and preferential access through RCEP and AANZFTA, the country retains a strong regional cost position.

However, competitiveness depends on total landed cost, not wages alone. Energy, logistics, and compliance costs should be benchmarked regularly to ensure Cambodia remains the optimal production base or to inform diversification across ASEAN.

Modernizing operations for efficiency

The new wage structure is also an opportunity to modernize. Investments in lean manufacturing, targeted automation, and digital management systems can yield durable efficiency gains. The IFC’s digital wage studies in Cambodia have shown that electronic payroll systems reduce administrative costs and strengthen worker retention. Incremental automation and better data visibility allow firms to sustain profitability despite rising wage floors.

Strengthening compliance and reputation

Adhering to Cambodia’s labor standards now forms part of global buyer due diligence. Transparent wage systems, accurate reporting, and open worker communication reduce audit risk and enhance brand trust. Participation in Better Factories Cambodia (BFC) — the joint ILO-IFC initiative — further demonstrates compliance leadership.

Strategic direction for investors

Foreign companies operating in Cambodia should translate these priorities into localized action. The first step is a wage-impact audit across all Cambodian entities to measure how the 2026 rates affect production costs, including allowances and seniority pay mandated under Prakas 214/25.

Boards should then reconfirm their QIP or EQIP status with the Council for the Development of Cambodia (CDC) to ensure tax holidays, import exemptions, and training deductions remain valid. Renewals or documentation updates can unlock immediate fiscal relief.

Active participation in GMAC and engagement with the National Council on Minimum Wage (NCMW) provide early visibility on policy trends beyond 2026 and help position companies as constructive industry stakeholders.

Operationally, firms should integrate new wage levels into budgets and supplier agreements to maintain financial predictability. Aligning labor management with Better Factories Cambodia (BFC) standards will meet international buyer expectations and reinforce compliance credibility.

By embedding these steps, companies create a Cambodia-specific roadmap that unites wage compliance, fiscal efficiency, and productivity improvement under one coherent management framework.
