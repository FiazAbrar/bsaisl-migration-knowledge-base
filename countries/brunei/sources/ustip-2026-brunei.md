# 2026 Trafficking in Persons Report: Brunei

## Brunei (Tier 2)

The Government of Brunei does not fully meet the minimum standards for the elimination of trafficking but is making significant efforts to do so. The government demonstrated overall increasing efforts compared with the previous reporting period; therefore, Brunei was upgraded to Tier 2. These efforts included convicting traffickers under the anti-trafficking law for the first time since 2016, investigating more cases, increasing the number of individuals screened for trafficking, and identifying more labor trafficking victims. The government created a committee to increase oversight of a Chinese government-affiliated worksite, including monitoring worker welfare and providing trafficking awareness trainings to staff. However, the government did not meet the minimum standards in several key areas. The government did not initiate any trafficking prosecutions or identify any sex trafficking victims. The government only formally identified victims when it pursued an associated prosecution under the trafficking law. Courts did not order restitution for trafficking victims as part of criminal sentences.

## Prosecution  

The government increased law enforcement efforts.

The 2019 Anti-Trafficking in Persons Act (Chapter 230) criminalized sex trafficking and labor trafficking and prescribed penalties of four to 30 years’ imprisonment and fines of between 10,000 and one million Brunei dollars (BND) (\$7,782 to \$778,210), which were sufficiently stringent and, with respect to sex trafficking, commensurate with penalties prescribed for other grave crimes, such as rape. The penal code also criminalized travel outside the country for commercial sex with children, prescribing a punishment of up to 10 years’ imprisonment, a fine, or both. The government also utilized Chapter 120 Section 5 of the Women and Girls Protection Act, which addressed “living on or trading in prostitution,” to prosecute potential sex trafficking crimes. The act prescribed penalties of up to five years’ imprisonment and a fine of up to 20,000 BND (\$15,564), which were significantly lower than those available under the trafficking law.

During the reporting period, the government reported investigating 22 cases (16 for forced labor and six for sex trafficking), compared with 15 cases during the previous reporting period. Authorities determined 16 of these cases lacked sufficient evidence of trafficking and pursued prosecutions for other crimes. The government did not initiate any trafficking prosecutions, compared with one prosecution initiated in the previous reporting period. Authorities continued a previously initiated prosecution of an alleged labor trafficker. The government convicted two labor traffickers under the Anti-Trafficking in Persons Act, representing the first convictions under the anti-trafficking law since 2016. The government sentenced both traffickers to more than five years’ imprisonment.

The government investigated and detained four immigration officials for allegedly using counterfeit and official stamps to facilitate immigration violations by foreign nationals. Authorities linked the scheme to human trafficking and other transnational organized crime. The government cooperated with other governments on law enforcement operations. The Royal Brunei Police Force Human Trafficking Unit (HTU) was the lead agency for trafficking investigations. The HTU and the Trafficking in Persons Assessment Team, comprising prosecutors, law enforcement, immigration, labor, and other officials, met monthly — and immediately upon identifying new suspected cases — to review suspected trafficking cases and determine whether they should be investigated as trafficking crimes or pursued under other laws. The government added the internal security department as a member of the assessment team during the reporting period, enabling agencies to utilize intelligence reports to strengthen investigations. The government provided anti-trafficking training and victim identification to law enforcement, labor officials, and hotel staff; law enforcement officials also attended numerous anti-trafficking trainings and workshops organized or funded by foreign governments, and international or multilateral organizations. The government did not report training judiciary officials on approaches to domestic human trafficking cases. Previous reports indicated investigators sometimes required proof of physical force, fraud, or coercion to initiate referrals as trafficking crimes. As a result, officials may not have investigated or prosecuted — as trafficking cases — cases in which the alleged traffickers used non-physical forms of coercion.

## Protection  

The government slightly increased efforts to identify and protect victims.

During the reporting period, the government identified nine adult male foreign labor trafficking victims but did not identify any sex trafficking victims, compared with identifying three adult male labor trafficking victims during the previous reporting period. Authorities reported conducting 466 preliminary screenings for trafficking indicators and referring 22 cases to the trafficking in persons assessment team for further review, compared with 123 preliminary screenings and 15 cases assessed in the previous reporting period. The government had SOPs for victim identification, referral, and protection. Through a two-stage identification process, officials referred the cases of potential victims, who displayed indicators of trafficking during an initial screening, to the Trafficking in Persons Assessment Team, which conducted a review of the victim’s case and provided an assessment to the attorney general’s chambers (AGC). If the AGC declined to prosecute a potential victim’s case under the trafficking law, the government did not formally identify these individuals as trafficking victims. By making formal victim identification contingent on the existence of a prosecution under the trafficking law, the government likely failed to identify and appropriately protect some victims. The government provided potential victims with a range of services while the AGC decided whether or not to prosecute. Officials may not have adequately implemented the SOPs in cases involving individuals in commercial sex or workers who had left their employers.

The government referred all nine labor trafficking victims to government-funded assistance and continued to provide assistance to nine victims associated with the two cases that resulted in convictions. Government services were available to both identified and potential victims, including shelter, medical care, basic necessities, psychological support, and counseling. During the reporting period, the government spent 20,515 BND (\$15,965) on direct victim care and assistance, as compared with 27,255 BND (\$21,210) allocated in the previous reporting period. The government operated: two dedicated shelters for adult trafficking victims (one for men and one for women), each with capacity for 17 adults, and two homes for women and children in need of protection, including potential child trafficking victims. The shelters could accommodate persons with disabilities. Victims were unable to leave the shelters at will; authorities required victims to either be chaperoned or reachable via phone at all times. Authorities allowed foreign potential victims who declined to reside in shelters to remain in alternative housing, when it was assessed as safe, while receiving support.

The government provided work permits to identified and potential trafficking victims for the duration of trafficking investigations, prosecutions and court proceedings, and issued permits to four potential victims during the reporting period. However, the government did not have formal policies to provide foreign trafficking victims legal alternatives to their removal to countries where they might face retribution or hardship. The government could issue restitution as part of criminal sentences and compensation through fines under the anti-trafficking law; but victims received no financial benefit. Courts imposed fines on the two convicted labor traffickers but ordered no restitution as neither had financial means to pay; in default, courts sentenced them to imprisonment and whipping. The government’s anti-trafficking in persons fund could be used to pay trafficking victims, but the government has never reported disbursing these funds to victims.

The Anti-Trafficking in Persons Act exempted trafficking victims from criminal prosecution for illegal entry, unlawful residence, or fraudulent travel or identity documents when these acts were a direct consequence of trafficking. Previously the government placed announcements in newspapers and official websites seeking public assistance to trace migrant workers who left their employers under contract — potentially due to exploitation — referring to them as “runaway workers.” These announcements may have caused victims to fear communicating with law enforcement officers and led to authorities penalizing unidentified victims for immigration offenses committed as a direct result of being trafficked. The government has since directed state-run news agencies not to use the term.

## Prevention  

The government increased prevention efforts.

The government maintained an interagency task force and implemented its 2025-2029 NAP. During the reporting period, HTU joined the security working committee on foreign labor under the national security committee to address security issues related to foreign labor. The government conducted numerous awareness raising efforts, and law enforcement agencies spent approximately 10,000 BND (\$7,782) on outreach efforts during the reporting period. The government also produced and distributed multilingual guidelines for employers and employees on working hours, salary payment, and leave entitlements in the private sector. The government funded and operated a trafficking and labor hotline. The ministry of home affairs department of labor had an online mechanism for workers to submit complaints; information received through these hotlines resulted in 250 preliminary screenings for trafficking indicators.

The government made efforts to reduce the demand for commercial sex acts. The government took some measures to regulate labor recruiters and hold them civilly liable for fraudulent recruitment. Brunei’s 2004 Employment Agencies Order (EAO) mandated licensing and regulation of recruitment agents. The EAO prohibited agencies from charging recruitment fees unless government regulation provided otherwise; the government permitted agencies to charge up to a \$2,000 BND (\$1,556) recruitment fee for migrant workers and required employers to pay the fees. Observers noted recruitment fees could be higher than this cap, with employers shifting some portion of recruitment fees onto the migrant workers. The government reported taking enforcement action under the EAO against six entities and individuals operating as unlicensed recruitment agencies, individual recruiters, or sub-brokers and against four licensed employment agencies that charged recruitment fees exceeding the established limit for domestic workers. Penalties ranged from \$1,000 to \$2,000 BND (\$778 to \$1,556) for unlicensed recruiters; agencies that overcharged recruitment fees received a warning letter and were required to refund the excess fees. In 2023, the government began implementing a phased minimum wage policy that initially applied to workers in the banking, finance, and technology sectors. Despite previous statements that domestic and construction workers would be included in subsequent phases, by the end of the reporting period the government had not extended the policy to either sector. The government required migrant workers to receive approval from their employers before departing Brunei to avoid incurring a \$600 BND (\$467) exit fine. Observers reported this rule left workers vulnerable to extortion or debt-based coercion by traffickers.

The government took some measures to prevent the use of forced labor or child labor in violation of international standards. It maintained labor recruitment MOUs with two foreign governments. The government conducted anti-trafficking awareness sessions for Chinese nationals employed on a government-affiliated project in Pulau Muara Besar, targeting managers and staff with information on trafficking indicators and reporting mechanisms.

## Trafficking Profile  

This section describes the nature and scope of trafficking based on recent government and civil society reporting. Bruneian and foreign traffickers exploit foreign nationals in Brunei in labor trafficking and, to a lesser extent, sex trafficking, and Bruneian nationals remain vulnerable to sex trafficking abroad. Traffickers exploit migrant workers in Brunei in construction, agriculture, domestic work, and other low wage sectors, including through the use of deceptive recruitment, contract switching, debt-based coercion, unauthorized wage deductions, non-payment of wages, passport retention, and restrictions on movement. Some employers and recruitment agencies continue to retain migrant workers’ passports. Foreign workers from Bangladesh and Timor Leste recruited through labor recruitment MOUs also remained at risk of exploitation when employers or intermediaries violated recruitment rules or employment contracts. Traffickers exploit foreign women and girls in sex trafficking in Brunei, including in massage parlors and other venues, often through fraudulent job offers or debt-based coercion. Previous reports indicated many migrant workers from Bangladesh and Indonesia incur large debts from recruiters in Brunei and their home countries, making them vulnerable to exploitative employers. Additionally, some recruiters demand thousands of dollars in recruitment fees, but, upon workers’ arrival in Brunei, fail to find them jobs. These workers, most of whom have significant recruitment debts, are vulnerable to traffickers and often resort to “freelancing,” which is illegal in Brunei. Trafficking syndicates may use Brunei as a transit point between other countries in the region. Chinese nationals may have faced abusive or exploitative labor conditions in Brunei at a Chinese state-affiliated project. Chinese- or Vietnamese-owned fishing vessels may exploit labor trafficking victims while conducting illegal, unreported, and unregulated fishing in Bruneian waters.
