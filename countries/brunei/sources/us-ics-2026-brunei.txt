			2026 Investment Climate Statements: Brunei

Bureau of Economic, Energy, and Business Affairs

							Download Report

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Brunei is a small, energy-rich sultanate on the northern coast of Borneo in Southeast Asia. Brunei boasts a well-educated, predominantly English-speaking population, good infrastructure, and a government focused on attracting foreign investment and projects. In parallel with Brunei’s efforts to attract foreign investment and create an open and transparent investment regime, the country has taken steps to streamline the process for entrepreneurs and investors to establish businesses.

Brunei’s economy remains highly dependent on hydrocarbons. Despite ongoing diversification efforts, the combined upstream oil and gas sector and downstream petrochemical industry account for approximately 80 percent of exports and 60 percent of GDP. The revenue from Brunei’s energy sector provides a comfortable quality of life for Bruneians by regional standards.

Brunei’s citizens:

Are not required to pay taxes

Have access to free education through the university level

Have free medical care and

Have subsidized housing and fuel

Brunei has a stable political climate and generally avoids major natural disasters. It offers a welcoming climate for potential investors due to:

Its central location in Southeast Asia

Good telecommunications and airline connections

Business tax credits in specified sectors and

No income, sales, or export taxes

Sectors offering U.S. business opportunities in Brunei include:

Aerospace and defense

Agribusiness

Construction

Petrochemicals

Energy and mining

Environmental technologies

Food processing and packaging

Franchising

Health technologies

Information and communication

Digital finance and

Services

In March 2025, Brunei introduced a five-year, multi-entry, long-term visa for foreigners with family, business, or professional ties to the country, including categories for business and high-demand professionals. This initiative aims to boost investment and integration alongside a new policy allowing foreign business owners and technical professionals to renew work permits up to age 65, raising the previous limit of 60.

In 2019, Brunei commenced implementation of its Sharia Penal Code (SPC), introducing the possibility of corporal and capital punishments including, under certain evidentiary circumstances, amputation for theft and death by stoning for offenses including sodomy, adultery, and blasphemy. The SPC functions in parallel with Brunei’s English common law-based civil penal code. Government officials emphasize that the most severe punishments are highly improbable due to the very high standard of proof required for conviction under the SPC. While the SPC does not specifically address business-related matters, potential investors should be aware that the SPC generated global controversy when it was implemented due to its draconian punishments. The Sultan declared a moratorium on the death penalty for Sharia crimes in response to the outcry, and there have been no recorded incidents of U.S. citizens or U.S. investments directly affected by Sharia law.

Section 1.

					 Openness to, and Restrictions upon, Foreign Investment							

Policies toward Foreign Direct Investment

Brunei has an open economy that welcomes foreign trade and foreign direct investment (FDI) as the government continues its economic diversification efforts to limit its long reliance on oil and gas exports.

FDI is important to Brunei as a central component of its economic diversification strategy under Wawasan Brunei 2035, Brunei’s 30-year national development plan. Brunei encourages FDI in the domestic economy through various investment incentives offered by the Brunei Economic Development Board (BEDB), including tax incentives, industrial land, and streamlined licensing. BEDB, under the Ministry of Economy, Trade, and Industry, is the government’s frontline agency that promotes and facilitates foreign investment into Brunei. BEDB assists investors in:

Identifying opportunities

Facilitating regulatory approvals

Coordinating with government agencies

Providing information on incentives and

Supporting project implementation

Brunei amended its laws to make establishing a business easier and quicker for entrepreneurs and investors. The Business License Act (Amendment) of 2016 exempts several business activities from licensing requirements, including:

Eateries, boarding and lodging houses, or other places of public resort

Street vendors and stalls

Motor vehicle dealers

Petrol stations, including places for storing petrol and flammable material

Timber store and furniture factories and

Retail shops and workshops

The Miscellaneous License Act (Amendment) of 2015 reduced the wait times for new business registrants to start operations, with low-risk businesses like eateries and shops able to start operations immediately.

Limits on Foreign Control and Right to Private Ownership and Establishment

While Brunei does not impose broad economy-wide statutory caps on foreign ownership, some sectors have had challenges obtaining licenses without a local partner. Brunei regulates foreign investment through sector-specific laws and administrative approvals. Foreign ownership of land remains highly restricted because the government owns most land.

The Companies Act requires locally incorporated companies to have at least one of the two directors—or if more than two directors, at least two of them—to be residents of Brunei, but companies may request exceptions. The corporate income tax rate is the same whether the company is locally or foreign-owned and managed.

All businesses in Brunei must be registered with the Registry of Companies and Business Names. Foreign investors can fully own incorporated companies, foreign company branches, or representative offices, but not sole proprietorships or partnerships. Brunei maintains its right to screen investment to ensure that foreign investments do not contradict or negatively impact the overall National Development Plan or the national interest.

More information on incorporation of companies can be found on the 

Ministry of Finance and Economy website

.

Business Facilitation

BEDB is the government statutory body established to facilitate a conducive business environment. BEDB provides business support services and advice on starting up or growing a business, which includes how to obtain permits, approvals, and licenses. Guidelines and information on government services for businesses can be found on the 

BusinessBN portal

, Brunei’s principal online business registration portal. Both domestic and foreign investors may use the online system to establish locally registered companies, although foreign investors must still satisfy applicable immigration, licensing, and sector-specific regulatory requirements.

Business registration can be done online at the 

Registry of Companies and Business Names (ROCBN) website

.

Section 2.

					 Investment and Taxation Treaties							

Brunei is a member of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), which has free trade agreements or comprehensive economic partnership agreements with:

Australia and New Zealand, jointly

China

India

Japan and

Republic of Korea

Brunei also has a bilateral economic partnership agreement with Japan.

Brunei has bilateral investment treaties (BITs) in force with:

Bahrain

Germany

Republic of Korea

Kuwait and

Ukraine

Brunei does not currently have a BIT or bilateral taxation treaty with the United States.

Brunei served as the ASEAN Coordinator in negotiations for the ASEAN-Australia-New Zealand Free Trade Agreement (AANZFTA). Brunei is the current country coordinator for ASEAN-European Union dialogue relations from 2024 to 2027.

Brunei is also a party to the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) and to the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP).

Section 3.

					 Legal Regime							

Transparency of the Regulatory System

Brunei’s regulatory system has limited transparency, particularly in lawmaking processes and impact assessments. Brunei does not maintain a formal notice-and-comment rulemaking process comparable to the U.S. Administrative Procedure Act. Each ministry is responsible for coordinating with the Attorney General’s Chambers to draft proposed legislation. Ministries may consult industry stakeholders on selected policies, but legislation does not receive broad review, and little input is provided from outside of the originating ministry. Brunei also lacks a centralized online publication equivalent to the U.S. Federal Register where proposed regulations are published. The sultan has the final authority to approve proposed legislation. Laws and regulations are readily accessible on the 

Attorney General’s Chambers website

.

International Regulatory Considerations

Brunei is an active member of ASEAN, through which it has concluded FTAs with:

Australia and New Zealand

China

India

Japan and

Republic of Korea

Brunei has been a member of the WTO since 1995.

Legal System and Judicial Independence

Brunei’s constitution does not specifically provide for judicial independence, but in practice, the court system operates without government interference. Brunei’s legal system includes two parallel systems: one based on English common law and the other based on Islamic law (Sharia). The common law judicial system is presided over by the Supreme Court, which comprises the Court of Appeal and the High Court.

Recognizing the importance of protecting investors’ rights and contract enforcement, Brunei established a Commercial Court in 2016. Courts recognize and enforce contracts, and parties may pursue arbitration or other forms of alternative dispute resolution where provided by contract.

Brunei’s Syariah Penal Code (SPC) operates in parallel with its common law-based penal code and provides for the possibility of harsher punishments. These include:

Stoning to death for rape, adultery, or sodomy and

Capital punishment, including stoning or hanging, for offenses such as apostasy, contempt of the Prophet Muhammad, or insulting the Quran

However, these forms of punishment require higher standards of proof than the common law-based penal code (for example, four pious men must personally witness an act of fornication to support the most severe Sharia-based penalties). Brunei maintains a 
de facto
 moratorium on the death penalty, which the Sultan confirmed in a 2019 public statement.

Laws and Regulations on Foreign Direct Investment

The basic legislation on investment includes the Investment Incentives Order, 2001 and the Income Tax Act (Chapter 35). The Investment Incentives Order, 2001 supports economic development in strategically important industrial and economic enterprises and, through the Ministry of Finance, offers investment incentives through a favorable tax regime.

Foreign ownership of companies is not restricted, although under the Companies Act, at least one of two directors of a locally incorporated company must be a resident of Brunei, unless granted an exemption from the appropriate authorities.

Competition and Antitrust Laws

Brunei’s Competition Act (Chapter 253), originally enacted as the Competition Order 2015, promotes market efficiency and consumer welfare by prohibiting anti-competitive conduct. The 

Competition Commission of Brunei Darussalam (CCBD)

 enforces the Act. The prohibition on anti-competitive agreements took effect in 2020. In August 2025, Brunei brought into force prohibitions on abuse of a dominant position and anti-competitive mergers, completing implementation of the Act’s three core competition prohibitions. The CCBD may investigate suspected violations and impose financial penalties.

Expropriation and Compensation

Brunei is a party to the 1987 ASEAN Agreement for the Promotion and Protection of Investments. There is no history of expropriation of foreign-owned property in Brunei, but there have been cases of domestically owned private property being expropriated for infrastructure development. The government provided compensation in such cases and claimants were afforded due process.

Dispute Settlement

ICSID Convention and New York Convention

Brunei is a party to the Convention on the Settlement of Investment Disputes between States and Nationals of Other States (ICSID Convention) and the Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards (New York Convention). Legislation related to dispute settlement is covered under Brunei’s Arbitration Order, 2009.

Investor-State Dispute Settlement

Brunei has a Commercial Court within the Intermediate Court that handles commercial cases and uses specialized procedures intended to expedite the resolution of business disputes. More information about Brunei’s judiciary system is available through the .

International Commercial Arbitration and Foreign Courts

Brunei Darussalam Arbitration Center (BDAC)

 provides arbitration and mediation services for domestic and international commercial disputes as alternatives to court proceedings.

The International Arbitration Order (IAO) regulates international and domestic arbitrations.

Bankruptcy/Insolvency Regulations

Brunei’s Bankruptcy Act (Cap. 67) governs personal bankruptcy proceedings. A creditor may petition the High Court for a debtor’s bankruptcy when the debt owed is BND 10,000 (about $7,060) or more. The Official Receiver’s Chambers administers bankruptcy estates and manages the financial affairs of bankrupt persons. Brunei’s Insolvency Order, 2016, provides the framework for corporate insolvency, including winding up and judicial management. 

Information about Brunei’s bankruptcy laws

 is available on the judiciary’s website.

Section 4.

					 Industrial Policies							

Investment Incentives

Brunei offers the following investment incentives to both domestic and foreign investors in priority sectors under Wawasan Brunei 2035:

Corporate income tax exemptions

Investment tax allowances and

Access to industrial land at competitive lease rates

BEDB facilitates these incentives, particularly in:

Downstream energy

Manufacturing

Logistics

Digital technology

Tourism and

Food production

The government may support strategically significant projects through government-linked companies (GLCs), infrastructure development, public-private partnerships, or regulatory facilitation. Additional incentives for large projects are negotiated on a case-by-case basis based on their contribution to economic diversification and employment.

Companies that export approved agricultural, forestry, and fishery products may qualify for income-tax relief on export profits under the Investment Incentives Order, 2001. Approved pioneer enterprises may also receive income-tax exemptions for qualifying activities. For non-pioneer enterprises, the export relief period is generally eight years. For pioneer enterprises, the export relief period is six years, subject to an overall maximum of 11 years in the circumstances specified by the Order. Brunei’s standard corporate income-tax rate is 18.5 percent.

Sole proprietorships and partnerships are not subject to tax. Individuals do not pay any capital gains tax, and profits arising from the sale of capital assets are not taxable. Under the Income Tax (Petroleum) Act, a company is subject to taxes of up to 55 percent for any petroleum operation pursuant to production sharing agreements.

Darussalam Assets

 is Brunei’s state-owned investment holding company. Established in December 2012, its shareholder is the Minister for Finance Corporation. Darussalam Assets manages its portfolio of GLCs based on commercial principles and good governance, with the objective of improving their performance and supporting Brunei’s economic development under Wawasan Brunei 2035.

Foreign Trade Zones/Free Ports/Trade Facilitation

Brunei has industrial sites that offer tax and customs incentives, but the sites do not have a separate legal regime. Instead, incentives are primarily provided through national investment laws and project-based incentives. Investors within sites typically negotiate packages with the government rather than automatically receiving benefits solely because they locate within a designated zone.

BEDB
, under the Ministry of Economy, Trade, and Industry, works closely with other relevant government agencies to facilitate the implementation of investors’ projects.

Brunei has one Free Trade Zone, the 95-hectare (235-acre) Terunjing Industrial Park, established in 2017 and located near Brunei International Airport and Muara Port. It functions like a customs-controlled free trade zone allowing qualifying businesses to:

Import goods

Manufacture goods

Assemble goods

Store goods and

Re-export goods

Qualifying businesses may receive customs and duty advantages, but fiscal incentives are not fully automatic or uniform.

Performance and Data Localization Requirements

The government of Brunei seeks to increase the number of Bruneians working in the private sector. Brunei’s Local Business Development Framework seeks to increase the use of local goods and services, train a domestic workforce, and develop Bruneian businesses by placing requirements on all companies operating in the oil and gas industry in Brunei to meet local hiring and contracting targets. These requirements also apply to information and communication technology firms that work on government projects. The Framework sets local content targets based on the difficulty of the project and the value of the contract, with more flexible local content requirements for projects requiring highly specialized technologies or with a high contract value.

Expatriate employment is controlled by a labor quota system administered by the Labor Department and the issuance of employment passes (i.e., work permits) by the Immigration Department. Brunei allows new companies to apply for special approval to expedite the recruitment of expatriate workers in select positions. According to the Ministry of Home Affairs, the special approval is only available to new companies for up to six months and covers businesses such as restaurants and retail stores. The special approval cuts the waiting time for a quota from 21 days to seven.

On January 1, 2026, Brunei implemented the Personal Data Protection Order (PDPO) to regulate the collection, use, and disclosure of personal data by organizations. While not explicitly requiring data localization, the PDPO establishes principles like consent, purpose limitation, and data breach notification, and may indirectly influence data storage choices. The PDPO includes controls on cross-border data transfers unless comparable protections are in place. Organizations operating in Brunei, including those based overseas, need to comply with the PDPO’s requirements.

Section 5.

					 Protection of Property Rights							

Real Property

Mortgages are recognized and enforced in Brunei; however, only Bruneian citizens can own land in Brunei indefinitely. Foreigners and permanent residents can only own properties for up to 99 years. Brunei’s Department of Economic Planning and Development does not publish FDI data for real estate. Each transfer of ownership in Brunei requires the approval of “His Majesty in Council,” which is a council of officials representing the Sultan. This process can be lengthy and opaque.

In 2025, Brunei amended the Land Code to ban past practices of proxy land sales to foreigners and permanent residents using powers of attorney and trust deeds. The amendments to the Land Code eliminated the recognition of powers of attorney and trust deeds as mechanisms in land transactions involving non-citizens. Brunei citizens are prohibited from holding land as nominees for non-citizens. The government may grant temporary occupation permits over state land for the following purposes:

Agricultural

Commercial

Housing or

Industrial

These permits are granted for renewable annual terms.

Intellectual Property

Brunei’s intellectual property rights (IPR) protection and enforcement regime is still in development but is increasingly strong and effective. Brunei is not listed on the U.S. Trade Representative’s 2026 Special 301 Priority Watch List or Watch List.

The Copyright (Amendment) Order, 2013 enhanced enforcement provisions for copyright infringement by:

Increasing the penalties for IP offenses

Adding new offenses

Strengthening the enforcement powers of the Royal Brunei Police Force and the Ministry of Finance’s Customs and Excise Department and

Allowing for sanctioned private prosecution

The amendments are designed to deter copyright infringements with fines of $7,400 to $14,800 per infringing copy, imprisonment for a term up to five years, or both. Enforcement agencies are authorized to:

Enter premises and arrest without warrant

Stop, search, and board vehicles, and

Access computerized and digitized data

The amendments further allow for the admissibility of evidence obtained covertly and protect the identity of informants. Statistics on seizures of counterfeit goods are unavailable.

The Brunei Intellectual Property Office (BruIPO), established in 2013 under the Attorney General’s Chambers, administers the registration of:

Patents

Trademarks

Industrial designs and

Plant varieties

Brunei is a party to the Geneva Act (1999) of the Hague Agreement Concerning the International Registration of Industrial Designs, allowing applicants to seek protection for industrial designs in multiple jurisdictions through the Hague System. Brunei is a party to eight World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO)-administered treaties, including the Madrid Protocol for the International Registration of Marks and the WIPO Performances and Phonograms Treaty.

For additional information about treaty obligations and points of contact at local IP offices, please see 

WIPO’s country profiles

.

Section 6.

					 Financial Sector							

Capital Markets and Portfolio Investment

Brunei does not currently have a domestic stock exchange or public equity market. Most investment occurs through the banking sector, private companies, government-linked enterprises, and the Islamic finance market. In March 2026, the government announced 

plans to launch Brunei’s first national securities exchange

 in 2027. Bursa Brunei Darussalam Sdn Bhd (BNX) was incorporated to develop and establish the exchange and supporting capital market infrastructure.

The capital market industry in Brunei is primarily governed by the Securities Markets Order, 2013 and the Securities Markets Regulations, 2015, which are both administered by Brunei Darussalam Central Bank. In addition, securities with any Sharia or Islamic component are governed by the Syariah Financial Supervisory Board Order, 2006.

Brunei has accepted the obligations under IMF Article VIII, Sections 2(a), 3, and 4, and maintains an exchange system that is free of restrictions on the making of payments and transfers for current international transactions and multiple currency practices.

Money and Banking System

Brunei has a small but vibrant banking sector, which includes both conventional and Islamic banking. Brunei’s two largest banks, Bank Islam Brunei Darussalam (BIBD) and Baiduri Bank, dominate the financial sector and together account for roughly 72 percent of the banking sector’s BND 20.9 billion ($16.2 billion) in total assets. As of the end of 2024, BIBD held approximately BND 11.2 billion ($8.3 billion) in total assets, while Baiduri Bank held approximately BND 4.1 billion ($3.0 billion).

The Brunei Darussalam Central Bank (BDCB) is the sole central authority for the banking sector, in addition to its role as the country’s central bank. Banks have high levels of liquidity, good capital adequacy ratios, and well-managed levels of non-performing loans. A few foreign banks such as Standard Chartered Bank (U.K.) and Bank of China (Hong Kong) have operations in Brunei. All banks fall under the supervision of BDCB, which has also established a credit bureau that centralizes information on applicants’ creditworthiness.

Brunei does not prohibit foreigners from opening bank accounts. Foreign individuals generally must provide valid identification, proof of legal residence or immigration status, and evidence of a local address or employment. Non-residents may face additional documentation requirements and may have access to a more limited range of banking services. Because Brunei does not have a FATCA intergovernmental agreement with the United States, most domestic banks have historically declined to open accounts for U.S. citizens or U.S.-incorporated entities.

The Brunei dollar (BND) is pegged to the Singapore dollar, and each currency is accepted in both countries.

Foreign Exchange and Remittances

Foreign Exchange

Brunei does not generally restrict foreign investors from converting, transferring, or repatriating funds associated with investments, including capital, profits, dividends, interest, royalties, and proceeds from the sale or liquidation of investments. Investment-related funds are generally freely convertible into major international currencies through authorized financial institutions. Brunei operates a currency board arrangement under which the Brunei dollar is exchanged at par with the Singapore dollar pursuant to the Currency Interchangeability Agreement between Brunei and Singapore. The Brunei dollar does not fluctuate against the Singapore dollar but fluctuates against other currencies in line with movements in the Singapore dollar.

Remittance Policies

Any person or company providing services for the transmission of money must be licensed by the Brunei government. Only Brunei citizens may hold remittance licenses. Local financial institutions such as BIBD, Baiduri Bank, and Standard Chartered Bank provide remittance services. Remittance companies require:

The customer’s full name

Identification number

Address and

Purpose of the remittance

They are also required to file suspicious transaction reports with BDCB.

Sovereign Wealth Funds

The government maintains the General Reserve Fund through the Brunei Investment Agency (BIA), under the guidance of the Brunei Ministry of Finance, which functions as a stabilization and savings fund. Established in 1983, BIA’s total assets are estimated to be $73 billion, according to the Sovereign Wealth Fund Institute, although official figures are not publicly disclosed. Publicly available information and analyst reports suggest that BIA primarily invests in foreign portfolio assets, real estate, and conservative fixed-income instruments. BIA receives the lowest possible score (1 out of 10) in transparency ratings by the Sovereign Wealth Fund Institute, and it does not publicly state that it adheres to the voluntary code of good practices for transparency and accountability known as the Santiago Principles.

Section 7.

					 State-owned Enterprises							

Brunei’s state-owned enterprises (SOEs), managed by Darussalam Assets under the Ministry of Finance, lead key sectors of the economy, including:

Oil and gas

Telecommunications

Transport and

Energy generation and distribution

These enterprises also receive preferential treatment when responding to government tenders. Some of the largest SOEs include the following:

The telecommunications industry is dominated by GLCs. The service providers are Datastream Digital (DST), Imagine, and Progresif. Unified National Networks (UNN), a government-owned wholesale network operator, operates the country’s telecommunications infrastructure and provides network services to the three retail providers.

Brunei Engineering Logistics and Training Solutions (BELTS), established in 1988 as a government-owned corporation, is responsible for managing the acquisition of a wide range of systems and equipment for the armed forces, security agencies, and other government stakeholders.

Brunei Energy Services and Trading (BEST) is the national oil company owned by the Brunei government. The company was granted all mineral rights in eight prime onshore and offshore petroleum blocks totaling approximately 7,935 square miles. BEST manages contracts with Shell and Petronas, which are exploring Brunei’s onshore and deep-water offshore blocks. The government continues to modify BEST’s role in the oil and gas industry. In 2019, the government established Petroleum Authority as the oil and gas sector’s regulatory body, a function that had been filled by BEST.

Royal Brunei Airlines is the country’s national carrier. The airline flies a combination of Boeing and Airbus aircraft.

Privatization Program

Brunei’s Ministry of Transportation and Info-Communications has made corporatization and privatization part of its strategic plan, which calls for the Ministry to shift its role from a service provider to a regulatory body with policy-setting responsibilities. Brunei continues to study initiatives to privatize state-owned agencies, such as public transportation services. In January 2026, the Postal Services Department was transferred to Darussalam Assets, removing it as a government department. These services are not yet completely privatized and there is no timeline for privatization. Guidelines regarding the role of foreign investors and the bidding process are not yet available.

Section 8.

					 Corruption							

Since 1982, Brunei has enforced the Emergency (Prevention of Corruption) Act. In 1984, the Act was renamed the Prevention of Corruption Act (Chapter 131). The Anti-Corruption Bureau (ACB) was established in 1982 for the purpose of enforcing the Act. The Prevention of Corruption Act provides specific powers to the ACB for the purpose of investigating accusations of corruption. The Act authorizes ACB to investigate certain offences under other written laws, provided such offences were disclosed during the course of an ACB investigation. Corrupt practices are punishable under the Prevention of Corruption Act, which also applies to Brunei citizens abroad. Brunei is a member of the International Association of Anti-Corruption Authorities.

U.S. companies do not generally identify corruption as an obstacle to conducting business in Brunei. The level and extent of reported corruption in Brunei is generally low. The Sultan has repeatedly stated in public addresses that corruption is unacceptable.

Apart from the Anti-Corruption Bureau, there are no international, regional, local, or nongovernmental organizations operating in Brunei that monitor corruption. Brunei is a party to the United Nations Convention against Corruption (UNCAC).

Government procurement generally uses a closed tender process. The government does not require full public disclosure of procurement evaluations or contract awards, and it may use limited or direct procurement for certain projects. Foreign firms may compete for public tenders. Some procurements include local participation requirements or favor firms with an established presence in Brunei.

Section 9.

					 Political and Security Environment							

Brunei is an absolute monarchy and has no recent history of political violence. The world’s longest-reigning living monarch, Sultan Hassanal Bolkiah, is an experienced and popular monarch who rules the country as Prime Minister while also retaining the titles of Minister of Finance and Minister of Defense.

The country experienced an uprising in 1962 when it was a British protectorate, which ended through the intervention of British troops. The country has been ruled peacefully under emergency law ever since. Brunei has managed to avoid demands for political reform by making use of its hydrocarbon revenues to provide its citizens with generous welfare benefits and subsidies.

Section 10.

					 Labor Policies and Practices							

Brunei relies heavily on foreign labor in lower-skill and lower-paying positions, with approximately 25 percent of the labor force coming from abroad to fulfill specific contracts. The largest percentage of foreign employees work in construction, followed by wholesale and retail trade, with the balance serving in:

Professional roles

Technical roles

Administrative roles and

Support roles

Most lower-skilled laborers in Brunei are from Bangladesh, Indonesia, and the Philippines, and enter the country on renewable two-year contracts.

The skilled labor pool includes both foreign workers on short-term visas and Bruneian citizens and permanent residents, who often are well-educated but who generally prefer to work for the government due to generous benefits such as:

Bonuses

Education allowances

Interest-free loans and

Housing allowances

In 2024, the Labor Force Survey stated that approximately 30 percent of the labor force was employed in the public sector. The Department of Labour regulates the hiring of foreign workers through its Foreign Worker License (LPA) system, which includes requirements intended to prioritize employment opportunities for Bruneian workers.

While the law permits the formation of trade union federations, it forbids affiliation with international labor organizations unless there is consent from the Minister of Home Affairs and the Department of Labor. Under the Trade Unions Act of 1961, unions must be registered with the government. The government prohibits strikes, and the law makes no explicit provision for the right to collective bargaining. The law prohibits employers from discriminating against workers in connection with union activities, but it does not provide for reinstatement for dismissal related to union activity.

All workers, including civil servants other than those serving in the military and those working as prison guards or police officers, may form and join trade unions of their choice without previous authorization or excessive requirements. The only active union in the country, which is composed of Brunei Shell Petroleum workers, appears to have had minimal activity in recent years. There are no other active unions or worker organizations.

Various domestic laws prohibit the employment of children under age 16. Parental consent and approval by the Labor Commission are required for those under age 18. Female workers under 18 years of age may not work at night or on offshore oil platforms. The Department of Labor enforces laws related to the employment of children. There were no reports of violations of child labor laws.

Brunei introduced its first minimum wage policy under the Employment (Minimum Wage) Order, 2023, starting with the banking, finance, and ICT sectors, setting a minimum of BND 500 (about $395) per month for full-time and BND 2.62 (about $2.05) per hour for part-time workers. This applies to both locals and foreigners, excluding:

Government staff

Interns

Domestic workers and

Others specified in the Employment Order, 2009

Effective April 1, 2025, a second phase expanded coverage to nine sub-industries across six additional sectors, with enforcement handled by the Labour Department. Government data in 2023 indicated that approximately 75,400 foreigners lived in Brunei. Foreign workers receive a mandatory brief on labor rights from the Department of Labor when they sign their employment contract. The government also inspects workplaces and maintains a telephone hotline for worker complaints. Immigration law allows prison sentences and caning for workers who overstay their work permits and for workers who fall into irregular status due to their employers’ negligence.
