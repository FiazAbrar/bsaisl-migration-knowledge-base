# 2026 Trafficking in Persons Report: Kuwait

## Kuwait (Tier 2)

The Government of Kuwait does not fully meet the minimum standards for the elimination of trafficking but is making significant efforts to do so. The government demonstrated overall increasing efforts compared with the previous reporting period; therefore, Kuwait remained on Tier 2. These efforts included significantly increasing prosecutions of suspected traffickers and identifying and referring more victims to care. The government also increased prevention efforts through increased public awareness campaigns. However, the government did not meet the minimum standards in several key areas. Officials investigated a minimal number of potential trafficking cases given the scale of reported labor violations while law enforcement and prosecutors often failed to recognize contractual or labor violations as potential indicators of a trafficking crime, which undermined government-wide anti-trafficking efforts, limited accountability for traffickers, weakened deterrence, and denied access to justice for victims. The government did not hold recruitment agencies accountable for deceptive or fraudulent recruitment tactics, nor did it adequately enforce the domestic worker law, which increased forced labor risks among this population. Finally, the government did not take any steps to reform its employer-based visa system, which continued to render migrant workers vulnerable to trafficking.

## Prosecution  

The government increased anti-trafficking law enforcement efforts.

The 2013 anti-trafficking law criminalized sex and labor trafficking and prescribed penalties of up to 15 years’ imprisonment for offenses involving an adult male victim and up to life imprisonment for those involving an adult female or child victim. These penalties were sufficiently stringent and, with regard to sex trafficking, commensurate with those prescribed for other grave crimes, such as rape.

During the reporting period, the government reported investigating 11 trafficking cases, prosecuting 64 suspects for forced labor and convicting two traffickers for the forced labor and murder of their domestic worker. This compared with three investigations, zero prosecutions, and zero convictions during the previous reporting period. Prosecutors charged all 64 suspects under the anti-trafficking law, coupled with related crimes such as money laundering and forgery of official and banking documents; the two convicted traffickers received convictions for both forced labor and murder involving a domestic worker. In addition, 12 defendants remained pending prosecution from previous reporting periods for suspected forced labor. The government did not provide sentencing data for convicted traffickers. The government did not investigate, prosecute, or convict any suspected sex traffickers. The government cooperated with other governments on law enforcement operations.

Corruption and official complicity in trafficking crimes remained significant concerns, inhibiting law enforcement action during the year. The government reported an ongoing investigation of a government employee allegedly complicit in trafficking crimes, but did not report other actions taken against complicit officials or acting on complicity concerns from previous years, including those involving diplomats. Some senior law enforcement officials denied the existence of trafficking in Kuwait, attributing the crime to victims’ lack of education and collusion with recruiters.

The ministry of interior (MOI) maintained a specialized anti-trafficking unit, and the public prosecution office handled prosecution of trafficking cases while retaining oversight over other crimes. During the reporting period, the public prosecutor established a specialized unit to combat human trafficking and migrant smuggling, broadly mandated to enforce the national referral system, standardize victim identification and protection procedures, improve investigation methods, provide technical guidance to prosecutors and law enforcement, build a comprehensive trafficking database, and organize specialized training for prosecutors and law enforcement. The government conducted anti-trafficking training for prosecution officials on the proper legal designation of trafficking cases; however, the judiciary continued to sometimes confuse trafficking with forcible movement and interpreted potential trafficking cases as “misdemeanor contractual violations.” The 2013 anti-trafficking law granted judges the discretion to determine which cases were classified as human trafficking and which were classified as misdemeanor contractual violations. This resulted in cases being treated as administrative labor violations rather than being prosecuted as criminal trafficking cases. This lack of a consistent application of a definition of human trafficking hindered the prosecution and conviction of traffickers in Kuwait. Kuwaiti authorities did not generally consider labor complaints or violations where trafficking indicators were present as potential trafficking crimes. Instead, officials resolved such violations — including cases of severe exploitation — through administrative mechanisms such as transfer of the employee to a different employer and mandated back payment of wages, fines, and blacklisting, or suspension of companies and agencies. Law enforcement primarily focused trafficking-related investigations, prosecutions, and convictions on crimes involving visa fraud. The government did not prosecute some cases of domestic servitude because of a lack of evidence or witness testimony. In previous years, the government only prosecuted severe cases of domestic servitude — usually involving significant bodily injury or death — as violations of other criminal laws and only sometimes under the anti-trafficking law as labor trafficking.

## Protection  

The government made mixed victim protection efforts.

In 2025, the government identified and referred 99 foreign male victims of forced labor to care. This compared with 71 victims of forced labor identified and zero referred for services in 2024. The government did not report identifying any female victims of forced labor or sex trafficking and, for the second consecutive year, did not identify any sex trafficking victims. The permanent national committee for the implementation of the national strategy to prevent trafficking in persons and smuggling of migrants (National Committee) worked to implement the NRM to identify and prevent cases of trafficking. Although front-line officials and investigators continued to follow prescribed procedures regarding victim identification and used the NRM to refer abused workers and potential trafficking victims to the shelter, observers reported many officials were not aware of the mechanism, hindering effectiveness. The NRM was not publicly available, limiting its use for non-government stakeholders. The government had an SOP and an NRM. Formal certification as a victim was not required to access services.

The government provided shelter, health care, counseling, legal services, and repatriation assistance directly. The government-run shelter received referrals from embassies, NGOs, international organizations, the domestic workers employment department (DWED), the MOI’s anti-trafficking unit, churches, private citizens, and other migrant workers; it reported accepting all referred workers into the shelter. The shelter served as a one-stop facility, providing medical and psycho-social care, food, and access to officials from various ministries to facilitate legal and repatriation assistance. International organizations and NGOs continued to have access to victims and reported the shelter assisted workers with repatriation or finding new employment. The government maintained a shelter dedicated to male migrant workers, including trafficking victims, which it opened during the previous reporting period; it offered shelter, healthcare, and legal assistance, among other services to residents. In 2025, the government allocated 218,000 KD (US\$707,792) for victim services directly, as compared to 191,000 KD (\$620,130) in 2024. NGOs and labor-source country embassies reported the majority of those admitted to the government shelter were potential labor trafficking victims who had fled poor working conditions, including physical and emotional abuse, excessive hours, delayed payment of wages, and passport confiscation. International organizations and NGOs continued to have access to victims and reported that the shelter assisted workers with repatriation or finding new employment. Public authority for manpower (PAM) maintained agreements with three civil society organizations, which included embedded offices in the shelter to provide access for such organizations to monitor shelter operations and provide assistance to its residents. Some foreign embassies maintained private domestic worker shelters and engaged the Kuwaiti government for compensation and legal redress for their nationals in exploitative conditions. The government provided victim-witness assistance to support victim participation in criminal justice proceedings. The government did not report issuing restitution for victims as part of criminal sentences.

Victims in the government shelter could change employers, and employers could not file “absconding” charges against admitted victims. The government allowed all trafficking victims the option to change employers or seek resettlement, though most chose repatriation. Although the government reported assisting victims with documentation and repatriation funding, observers noted slow processing of exit clearances, with some cases resolved by foreign governments funding repatriation. Further, PAM announced that, as of July 2025, expatriate employees in the private sector required permission from their employers to leave the country. This new rule affected approximately 1.65 million expatriates who made up 96 percent of Kuwait’s private sector employees. Due to inadequate screening and inconsistent implementation of the NRM’s victim identification procedures among vulnerable populations, the government did not take effective measures to prevent the inappropriate penalization of potential victims solely for unlawful acts committed as a direct result of being trafficked. Some potential victims left the country without owed wages during protracted labor disputes.

## Prevention  

The government maintained prevention efforts.

The government maintained the National Committee as its interagency task force, met six times, and adopted a NAP for 2025-2028. The government allocated 56,525,200 KD (US\$183,523,377) for prevention efforts. The ministry of information, in coordination with the National Committee, funded and implemented public awareness activities. Efforts included implementing public awareness campaigns through traditional and social media, including creating video content about victims’ rights and pathways for reporting exploitation. In addition, content to raise awareness about the anti-human trafficking law was produced in both Arabic and English, targeting citizens and residents, including domestic workers and low-income groups. The government funded and operated an anti-trafficking hotline to assist potential trafficking victims. The government did not make efforts to reduce the demand for commercial sex acts.

The government did not effectively regulate labor recruiters and did not hold them civilly or criminally liable for fraudulent recruitment. The public prosecutor’s office had a function on its website to report potential trafficking crimes electronically. The National Committee continued to provide a reporting method via its official website, which also hosted awareness materials; the government did not report whether either mechanism received reports of potential trafficking cases. The MOI continued to operate a trafficking-specific hotline, and PAM also continued to operate a hotline at the women’s government shelter. The government reported conducting field inspection tours of labor recruitment offices and companies to verify compliance with applicable laws and regulations but did not report taking enforcement actions against recruitment agencies.

PAM continued to regulate foreign-worker recruitment, recruitment agency licensing, and labor relations, including disputes among workers, employers, and recruitment agencies for the private and public sector. PAM officials used arbitration, administrative fines, and closure of recruitment firms and companies to resolve most disputes and complaints, including potential labor trafficking crimes. If the parties could not agree on a settlement, officials referred the case to the labor courts.

Although the labor law prohibited employers from recruiting foreign workers unless authorized by PAM, the law did not explicitly prohibit worker-paid recruitment fees. Article 10 of the labor law stated the government “shall issue a resolution setting forth the procedures, documents and fees that shall be paid by the employer”; however, it did not issue such a resolution. The domestic worker law prohibited charging recruitment or placement fees to workers. The government reported that cases of workers paying fees would be considered fraud under the penal code; however, it did not report efforts to address such instances during the reporting period. Migrant workers were reportedly charged recruitment fees in their home countries, and Kuwait’s employer-based visa system often forced them to remain in abusive employment to repay illegal fees. Despite explicit prohibitions on passport confiscation — a well-known trafficking indicator — the government did not report penalizing noncompliant employers with deterrent penalties or referring any complaints or violations to law enforcement for criminal investigation as a potential trafficking crime. Additionally, the domestic worker law allowed employers to take a worker’s passport with the approval of the domestic worker, which limited the effectiveness of the prohibition. As such, instances of this practice remained pervasive in Kuwait and rendered many workers vulnerable to trafficking.

Despite strong legal protections, domestic workers regularly experienced conditions indicative of labor trafficking, including being forced to work longer than 12 hours per day without overtime compensation, wage theft, and denial of rest days and end-of-service benefits. Although the law required DWED to provide domestic workers standardized employment contracts, many workers reported being subjected to contract switching, were not aware a standard contract was required, did not understand the contents of their contract, or did not have a copy of their contract in their possession; such actions increased vulnerability to trafficking. The law did not allow DWED to inspect private homes, which limited the enforcement of the domestic worker law. The government maintained an online platform for domestic workers to submit complaints, including for trafficking, that aimed to make grievance mechanisms more accessible and reportedly allowed the DWED to track recruitment agency inspections and complaints. The DWED continued to investigate domestic worker recruitment agencies to ensure compliance with the domestic worker law. It also initiated investigations based on grievances filed by domestic workers, employers, recruitment offices and companies, and embassies of labor-source countries. The government arbitrated such grievances either through extrajudicial administrative proceedings or through the labor courts. The DWED could refer suspected trafficking cases to the MOI for further investigation.

In October 2024, the minister of interior issued a directive for increased monitoring and inspections of companies to verify salary payments. However, private sector workers were often forced to withdraw cash from ATM cards after receiving salaries electronically, and return the cash back to their employers, so as to make it difficult to prove wage theft. The residency law included provisions and penalties for wage theft applicable to individual employers and company owners. However, the government did not report identifying wage theft cases via inspections or penalizing any employers. The government limited job mobility for foreign workers, only enabling private sector workers to transfer jobs without employer approval after three years of consecutive service and with three months’ notice to the employer, while domestic workers remained unable to change jobs at any time without employer approval; such restrictions increased workers’ vulnerability to trafficking. In cases of abuse or mistreatment, a private sector worker could submit a complaint to PAM against his or her employer that would allow a transfer; however, transfers still required approval from PAM. These limits to job mobility, coupled with Kuwait’s employer-based visa system, rendered workers unable to leave exploitative employment, increasing their risk to trafficking.

## Trafficking Profile  

This section describes the nature and scope of trafficking based on recent government and civil society reporting. Human traffickers exploit domestic and foreign victims in Kuwait, and traffickers exploit victims from Kuwait abroad. Human traffickers exploit foreign victims in Kuwait, primarily men and women from Bangladesh, Egypt, India, Pakistan, the Philippines, Sri Lanka, and increasingly sub-Saharan Africa. Unskilled laborers and female domestic workers are especially vulnerable, often facing limited assistance outside their worksite or employer’s home. Upon arrival, visa sponsors sometimes exploit migrants in labor and sex trafficking through illegal measures: non-payment of wages, excessive hours, denial of food, substandard housing, threats, harassment, and abuse. Employers often confiscate passports to prevent flight, control conduct, extort money, or coerce workers against contract terms. Many migrants incur hefty recruitment fees or are coerced into paying broker fees in Kuwait, leading to trafficking and debt bondage. There were reports of an unknown number of Cuban regime-affiliated medical workers at a private clinic in Kuwait; the Cuba regime often subjects Cuban medical workers abroad to conditions indicative of forced labor.

“Visa trading” is a common trafficking manifestation in Kuwait, with illegal recruitment companies using deceptive practices to bring workers to Kuwait via unenforceable contracts and fraudulent visas. Some officials allegedly accept bribes or sell work permits to illegal labor recruiters. Kuwait’s sponsorship law ties workers’ legal residency to their employer, restricting their movements and penalizing exits from abusive workplaces. Despite limited reforms, challenges persist for job changes, including illegal fees from former employers and threats of “absconding,” which refers to charges placed on a worker for leaving their place of employment, even if a worker is fleeing an abusive employer. Without protection, domestic workers who flee abusive employers may become victims of sex trafficking, facing threats of reporting their undocumented status.
