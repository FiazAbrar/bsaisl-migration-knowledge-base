South Korea’s migrant workers trapped in a legal cage

Published: 31 October 2025 Reading Time: 5 mins

Authors

  •
  • Eojin Park

    Princeton University

In Brief

South Korea’s Employment Permit System (EPS) has delivered stable recruitment and supported millions of migrant workers, yet it entrenches dependency and restricts mobility, leaving migrants vulnerable to abuse and exploitation. Workplace fatalities are rising and international bodies have urged reform. While South Korea has expanded skilled visa quotas, its immigration framework remains narrow and precarious, offering limited paths to integration. Without easing restrictions and strengthening protections, the EPS risks sustaining economic growth at the expense of the migrants the country depends on.

South Korea prides itself on being a democratic beacon in East Asia — a nation of candlelight rallies and protests featuring K-pop light sticks. But behind this progressive image lies a labour system that mistreats low-skilled migrant workers. Without meaningful reform, South Korea’s immigration laws will continue to sustain its economy at the expense of these workers’ rights and dignity.

At the centre of this contradiction is the Employment Permit System (EPS), introduced in 2004 to curb abuse in private labour recruitment. Over time, this system has entrenched legal dependency and limited mobility for migrant workers, especially those from South and Southeast Asia.

In 2024, South Korea had around 1.01 million foreign workers — 64.7 per cent of the total migrant population. About 303,000 of these workers were on E-9 visas, recruited under the EPS from 17 countries, including the Philippines, Nepal, Vietnam, Indonesia, Bangladesh and Tajikistan, which joined in 2023. For many, remittances are the primary incentive — the Philippines’ central bank recorded US$853.75 million sent home from South Korea in 2024 alone.

Despite its scale, the EPS severely limits the mobility of workers. Article 25 of South Korea’s Act on the Employment of Foreign Workers allows migrants to change employers only three times during a three-year contract, with two more transfers permitted under an extension. Even then, transfers must satisfy specific conditions, limited to contract expiration, employer consent or employer fault as judged by South Korean authorities.

This system leaves workers vulnerable to abuse. As early as 2009, Amnesty International documented how EPS workers felt ‘disposable’. International bodies like the UN Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination and the UN General Assembly’s Human Rights Council have urged reform.

The risks are not hypothetical. According to the Ministry of Employment and Labor, workplace injuries and deaths disproportionately impact migrant workers. While non-Korean citizens only constitute 3.5 per cent of the national workforce, they represent 13 per cent of workplace deaths. Meanwhile, statistics published by the Korea Workers’ Compensation and Welfare Service revealed that injury and illness claims made by foreign labourers rose by 4.5 per cent since 2024, increasing from 4950 to 5173 submissions. Construction sites were the deadliest sector, accounting for 62.5 per cent, followed by manufacturing at 22.7 per cent.

Foreign labour schemes like the EPS are not unique to South Korea. Many countries facing structural labour shortages in industries that locals avoid rely on similar programs, often with strict limits on worker mobility. Singapore’s Training Work Permit limits unskilled foreign trainees to six-month terms, while Article 59 of Taiwan’s Employment Service Act restricts switching employers to exceptional circumstances, like an employer’s death or complete cessation of operations.

South Korea is also beginning to amend its immigration policy. While the EPS is still the backbone of its foreign labour system, a 2023 government announcement expanded the quota of E-7-4 visas for skilled workers from 5000 to 35,000, alongside higher E-9 visa allocations. These changes signal a gradual shift towards a more diversified labour policy.

Yet South Korea’s broader immigration landscape complicates the picture. Ministry of Justice data from 2024 shows that, of the 397,522 undocumented immigrants in South Korea, 134,580 were originally legally registered workers — including E-9 visa holders — who overstayed their visa or lost status. But the majority were short-term visa overstayers — 260,818, which is almost double the number of undocumented immigrants who were originally legally registered EPS workers. This suggests that while the EPS may push some into irregular status, it is not the main driver of South Korea’s undocumented migration problem.

Still, without stronger protections, South Korea’s immigration may prove unsustainable. The E-9 visa functions as a closed system, with every current holder entering directly through the EPS and not from other visa categories. Some do transition out, most often into marriage immigration, general residency, visiting employment or miscellaneous other visas. According to 2024 KOSIS statistics, 41.9 per cent of foreigners once held E-9 status. These patterns underscore a structural reality — in South Korea, workers are brought in temporarily to fill low-skilled jobs, with almost no path to long-term integration except through family ties or exceptional schemes.

Japan has already moved to strengthen labour rights for immigrant workers. In 2027, the government will replace its Technical Intern Training Program with the Employment for Skill Development Program, which aims to protect the rights of foreign nationals by allowing them to change employers more freely under defined conditions. The reforms directly address criticisms from international organisations such as the UN Human Rights Commission, which, in a 2024 report, criticised the Technical Intern Training Program for subjecting migrant women to low wages and poor working conditions.

Unless South Korea adopts similar reforms, the EPS will remain controversial. At a minimum, South Korea should ease mobility restrictions to allow job transfers without employer consent in cases of abuse or health concerns. Ratifying International Labour Organization Conventions No. 97 and 143 would also help to align South Korean labour law with international standards.

The EPS has provided stability in recruitment and enabled millions of workers to earn a living abroad. But without stronger protections, it risks entrenching a system in which migrants are central to South Korea’s economy yet marginalised by its laws. How South Korea chooses to treat its migrant workforce will shape not only its labour market, but its international human rights reputation.
