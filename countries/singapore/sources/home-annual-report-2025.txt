Our Purpose

 The Humanitarian Organization for Migration Economics
 (HOME) is dedicated to supporting and empowering
 migrant workers who experience abuse and
 exploitation. Three pillars guide us: Welfare,
 Empowerment and Advocacy.
 We provide migrant workers with timely crisis
 intervention and sustained support through education
 and skills training programs. Furthermore, we actively
 champion the rights of migrant workers through
 advocacy campaigns and public education efforts.

                         Our Vision

                                                                    A world where migrant workers can live
                                                                    and work with dignity and have equal
                                                                    access to fundamental rights.

Our Mission

  We advance the welfare of migrant workers by
  providing shelter, critical casework, financial, legal, and
  medical support.
  We empower migrant workers not only through skills
  training, but through peer-led programmes that foster
  leadership, solidarity, and community-driven change.
  We partner multiple stakeholders, including
  corporations, government agencies, and community
  groups to advocate for inclusion, access to justice, and
  equality.

 In March 2025, we published a research report on the
 transportation of migrant workers on lorries, calling for a
 ban of this unsafe practice that has taken too many
 lives. A year later, the government announced that
 ferrying workers on caged lorries will be banned from
 2027. HOME has made clear that this gesture will not
 significantly reduce the risks of lorry transport, since
 only 1 to 2 per cent of lorries in Singapore are caged.
 We will continue to campaign for a complete ban of
 lorry transport for migrant workers.

 Advocacy is one of HOME's core missions and new
 concerns are emerging. Over the past year, we have
 seen cases of migrant workers brought into Singapore
 under the promise of a particular job, only to find
 themselves deployed elsewhere, into work with severe
 conditions and below-subsistence pay. We will monitor
 this trend closely. Alongside the CritiCare Fund for
 Migrant Workers, we are also working on a set of
 recommendations to improve migrant workers' access
 to treatment for critical illnesses.

Renewal

 Whether through events, services and programmes for migrant workers, or research and
 policy advocacy, the way ahead will demand invention, daring, attention and persistence.
 These are qualities HOME has in abundance, and which I have seen demonstrated often
 by everyone who makes this work possible.

 This Board will complete its two-year term in June 2026. It has been a privilege to serve

 uncertain times. My deepest thanks go to every member of HOME's staff, whose
 dedication is the engine behind all that we do.

 HOME's work is sustained, above all, by the generosity of our volunteers and supporters
 ­ people who believe, as we do, that every worker deserves dignity. We are fortunate to
 have you in our corner. Thank you.

Message from our
Executive Director

 Dear Friends of HOME,
 Over the past year, it has become increasingly clear that the challenges faced by migrant
 workers in Singapore are not only persistent, they are evolving in scale and complexity.
 Across our helpdesks, shelter, and helplines, we continue to see workers grappling with
 multiple, overlapping challenges -- long working hours, abusive employer behaviour,
 crushing financial pressures (in large part due to indebtedness from recruitment fees),
 inadequate access to healthcare, and numerous barriers to seeking help. These patterns
 reflect deep structural inequities in the systems that govern migrant labour.
 In particular, more migrant workers are approaching us after being deceived about the
 nature of their employment, arriving in Singapore to find themselves in roles with
 significantly different conditions and expectations. Some are placed into irregular work
 situations, yet face serious risks when seeking help, including the possibility of being
 penalised and debarred from working in Singapore. This creates a difficult dilemma,
 where those who have been deceived may feel unable to come forward.
 Political unrest has also driven more Myanmar workers to migrate for work, and we have
 seen a sharp increase in Myanmar workers reaching out for support. Many are in
 especially precarious circumstances, trapped in exploitative conditions yet fearful of
 being repatriated to a home country in turmoil. Their cases remind us how important it is
 that our support remains accessible and responsive to the changing realities of the
 migrant workforce.

 sense of belonging. Many of these initiatives are shaped and led by migrant workers,
 reflecting a community that is not only supported, but actively shaping the direction of
 our work.
 Our work remains guided by our three core pillars: welfare, advocacy, and empowerment

 policy and practice. In the year ahead, we will continue to advocate for stronger rest day

 seeking redress. This must include clear safeguards for workers who have been
 deceived or placed in irregular situations, including situations that may amount to forced
 labour or trafficking.
 We approach the coming year with a clear sense of purpose. While the environment in
 which we operate remains difficult, we are sustained by the community around us -- the
 staff, volunteers, partners, donors, and migrant workers who continue to place their trust
 in HOME.

Women's Crisis Shelter

 As of December 2025, there were 316,900 migrant domestic workers (MDWs) in
 Singapore. Many work behind closed doors, in private homes where oversight is limited
 and isolation is common. Within these spaces, risks of physical, emotional, and
 psychological abuse often go unseen.

 Against this backdrop, HOME's Women's Crisis Shelter remains a critical lifeline --
 a place of safety, dignity, and recovery for migrant women who have experienced abuse,
 exploitation, and unsafe living conditions.

 In 2025, HOME provided shelter to 501 women migrant workers. Beyond those who
 entered the Shelter, approximately 1,200 domestic workers reached out through our
 helplines, where HOME provides advice and support.

 At its core, the shelter is not only a place of refuge, but a space for healing and
 rebuilding. Our team of volunteer counsellors provided emotional support to 318
 residents, helping women process trauma, regain a sense of self, and begin to imagine
 what comes next.

Helplines: First Response
in Moments of Crisis

HOME operates dedicated helplines for both migrant domestic workers and non-
domestic migrant workers, providing an accessible first point of contact for those
seeking advice or assistance.

In 2025, we received approximately 2,200 calls for assistance across our helplines. In
many cases, workers are encouraged to first resolve issues directly with their employers
or employment agents, as this can be the most immediate and practical pathway to
addressing concerns while sustaining the employment relationship. However, where
workers feel unsafe, at risk, or unable to do so, they are referred to our helpdesks for
further support, including casework assistance and, where necessary, access to shelter
for women.

Complementing these services, MDW volunteers from our Sunday Helpdesks also
provide peer support through WhatsApp, extending HOME's reach into the community
and ensuring that workers have trusted channels to seek help when needed.

Helpdesks: Casework Support

HOME operates a network of dedicated
helpdesks that serve migrant workers
facing abuse, exploitation, and
employment-related disputes. These
helpdesks provide free support for
workers who may otherwise lack the
resources or knowledge to navigate
complex systems in Singapore.

The Migrant Domestic Worker Helpdesk is co-located with our women's shelter,
enabling timely and coordinated intervention for workers in crisis. This allows for prompt
assessment and referral to appropriate support services, including safe accommodation,
casework assistance, and counselling where needed.

HOME also operates a Migrant Worker Helpdesk for workers outside of domestic work,
including those in the construction, marine shipyard, process, manufacturing, and service
sectors. Support provided includes assistance with salary disputes, workplace injuries,
illegal deployment, and other employment-related issues, with guidance on available
avenues for recourse.

On Sundays, HOME extends its reach through peer-led helpdesks located in community
spaces where migrant domestic workers gather on their rest days. These helpdesks are
run by trained migrant worker volunteers, who provide frontline support in a manner that
is linguistically and culturally accessible. They work closely with HOME's casework and
shelter teams to facilitate appropriate referrals and follow-up support.

Across 2025, the cases seen at the helpdesks reflect a broader pattern: workers
continue to navigate systems that are complex, inconsistent, and difficult to access
without support.

HOME's role remains to bridge this gap -- providing not only immediate assistance, but
sustained advocacy to ensure that migrant workers are able to access fair and
meaningful remedies.

Casework Training for
Volunteers and Interns

 HOME's casework team also plays a key role in strengthening volunteer capacity by
 conducting regular training sessions. These sessions serve as platforms to discuss
 emerging trends, share insights from ongoing cases, and provide guidance and support
 to volunteers in their work.

 These annual training sessions were also designed as comprehensive refreshers,
 covering common issues encountered, best practices in case documentation, and
 updates to casework processes, ensuring that volunteers remain well-equipped to
 deliver consistent and effective support.

 At the same time, every new volunteer undergoes a structured and comprehensive
 onboarding process. This training equips them with the necessary knowledge, skills, and
 confidence to effectively support cases, ensuring they are well-prepared to respond to
 the needs of those they assist.

MDW Helpdesk: Top Issues

 Many MDWs face not just one, but several of these issues simultaneously --
 compounding their vulnerability and making it harder to seek redress or protection.

               Verbal Abuse & Threats

                         The most commonly reported issue at the shelter is verbal abuse where
                         workers face scolding, being called names or shouted at by their
                         employers. Such behaviours are often coupled with threats of repatriation,
                         blacklisting, or being reported to the authorities. These tactics exploit the
                         employer's unilateral ability to cancel work permits or leave negative
                         records, putting MDWs' livelihoods and futures at risk.

               Overwork

                         Overwork is rampant, with many working 12­18 hours daily without adequate
                         rest. As MDWs are not covered under the Employment Act, they have no
                         limits on working hours. Those tasked with eldercare are often sleep-
                         deprived, sometimes having to perform almost round-the-clock care, and
                         asked to perform tasks without training.

               Food-Related Abuse

                         Food-related abuse remains prevalent: some are given only leftovers,
                         inadequate nutrition, denied food as punishment, or forced to eat meals
                         that contradict religious dietary restrictions.

               Confiscation of Identity and
               Employment Documents

                         Many MDWs still have their passports and work permits confiscated by
                         their employers, even though this is against the law. Employment
                         documents such as In Principle Approval (IPA) and contracts are regularly
                         not given to workers, leaving them unsure about their rights in areas such
                         as rest days and salaries.

               Rest Day Issues

                         Despite the 2023 regulation mandating one non-waivable monthly rest
                         day, rest day violations persist, with MDWs report being denied rest days
                         altogether (especially early in their contracts). Others report having to do
                         substantial work on their days off (as there is no legal definition of a
                         duration of a rest day for MDWs), or having strict curfews that severely
                         limit the free time they have on their only rest day.

Trends

In 2025, the MDW Helpdesk assisted a total of 480 domestic workers. In this section, we
discuss casework trends we have observed in the course of the year.

Rise in Sexual Abuse Cases

                              In the latter half of 2025, HOME observed a concerning increase in
                              sexual abuse cases among shelter residents. This has led to our
                              casework team working to implement structured protocols for sexual
                              abuse cases to ensure that survivors are sufficiently informed about
                              their rights and options, and can access redress, as well as the
                              necessary emotional support.

                              In one case, HOME supported a sexual assault survivor in exploring
                              her options to seek redress, eventually connecting her with pro bono
                              lawyers to pursue a civil lawsuit.

                              HOME provided counselling support and ensured continuity in case
                              handling to minimise re-traumatisation. HOME also worked with the
                              pro-bono lawyer in gathering and distilling evidence. She has since
                              returned home, and her case remains ongoing, with her lawyers
                              working on a settlement.

Deportation of Myanmar Workers

HOME has also observed a troubling increase in cases
involving Myanmar migrant domestic workers at our
shelter who are compelled to return to Myanmar against
their will. Many of these workers had sought job
transfers; however, as the ability to change employers
remains contingent on employer consent, they were
instead forced into repatriation once their employment
came to an end. Under MOM regulations, employers are
required to repatriate workers back to their home
countries, but workers can choose to go to another
destination with the consent of their employers, who are
obliged to purchase their repatriation ticket.

These MDWs have said that they were unable to safely       Read post here
return to Myanmar due to ongoing conflict or potential
political persecution. Despite requesting repatriation to
a third country, employers insist on sending them back
to Myanmar. They are left with no alternative but to
return to circumstances that place them at even greater
risk and vulnerability.

HOME will continue to advocate for the easing of repatriation rules when they work to the
detriment of migrant workers. HOME recommends that in the event of a dispute over
repatriation destinations, workers should be allowed to purchase a flight to their country of
their choosing. Workers should also be allowed to transfer employers with clear notice
periods; the current repatriation regime that gives employers near-total discretion over a
worker's repatriation and destination makes it an avenue for retaliation against workers who
report abuse.

Case Study: Min's Story

Min came to HOME's shelter as she could no longer tolerate the verbal abuse from her
employers, which included consistent threats of being reported to the police when her
employers were angry at her. She also found her food situation inadequate, where she was
made to eat leftovers and expired food. Min wanted to transfer to another employer.

HOME raised her case to MOM but, ultimately, the decision on whether Min would be able to
transfer was left to her employer, who decided to repatriate her. Min's hometown in Myanmar
had been completely destroyed by natural disaster, and her family members had become
displaced. She was also unable to contact them. Min requested to go to Thailand as she had
extended family there, but her employer insisted on sending her back to Myanmar. This was
even though Min said she would pay for her own ticket to Thailand.

Unfortunately, even after the matter was referred to the MOM, she was told that the decision
over the repatriation destination would default to Myanmar, if no agreement was reached with
the employer. This allowed Min's employer to continue to wield power over her circumstances
even after her employment had ended, and on her future.

Caregiving Burdens on MDWs

                              As in previous years, HOME has observed a continued rise in migrant
                              domestic workers undertaking specialised care roles, particularly in
                              eldercare. In many cases, these workers are deployed without
                              adequate training, leaving them ill-prepared to manage the complex
                              and demanding realities of caregiving. As a result, many arrive at our
                              shelter physically and emotionally exhausted.

    Case Study: Safira's Story

                                              Safira, an Indonesian MDW was tasked with caring
                                              for her employer's immobile elderly mother, who
                                              weighed 104kg, far more than what she had been
                                              told before starting work.

    Full story here  Despite having no formal training and being warned
                     by hospital staff that such lifting required multiple
                     caregivers, Safira was forced to carry out physically
                     demanding transfers alone, including lifting her
                     employer's mother from bed to wheelchair and into
                     the bathroom. Within four months, she suffered
                     significant weight loss and chronic pain, yet was
                     warned not to fall ill or seek help, with her employer
                     telling her, "You cannot sick, ya. If you sick, then mati
                     (dead) ah."

    In addition to caregiving, Safira was responsible for all household chores for a
    family of five, often repeating tasks to meet unreasonable expectations and
    performing unsafe duties. Her workday stretched from 5:30 AM to 10 PM, with
    frequent interruptions at night to care for her employer's mother, leaving her with
    little rest. She was denied breaks and scolded if she sat down. The abuse extended
    beyond physical overwork into persistent verbal and psychological harm. Safira was
    insulted daily, called "stupid" and "gila (mad)", and subjected to religious mockery.
    As a Muslim, she was deliberately given food containing pork and called "babi",
    while being told eating pork would make her "smarter". Her employer also
    humiliated her with degrading remarks, accusing her of having a boyfriend and
    making explicit comments about her body.

    After four months, Safira fled to HOME's shelter and sought assistance to pursue
    her case and request a transfer so she could continue working. Despite providing
    evidence of abuse, including audio recordings, her complaints to MOM and SPF
    were not taken up for further investigation, and her transfer request was denied
    despite repeated appeals by HOME. With no viable pathway to continue
    employment in Singapore, Safira eventually returned to Indonesia. Her case reflects
    the severe caregiving burdens placed on migrant domestic workers, and the gaps
    in protection for those experiencing overlapping forms of physical, psychological,
    and emotional abuse.

Legal Support for MDWs

                                      HOME supports migrant domestic workers (MDWs) who require
                                      legal representation, most often after they have been charged
                                      with offences. We assist them in applying for pro bono legal aid
                                      or connect them with volunteer lawyers from our network.
                                      HOME works with lawyers from 15 law firms to ensure that our
                                      clients have access to legal assistance when required.
                                      Many migrant workers face significant barriers to accessing
                                      legal representation, and some are left to navigate an unfamiliar
                                      court system on their own. In such circumstances, individuals
                                      may plead guilty simply because they feel they have no viable
                                      alternative.
                                      HOME seeks to address this gap by ensuring that MDWs are
                                      aware of their rights and are empowered to make informed
                                      decisions about their cases.
                                      We also ensure that MDW residents at our shelter are given the
                                      opportunity to seek employment where possible. Many migrant
                                      workers undergoing investigations are unaware that such
                                      options are available to them.

     Case Study: Nan's Story

        Nan arrived at HOME's shelter facing charges for the unlawful use of her
        employer's credit card. HOME promptly assisted her in applying for legal
        representation under the Criminal Legal Aid Scheme through Pro Bono SG.
        Working closely with her lawyer, HOME supported Nan in reviewing legal
        documents and representations, provided translation services, and liaised with her
        investigation officer from the Singapore Police Force to negotiate a restitution
        payment plan, which ultimately contributed to a mitigated sentence.
        Nan chose to plead guilty to the charges, and HOME, together with her lawyer,
        ensured she fully understood the implications of this decision. Following her
        sentencing and release, Nan returned to the HOME shelter to express her
        heartfelt gratitude for the support she had received, before being repatriated.

overlapping challenges. Salary disputes are often compounded by missing or falsified
payslips, or by workers being coerced into signing blank documents. Deceptive
recruitment may lead to sudden dismissal, while workplace injuries can result in prolonged
periods without income or clarity on compensation.

These issues are often interconnected. What begins as thousands of dollars of recruitment

vulnerabilities at different stages of a worker's journey reinforce one another, making it
difficult for workers to resolve their situations without support from NGOs like HOME.

Top Issues: Overview

Wage Theft and Documentation Gaps

Salary-related violations remained the most prevalent issue
across our Migrant Worker Helpdesk caseload, with 1,304
instances in 2025. These included:

               Non-payment and underpayment of wages,
               including overtime paid at incorrect rates.
               Denial of rest day and public holiday pay.
               Salaries significantly below what was stated in
               the worker's In-Principle Approval (IPA) letter.
               Unlawful deductions.

These cases are particularly difficult to resolve because wage theft is often accompanied
by missing or falsified documentation. Workers frequently reported receiving no itemised
payslips, having no access to time records, being asked to sign blank or untranslated
documents, and not being given copies of their IPA or employment contracts. In some
cases, passports and employment documents were withheld entirely.

The absence of payslips and time records remains a key barrier when workers attempt to
pursue salary claims at the Employment Claims Tribunals (ECT). These documentation gaps
do not occur in isolation; they significantly weaken workers' ability to prove their claims and
access meaningful redress.

Deceptive Recruitment and Work Pass Abuse

The payment of high recruitment fees remains a persistent issue. In 2025, HOME assisted
workers who had paid between S$4,500 and S$10,000 to secure employment, leaving many
heavily indebted upon arrival and highly vulnerable.

                                              Many workers were recruited for jobs that either did not exist
                                              or differed significantly from what had been promised. HOME
                                              recorded 204 cases involving deception over job scope,
                                              salary, or working conditions. There were also numerous
                                              instances of illegal deployment and occupation mismatches,
                                              where workers were issued IPAs for one role but deployed to
                                              entirely different sectors upon arrival, such as food
                                              processing workers sent to massage parlours or performing
                                              artistes placed in restaurant jobs. In more serious cases,
                                              workers were exposed to roles involving sexual exploitation.

 HOME also observed misuse of higher-tier work passes, including S Passes, Training
 Employment Passes, and Employment Passes, by employers operating shell or phantom
 companies. Workers were hired under these passes, which require higher declared salaries,
 but subsequently placed in low-paid roles that did not match their IPA terms, potentially to
 circumvent quota or levy restrictions.

 Workers in these situations face a difficult bind. Having incurred substantial debt, many feel
 unable to leave or report abuse for fear of repatriation with unpaid loans. At the same time,
 they may be exposed to legal risk for breaching the conditions of their work pass. HOME
 supported these workers in filing complaints with the Ministry of Manpower and, where

 placements and repatriation stays during investigations.

Legal Support

                                 In 2025, HOME assisted an increasing number of workers whose
                                 cases were referred to the Employment Claims Tribunals (ECT),
                                 particularly for wrongful dismissal and salary-related disputes.

                                 By the end of the year, the Helpdesk had handled 20 ongoing or

      Case Study: Recovering $16,000 in Unpaid Wages

           Sally, a cleaner, approached HOME after her
           employer failed to pay her overtime wages. The
           employer contended that she held a supervisory role
           and was therefore not entitled to overtime pay.
           HOME took an active role in supporting Sally by
           assisting her in filing a claim with the Employment
           Claims Tribunals (ECT) and working closely with her
           to build a robust case. This included gathering and
           substantiating evidence to refute the employer's
           claims.

           We further supported Sally in drafting her witness statement, supporting her
           witnesses in preparing their statements, reviewed evidence for her case and
           supported the costs of professional transcription and translation of audio
           recordings.

           In addition, HOME provided hands-on assistance in navigating the Community
           Justice and Tribunals System (CJTS), ensuring that all evidence and documentation
           were properly uploaded and that correspondence with the court was timely and
           accurate. Through this comprehensive support, Sally was ultimately successful in
           her claim and was awarded S$16,000 in unpaid salary. She has since returned home
           and is moving forward with greater stability.

Sunday Helpdesks:

 In 2025, HOME continued to operate our Indonesian and Myanmar Sunday helpdesks at
 City Plaza and Peninsula Plaza respectively. In October, we relocated our Filipino Sunday
 Helpdesk-- previously based at Lucky Plaza since 2006 -- to our office in Geylang,
 allowing for closer integration with our broader services and more coordinated support.

 These helpdesks are often the first point of contact for domestic workers facing issues
 such as abuse, exploitation or uncertainty about their rights.

 What makes them distinctive is that they are peer-led -- run by volunteer MDWs who bring
 lived experience, language skills, and trust. Through listening, translating, and guiding, they
 help fellow workers take their first steps towards resolving their situations.

 Where necessary, the helpdesk volunteers will escalate matters to our caseworkers or
 refer the distressed domestic worker to our shelter.

 More than service points, these Sunday helpdesks reflect our belief that migrant workers
 are not just recipients of support, but active agents of care within their own communities.

 In September 2025, HOME introduced a monthly support group for Sunday helpdesk
 volunteers, facilitated by professional therapists and counsellors. This initiative recognises
 the emotional demands of peer support work, and ensures that volunteers are provided
 with a dedicated space for reflection, care, and psychological support. Through these
 sessions, HOME seeks to sustain the wellbeing of its volunteers, equipping them to
 continue supporting others while also attending to their own emotional needs.

                 ADVOCACY

Grounded in
Casework:
Identifying
Systemic Issues

Advocacy at HOME is grounded in the realities we encounter daily through our work with
migrant workers. Across our helpdesks, helplines, and shelter, we see recurring patterns
-- unsafe and exploitative working conditions, persistent wage theft, barriers to
healthcare, deceptive recruitment practices, restrictions on workers' autonomy, and, in
more serious cases, situations that may amount to forced labour or trafficking.

These experiences shape our advocacy. They are deepened through research,
partnerships, community engagement, and ongoing dialogue with stakeholders. We also
work closely with other civil society organisations to surface concerns, share knowledge,
and amplify issues collectively. Advocacy at HOME is embedded across our work --
bringing together casework insights, public engagement, communications, media
outreach, and policy advocacy to push for meaningful and lasting change.

Board Meetings and Attendance

 The Board meets regularly throughout the year to review organisational performance,
 provide oversight on key developments, and guide HOME's direction and priorities. In
 FY2025, a total of 4 Board meetings (outside of subcommittee meetings) and 1 Annual
 General Meeting were held.

 The Board also convened a strategic workshop to reflect on organisational priorities and
 longer-term direction. At year's end, the Board also held a joint session with staff,
 creating space for staff to share their year-end achievements and aspirations for the year
 ahead, and to strengthen alignment across the organisation.

 Board members actively contribute their expertise through both full Board discussions
 and subcommittee work, supporting informed decision-making and effective oversight.

Governance

 HOME is committed to upholding strong standards of accountability, transparency,
 and ethical practice. The Board provides oversight of the organisation's governance
 framework, including policies and processes relating to risk management, financial
 stewardship, and regulatory compliance.

 The Board also reviews organisational performance, approves key policies and
 budgets, and ensures that HOME's work remains aligned with its mission, values, and
 charitable objectives.

Remuneration

 All Board and Board Committee members serve on a voluntary basis and contribute their
 time and expertise pro bono. They do not receive any allowance, honorarium, or
 remuneration for their services.

Related Entities

 HOME does not have any related entities. There are no Board members who are close
 family members of the Executive Director or any staff member.

Management of Conflict of Interest

 HOME has a documented conflict-of-interest policy to safeguard the integrity of its
 governance and decision-making processes. All Board members are required to review
 and acknowledge the policy, declare any actual or potential conflicts of interest, and
 complete a declaration form.

 Where a conflict arises, the individual concerned will disclose the nature of the interest
 and recuse themselves from the relevant discussions, decision-making, and voting.

Organisational Policies

Constitutional Revision

In 2024, HOME undertook a review of its Constitution -- the Society's governing
instrument -- to ensure alignment with evolving operational needs, governance
expectations, and regulatory standards. Overseen by a temporary Constitution Review
Subcommittee led by a Board member with legal training, the review included updates to
provisions relating to Board composition and appointment, membership categories,
financial processes, and dissolution procedures, among others. The revised Constitution
was finalised and approved by the Registrar of Societies in July 2025.

Other Policies

HOME continued to strengthen and review key organisational policies and internal
systems in 2025 to support good governance, accountability, staff and volunteer
safeguarding, and operational effectiveness.

3 Advancing Advocacy Our advocacy pipeline for 2026 is anchored in the work we began in 2025 and
             shaped by what we are seeing at our frontlines. The rise in deceptive recruitment
             -- workers arriving to find their jobs, salaries, or conditions bear little
             resemblance to what they were promised -- is one of the most troubling trends
             we have encountered in recent years. HOME will deepen its research and
             advocacy on this issue, documenting patterns and identifying gaps in existing
             protections.
             We will continue pushing for a complete ban on lorry transport for migrant
             workers -- the partial ban on caged lorries falls far short of what is needed --
             and press on with the #RestDayAllDay campaign and our research on food
             insecurity. We will also advocate for stronger safeguards for workers who come
             forward to report exploitation, so that the fear of debarment or penalisation does
             not silence those who have already been wronged.
             Public engagement remains a vital part of our advocacy. We will continue
             working with academic institutions and student groups to build awareness and
             empathy around migrant worker issues, and maintain regular media engagement
             -- through features and timely responses to policy developments -- to keep
             these issues visible and ensure that workers' experiences inform public
             discourse. More broadly, we will sustain our engagement with policymakers,
             parliamentarians, embassies, and regional and international forums.
