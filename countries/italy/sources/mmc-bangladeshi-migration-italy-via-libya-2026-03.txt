1. Executive summary

Bangladesh has long relied on overseas labour migration as a pillar of its economic and social landscape. Millions of
households depend on remittances for stability, mobility, and long-term security, and migration has become deeply
embedded in national development pathways. In this context, Libya and Italy have re-emerged in recent years as
increasingly significant destinations within a wider ecosystem of regular and irregular movement. Although migration
to the Gulf and Malaysia continues to dominate in scale, the Bangladesh­Libya­Italy corridor has evolved into one of
the most organised and adaptive routes linking South Asia to Europe.

This study examines the architecture and operational logic of that corridor. Based on 80 qualitative interviews across
Bangladesh, Libya, and Italy, it addresses an important evidence gap: how Bangladeshi migrants reach Italy through
Libya, how intermediaries and facilitators structure their journeys, and how risk and exploitation accumulate along
the way. What emerges is a route defined not by rupture but by continuity. It is a system that extends Bangladesh's
long-standing labour migration infrastructure into irregular terrain when formal opportunities shrink.

A continuum of profiles: from planning to deception to exploitation

Movement along this corridor reflects six interconnected profiles, representing gradations of agency, constraint, and
vulnerability:

1. Planned labour migrants, who enter Libya through semi-regular channels to work.

2. Italy aspirants, who travel intentionally to Libya to continue to Europe.

3. Debt-driven reroute migrants, whose failed or costly regular migration to the Gulf pushes them toward Europe.

4. Deceived migrants, who are misled about their destination and find themselves in Libya unknowingly.

5. Trafficked migrants, who fall into debt bondage, forced labour, or resale between brokers in Libya.

6. Returnees or escapees, who attempt to flee Libya after cycles of violence, extortion, or detention.

These profiles are not discrete categories but points along a continuum. Migrants may move from planning to deception,
from smuggling to exploitation, and from agency to coercion depending on circumstances. This continuum forms the
analytical framework of the study and underscores how vulnerability is produced and compounded throughout the
migration process.

Structural pressures and recalibrated opportunity

Bangladesh's reliance on overseas employment is increasingly shaped by economic deterioration, rising inflation,
underemployment, and disillusionment with Gulf migration, which is widely perceived as exploitative, debt-inducing,
and offering little long-term mobility. At the same time, Italy exerts a strong motivational pull. Its large Bangladeshi
diaspora, past regularisation campaigns, and reputation for eventual stability and belonging create a sense that Italy
offers a path to both livelihood and transformation. These narratives circulate through family networks and social
reputation, reinforcing migration as a collective household strategy rather than an individual decision.

Shrinking access to regular pathways amplifies these pressures. Prospective migrants frequently begin with the
intention to migrate legally, approaching recruitment agencies or applying for visas, but are steered toward irregular
options by the same intermediaries they rely on for information and documentation. In Bangladesh's dense recruitment
ecosystem, the distinction between regular and irregular channels quickly collapses in practice because the actors,
processes, and paperwork frequently overlap. This study finds that irregular migration to Italy is often the final stage
of an attempted legal migration process rather than an alternative to it.

Eastern Libya as the organising centre of the route

Since 2020, a semi-formal entry system in eastern Libya has become the backbone of the corridor. Under this system,
migrants enter Benghazi legally using entry clearances issued by the Libyan National Army's Military Investment
Authority. This structure has reshaped the entire corridor:

· Recruitment in Bangladesh is organised around delivering migrants directly into eastern Libya with valid
    passports, visas, and flight itineraries that mirror regular labour migration.

· Transit routes through Dubai, Egypt, Kuwait, Turkey, India, or Sri Lanka function as feeder systems designed to
    synchronise documentation, verify payments, and route groups into Benghazi.

· Arrival in Benghazi initiates a structured sequence of registration, health screening, nationality-segregated
    holding sites, and coordinated transportation across the country.

Initially established for Syrians through Cham Wings Airlines, the eastern system has been increasingly dominated
by Bangladeshi arrivals. In its early phase, migrants entering Benghazi were moved to Tobruk, where boats departed
directly from the eastern coast toward Italy. This changed in 2023 when increased scrutiny and selective enforcement
in the east made Tobruk departures less feasible. As conditions shifted, networks adapted by redirecting migrants
from Benghazi to the western coastal hubs of Zuwara and Zawiya, which became the main points of departure to
Italy. Despite this relocation of the final leg, eastern Libya remained the organising centre of the route.

A modular, multi-layered network across three regions

The Bangladesh­Libya­Italy corridor functions through a segmented structure in which different actors control
specific parts of the journey.

· Brokers in Bangladesh handle recruitment, documentation, fee negotiation, and communication with families.

· Transit facilitators based in the UAE, Egypt, Kuwait, Turkey, India, and Sri Lanka coordinate group movement and
    manage handovers between networks.

· Libyan actors, including airport sponsors, armed groups, drivers, and coastal smugglers, regulate movement
    inside Libya and control access to onward travel.

These actors are linked through communication channels, trust-based relationships, and shared financial arrangements.
Movement inside Libya follows a chain of negotiated hubs -- from Benghazi in the east, through Ajdabiya, Sirte, and
Shwerayif, to the coastal centres of Zuwara and Zawiya -- with responsibility passing from one facilitator to the next.

This segmented architecture enables rapid adaptation to political and enforcement shifts. When restrictions increased
in eastern Libya at the end of 2023, networks redirected migrants to the western coast for departure to Italy, ensuring
that the overall flow continued even as operational routes changed.

A protection economy built on predictability and coercion

Bangladeshi migrants are uniquely targeted for extortion due to their strong family support networks, which make
them reliable payers. The result is a protection economy in which financial extraction occurs at every stage:

· confiscation of wages or documents;

· inflated fees for food, security, or onward travel;

· staged or genuine kidnappings for ransom;

· periods of captivity in holding sites or "game houses";

· forced labour during delays or detention;

· resale between brokers.

These practices reflect a continuum between smuggling and trafficking rather than two distinct systems. Migrants
frequently transition from one to the other depending on debt, payment failure, or capture by armed groups. The
purpose of exploitation, whether through labour, ransom, or coercion, is embedded in the operational logic of the route.
Even interception at sea does not end the cycle. Many migrants are handed back to Bangladeshi intermediaries along
the western coast and forced to pay again for release or onward movement.

The economics of the system

The study finds that the route operates through standardised price tiers. Most Bangladeshis pay between USD
10,000­14,000 for the full journey from Bangladesh to Italy, with many ultimately spending USD 15,000­17,000 after
extortion and additional costs inside Libya. Using 2024 arrival figures in Italy as the baseline and applying these
documented price structures across scenario-based models, the estimated size of the Bangladesh­Libya­Italy
smuggling economy is USD 160­190 million per year. This represents a lower-bound estimate, excluding those who
paid but never arrived in Italy. These revenues sustain actors across the route's layered architecture and reinforce the
economic incentives underlying the system.

Key insights

· Irregular migration is the adaptive extension of Bangladesh's formal labour migration system. When regular
    pathways become costly, slow, or inaccessible, the same brokers, documentation channels, and trust structures
    shift into informal terrain.

· Eastern Libya's semi-formal entry system serves as the organising centre of the corridor, structuring recruitment
    at origin, transit flows, and onward movement across Libya.

· The distinction between regular and irregular migration collapses in practice. Migrants often begin through legal
    channels but are redirected into irregular pathways by intermediaries who manipulate both formal procedures and
    social trust.

· Protection risks are systemic, not incidental. Extortion, ransom, and exploitation are integral components of the
    route's political economy, sustained by armed groups and facilitated by Bangladeshi intermediaries.

· Smuggling and trafficking form a continuum, shaped by debt, coercion, and the resale of migrants between
    actors.

· The network is segmented, resilient, and highly adaptive, responding rapidly to enforcement dynamics in Libya
    and enabling continuous flows.

· The smuggling economy is large and economically structured, generating at least USD 160­190 million annually
    and reinforcing the financial incentives that drive the system.

· Effective governance must address the entire route, from recruitment ecosystems in Bangladesh to the hybrid
    governance structures in Libya and the integration environment in Italy.

2. Introduction

Bangladesh is one of the most significant countries of origin within the global migration landscape, with millions
of its citizens seeking opportunities abroad through both regular and irregular means. Overseas employment has
long been a defining feature of Bangladesh's development trajectory, supporting household incomes and national
growth through remittances. Today, the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries and Southeast Asia remain the
primary destinations for Bangladeshi labour migrants. However, Libya and Italy have also formed part of this broader
migration landscape for decades.

In recent years, Bangladeshi nationals have consistently ranked among the top nationalities arriving irregularly in
Italy, particularly via the Central Mediterranean Route. Although this corridor remains small compared to established
labour migration channels to the Gulf and Malaysia, it has become an increasingly visible and organised pathway to
Europe. This trend has been enabled by the emergence of a hybrid migration system in Libya, in which formal entry
and residence mechanisms coexist with irregular onward movement. Within this system, migrants are able to enter
Libya through semi-regular channels but subsequently rely on smuggling networks to reach Italy.

At the same time, socioeconomic pressures in Bangladesh have intensified, marked by rising unemployment,
high living costs, and limited diversification of the domestic labour market. In parallel, disillusionment with regular
migration pathways to the Gulf and Malaysia, often characterised by debt, exploitation, and restricted mobility, has
affected their appeal. These overlapping dynamics have created fertile ground for brokers and intermediaries to
promote the Libya­Italy route as a viable and desirable alternative.

Italy is perceived as a destination where migrants can aspire not only to better economic opportunities but also
to a greater sense of dignity and long-term security. Stories of regularization through past campaigns, family
reunification, and social mobility within Italy's large Bangladeshi diaspora have strengthened its image as a country
where hard work can eventually lead to stability and belonging. For many aspiring migrants, Italy represents an
escape from the exploitative yet legal systems of Gulf migration, embodying the possibility of both livelihood and
transformation.

In light of the growing number of Bangladeshi migrants arriving in Italy via Libya, this report seeks to examine the
mechanisms underpinning this movement and to address a critical evidence gap. Despite the visibility of this corridor,
little is known about how Bangladeshi migrants reach Italy, or about the actors and processes that facilitate their
journeys. This study therefore explores the motivations, journey planning, facilitation networks, financial arrangements,
and protection risks that shape this evolving migration system. By mapping these interconnected dimensions across
origin, transit, and destination contexts, the report contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of one of the
most complex and underexplored migration corridors linking South Asia and the Central Mediterranean.

3. Methodology

3.1 Objectives

While recent years have seen a sharp rise in the number of Bangladeshi nationals arriving irregularly in Italy,
there remains a critical evidence gap regarding the mechanisms underpinning this movement--from motivations
and journey planning to facilitation networks, financial arrangements, and protection risks. In response, this research
adopts a holistic approach that goes beyond quantitative trends to explore the lived experiences, decisions, and
systems shaping the migration process.

The specific objectives of this study are:

1. Map Routes, Modalities, and Protection Risks
    Primary migration routes and pathways used by Bangladeshi migrants to reach Italy, including associated risks
    faced during different phases of the journey.

2. Identify Drivers and Decision-Making Factors
    Underlying motivations behind Bangladeshi migration to Italy as well as the factors that influence some to migrate
    regularly and others to migrate irregularly.

3. Examine the Role of Intermediaries and Facilitation Networks
    To assess how migration is facilitated and monetized.

4. Intentions and Onward Movements
    To examine how initial migration intentions evolve throughout the journey.

5. Generate Policy- and Programming-Relevant Insights
    To develop practical, evidence-based recommendations for policymakers for more effective, humane, and context-
    specific migration governance.

The analysis is informed by 80 qualitative interviews conducted across three countries: Bangladesh, Libya and
Italy. A total of 35 Key informant interviews were distributed across Bangladesh (18), Libya (10), and Italy (7); and
45 in-depth interviews with migrants and potential migrants were distributed across Bangladesh (30) and Italy (15).

This study employs a three-module qualitative research design consisting of: (i) a desk review of existing literature,
policy documents, and quantitative datasets related to Bangladeshi migration; (ii) Key Informant Interviews (KIIs)
with stakeholders across Bangladesh, Libya, and Italy; and (iii) In-Depth Interviews (IDIs) with Bangladeshi migrants,
returnees, and potential migrants. Grounded in a route-based framework, the methodology follows the migration
journey across countries of origin, transit, and destination, enabling analysis at multiple levels along the migration
continuum. This design links structural and institutional dynamics with the lived experiences of migrants themselves,
providing an integrated understanding of how decisions, risks, and opportunities evolve across the route.

Key Informant Interviews (KIIs) were conducted with stakeholders across Bangladesh, Libya, and Italy, including
representatives of NGOs, UN agencies, research institutions, local authorities, and security actors. These interviews
generated insights into policy environments, protection challenges, and operational realities surrounding irregular
migration.

In-Depth Interviews (IDIs) were carried out with Bangladeshi migrants (In Italy), returnees and potential migrants (in
Bangladesh) focusing on individual experiences and decision-making processes along the migration continuum. The
interviews were designed to allow for open, reflective dialogue, enabling participants to narrate their journeys in detail
and at their own pace. Discussions explored motivations to migrate, access to information, recruitment and financing
arrangements, experiences during transit and stay in Libya, and outcomes following arrival in Italy. This in-depth and
conversational approach allowed the research to capture both the emotional texture and the structural realities of
migration, revealing how aspiration, coercion, and adaptation intersect in shaping mobility decisions.

Together, KIIs and IDIs enabled the study to triangulate data across structural, institutional, and human levels,
linking macro-level systems with micro-level experience. The research design adhered to strict ethical standards,
ensuring confidentiality, voluntary participation, and sensitivity to protection concerns, particularly in contexts
involving trauma, detention, or exploitation.

In addition to the interviews conducted for this study, the analysis incorporates insights generated through the
author's long-standing field research on smuggling economies and mobility systems in Libya, undertaken over
multiple research cycles since 2011.

4. Profiles

This research reveals that Bangladeshi migration to and through Libya is not a single, uniform phenomenon
but rather a spectrum of experiences shaped by differing levels of agency, information, and exposure to risk.
Migrants' pathways vary according to how informed their decisions are, the degree of deception they encounter, and
the conditions they face along the route. These diverse trajectories can be charted across a continuum of vulnerability,
ranging from low-risk or planned migration, where individuals travel to Libya through relatively structured channels
with valid documents and clear intentions to work there; to medium-risk or deceptive journeys, where misinformation
and financial pressure push migrants into irregularity; and finally to high-risk or exploitative experiences, where
individuals are subjected to trafficking and forced labour.

4.1 Low risk/planned

4.1.1 Planned labour migrant in Libya

This profile represents the least vulnerable group within the spectrum of Bangladeshi migration to Libya. These
migrants travel from Bangladesh with the intention of working legally in Libya, often through semi-formal or formal
labour channels, and without plans for onward movement to Europe. Their journeys are comparatively structured and
better resourced, reflecting a higher degree of agency and informed decision-making than other profiles in this study.

Historically, labour mobility schemes between the Bangladeshi and Libyan governments (see Figure 3) enabled
official recruitment and placement, channeling arrivals primarily to western Libya, where the internationally
recognised government and its labour administration are based. In recent years, however, an increasing number
of Bangladeshi workers have entered through eastern Libya, drawn by reconstruction activities under the Libyan
National Army (LNA), which have generated a demand for foreign labour and an alternative system of entry.

According to IOM's Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM), in 2024, only 11 per cent of Bangladeshi migrants in
Libya reported using migration facilitators to reach the country. The majority stated that they held valid passports
and work permits, suggesting entry through relatively regular channels. Among those employed, 87 per cent reported
having permanent or fixed-term contracts, which is higher than the rate for all migrants in Libya (78 per cent). These
findings suggest two distinct Bangladeshi populations in Libya: one that migrates to work and remains (captured by
DTM statistics), and another that transits Libya for irregular onward movement to Italy (most likely not captured by
DTM).

While this group faces fewer risks than others in the spectrum, it is important to recognise that the risk of
exploitation, wage theft, kidnapping, and poor living conditions remain common. What distinguishes this profile
is not the absence of vulnerability, but the relative predictability and stability of their migration experience compared
to those who enter Libya irregularly or are deceived along the way. Their journeys tend to be self-financed--through
family savings or modest loans--and largely insulated from the high-risk smuggling networks that dominate the
Libya­Italy corridor.

However, as the profiles presented later in this section demonstrate, even planned and documented migration can
deteriorate under the pressures of Libya's volatile security environment and governance gaps. Several migrants
who initially entered Libya through regular channels ultimately found themselves trapped in cycles of abuse or
extortion, prompting their eventual departure. These experiences are explored in greater depth in the "Returnee/
Escapee" profile that concludes this spectrum.

4.1.2 Italy aspirant

This profile consists primarily of young men who travel from Bangladesh to Libya with the explicit aim of reaching
Italy. They represent the more mobile end of the low-risk category: migrants whose journeys unfold largely according
to plan, even within an inherently volatile and dangerous environment. Since 2022, most have entered through eastern
Libya, taking advantage of the relatively open visa and entry system that has emerged under the Libyan National
Army (LNA), detailed in subsequent sections.

In DTM Europe's 2024 Flow Monitoring Survey, Bangladeshi respondents were primarily surveyed in Italy, reflecting
their strong presence among arrivals on both the Central Mediterranean and Western Balkans-Italy routes. The
Bangladesh-specific profile shows a highly consistent demographic pattern: all respondents were men, and nearly
three-quarters were aged 18­29. Many reported lower-secondary education and previous employment before
departure, with most having worked in sectors such as agriculture and construction. Economic motivations dominated
their migration decisions, cited by roughly three-quarters of Bangladeshi respondents (76­78%). The concentration of
Bangladeshi interviews in Italy, together with the profiles of those surveyed there, underscores Italy's central role as
the main point of arrival and primary destination among Bangladeshi migrants captured in the 2024 FMS.

Interviews for this study reinforce these patterns and demonstrate that the majority came from close-knit family
structures in which migration decisions were collective. Fathers or older male relatives typically negotiated with
brokers and financed the journey. Migration was therefore not an individual act of risk-taking but a family project
to secure long-term stability. Unlike Gulf migration, which is temporary and cyclical, movement towards Europe is
perceived as permanent. These migrants are generally educated enough to aspire to better opportunities abroad but
lack the qualifications to access regular migration channels.

While their journeys are considered the smoothest within this spectrum, they are far from risk-free. Even those
who move quickly through Libya must navigate territories controlled by armed groups, repeated checkpoints, and
widespread extortion. Passage from eastern to western Libya exposes them to the same networks of coercion and
rent extraction that entrap others for far longer. Their crossings to Italy, which are usually by sea from the western
coast, remain highly perilous. However, compared to other profiles, they face fewer unexpected disruptions and a
higher likelihood of arrival. In this sense, the Italy Aspirant embodies the most "successful" iteration of a high-risk
system, where relative luck, timing, and connections reduce, but never eliminate, the dangers of the route.

4.2 Medium risk/deception

4.2.1 Debt-driven reroute

This profile captures migrants whose irregular journey to Europe originates in financial distress and unmet
expectations from earlier labour migration experiences elsewhere. Many first migrate legally to the Gulf, investing
heavily in recruitment, documentation, and travel. Once abroad, they often discover that promised salaries or conditions
fall far short of expectations, leaving them unable to recover their costs. Debt then becomes a key driver of onward
migration, turning economic frustration into renewed mobility.

While still abroad, particularly in cities such as Dubai, some of these migrants are approached by intermediaries
offering "European jobs." Disillusioned by low wages and restrictive contracts, they see these offers as their only
chance to escape debt and achieve the prosperity they originally sought. Smugglers exploit this vulnerability, marketing
onward movement to Libya as a legitimate or inevitable next step toward Europe.

Others return to Bangladesh after multiple unsuccessful migration attempts, determined to try again but this
time to Europe directly. In both cases, the decision to move irregularly arises less from aspiration than from economic
compulsion--a final attempt to resolve debts and meet family expectations after years of unfulfilled regular migration.

Across this profile, the boundary between voluntary choice and coercion is blurred. Migration decisions occur under
severe financial pressure, and brokers deliberately frame Libya as the "next stage" in an otherwise familiar migration
cycle, transforming vulnerability itself into a profitable market.

4.2.2 Deceived migrant

This profile encompasses those who embark on their journey from Bangladesh under false pretenses. Several
respondents reported being told they were travelling to Kuwait or Malaysia for legitimate employment, only to discover
mid-journey, or even upon arrival, that their destination was Libya. In some cases, migrants only realised this after
landing in Egypt or during transit in Dubai, when facilitators confiscated their documents. In each case, deception
began at the recruitment stage, by facilitators who exploited migrants' trust and limited knowledge of geography and
travel procedures.

These migrants rarely have the intention to reach Europe at the outset. Their journeys to Libya are the product of
misinformation and manipulation, exposing them to both debt and coercion. By the time they realise the deception,
they are often already in transit; without valid visas for the countries they are passing through, without their passports,
and fully embedded within the smuggling network. At that point, there is little possibility of withdrawal or seeking
assistance. Continuing the journey becomes the only viable option, both because of practical constraints and the fear
of financial ruin or social disgrace if they return home empty-handed.

While these journeys involve two of the constitutive elements of trafficking under the Palermo Protocol (the Act of
transport and the Means of deception or abuse of vulnerability), they do not involve the third element, which
is a purpose of exploitation. In this profile, the intent behind the deception is primarily to extract migration fees or
transport migrants into Libya for onward smuggling, rather than to exploit their labour or body once in Libya. As
such, although these migrants experience clear violations of their rights and significant coercion, their experiences
do not constitute trafficking unless or until a purpose of exploitation emerges later along the route. This distinction is
important, as many migrants who begin as deceived travellers only become trafficked once they fall into situations of
forced labour, debt bondage, or other exploitative arrangements inside Libya, as described in the subsequent profile.

4.3.1 Trafficked migrant

This profile reflects migrants who fall into debt bondage and labour exploitation once in Libya. Many enter without
paying the full cost upfront, being told that they can settle the remainder after arrival or once they begin working.
Instead, when they arrive, they are forced to work under the control of brokers or employers to repay inflated travel
debts. Wages are withheld, movement restricted, and identity documents confiscated.
Others are promised specific jobs and salaries in Libya but are placed in exploitative conditions upon arrival:
lower-paid, irregular work, or no pay at all. Some are even sold to other brokers or employers, illustrating the
commodification of labour within Libya's broker-controlled economy.

These dynamics meet all three elements of the Palermo Protocol definition of trafficking: recruitment through
deception, transport across borders under false promises, and exploitation through abuse of vulnerability and control
of labour. Physical violence may not always be visible, but control is maintained through indebtedness, dependency,
and fear. The full spectrum of trafficking risks is explored further in section 3.

4.3.2 Returnee/escapee

This final profile represents those who leave Libya, either towards Italy or back to Bangladesh, after enduring
violence, insecurity, or repeated exploitation. Many arrived in Libya for work but faced continuous extortion by
security forces, including arbitrary detention and ransom demands by police or militias.

One respondent described being arrested multiple times by police and forced to pay for release until he was
destitute. Unable to return home due to debt and shame, he decided to attempt the sea crossing to Italy as an escape
route rather than a planned migration. Others who manage to return to Bangladesh often do so through brokers after
prolonged hardship, sometimes after contacting the same intermediaries who arranged their initial migration.

For these migrants, onward or return movement is not an act of opportunity but of survival: an attempt to flee a
cycle of violence, exploitation, and debt from which formal protection mechanisms offer no relief.

5. Drivers of migration and decision-
making

The decision of Bangladeshi migrants to undertake the journey to Libya, and onward to Italy, is rooted in a
complex interplay of factors. Migration from Bangladesh does not arise from a single push or pull factor but from
an ecosystem of intertwined drivers. At one end are the enduring structural realities: limited domestic employment,
debt, and underemployment within a saturated labour market. At the other, are the compelling narratives of success
emanating from the Bangladeshi diaspora in Italy. Between these poles lies a spectrum of individual and family
motivations shaped by precarity, pride, and social mobility.

5.1 Push factors from Bangladesh

Bangladesh's high levels of outward migration are driven by a combination of structural economic constraints,
demographic pressure, and declining domestic opportunities. Migration has become an integral part of the country's
development model, sustained by limited job creation at home and deepening household reliance on remittances.

5.1.1 Migration as a structural feature of Bangladesh's economy

Bangladesh is one of the world's leading countries of origin for migrant labour, ranking sixth globally in 2020
with approximately 7.4 million nationals living abroad, equivalent to around 4.4 per cent of the population.1 Labour
migration has become a structural component of Bangladesh's economic and social fabric, deeply embedded in both
national policy and household strategies. For decades, the country has relied on overseas employment to manage
labour-market surpluses, reduce unemployment, and generate remittances. These remittances play a stabilising role
in macroeconomic management, supporting household consumption, savings, and foreign-exchange reserves.2

5.1.2 Structural limitations and domestic labour market constraints

Despite sustained growth over the past two decades, the Bangladeshi economy remains unable to absorb its
rapidly expanding workforce. The domestic labour market is heavily concentrated in low-wage, low-productivity
sectors, particularly in garment manufacturing, construction, and informal services. Employment creation has not kept
pace with population growth, and underemployment remains widespread, especially among young people and rural
populations. Over time, migration has evolved from an individual pursuit into a systemic livelihood strategy, embedded
in social expectations and intergenerational planning.3

5.1.3 Economic deterioration and intensifying migration pressures

Recent macroeconomic deterioration has intensified these structural drivers. According to the European Union
Agency for Asylum (EUAA), Bangladesh's GDP growth fell from 5.1 per cent in 2024 to 3.3 per cent in early 2025, due
to political unrest, reduced investor confidence, and constrained fiscal capacity. The International Crisis Group, cited in
the same report, attributes these challenges to economic mismanagement, corruption, and cronyism. Simultaneously,
high inflation, a balance-of-payments deficit, and declining foreign-exchange reserves have eroded purchasing power
and weakened domestic employment conditions. Protests in mid-2024 further disrupted supply chains and exports,
especially in the garment sector, compounding the sense of economic fragility. Interviews conducted for this study
mirror these findings: several migrants described failed attempts to improve their livelihoods through local business
ventures or agriculture, leading to debt accumulation and renewed pressure to seek opportunities abroad.4

5.1.4 Diversification of migration routes under structural pressure

Within this context, migration has become not merely an option but an economic necessity. Each year, an estimated
500,000 Bangladeshis migrate for overseas employment, primarily to Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries and
Southeast Asia.5 However, as opportunities in these regions narrow--due to restrictive visa policies, recruitment
monopolies, and rising migration costs - migrants are increasingly diversifying their destinations. While migration to
Italy and other parts of Europe is not new, it has gained increased prominence in this context. Thus, migration toward
Italy today represents not the emergence of a new trend, but the reconfiguration of an existing one: an adaptive
response within Bangladesh's long-established migration system, where structural pressures, household debt, and
aspirational narratives continue to sustain outward mobility.

5.1.5 Economic vulnerability as a gateway to exploitation

This pervasive sense of precarity renders potential migrants highly susceptible to recruitment narratives that
promise quick success abroad. Brokers and intermediaries exploit these structural vulnerabilities, presenting migration
as the only viable route to economic progress. In doing so, they convert systemic unemployment and disillusionment
into individualised motivation, ensuring a continuous supply of new clients for irregular migration networks.

5.2 Pull factors to Italy

Italy exerts a strong and multi-layered pull on prospective Bangladeshi migrants, shaped by a combination of
historical opportunity, social aspiration, and practical accessibility. While the decision to migrate often begins as a
response to economic hardship, Italy's particular appeal lies in its reputation as both a land of opportunity and a more
humane alternative to Gulf migration.

5.2.1 Perceived opportunities and legal precedents

Many respondents cited Italy's past regularisation campaigns as a key influence on their migration decisions.
Stories of undocumented migrants who later obtained residence permits and secured stable employment circulate
widely through Bangladeshi communities, fuelling the belief that Italy remains accessible even to those who arrive
irregularly. These narratives create a sense of possibility, an impression that Italy offers "a second chance" unavailable
elsewhere in Europe.

This perception also has tangible effects in Bangladesh. Loans are reportedly easier to obtain when borrowers
declare that the funds are for migration to Europe, particularly to Italy, because lenders view such journeys as more
profitable and more likely to result in remittances. In this sense, Italy's reputation enhances both financial access and
social legitimacy for potential migrants.

5.2.2 Diaspora networks and informal recruitment

The long-established Bangladeshi diaspora in Italy reinforces these pull dynamics. While the United Kingdom hosts
the largest Bangladeshi community in Europe's broader region, Italy now has the largest Bangladeshi diaspora within
the European Union, with networks extending across major cities such as Rome, Milan, and Naples. Within these
enclaves, migrants can live and work using only Bangla, with minimal need for Italian or English. This linguistic and
cultural familiarity lowers the psychological and practical barriers to entry, making Italy appear uniquely navigable
among European destinations.

Diaspora-based businesses also play a recruitment role. Bangladeshi entrepreneurs who run restaurants, retail shops,
and small enterprises in Italy often rely on relatives and acquaintances from Bangladesh to expand their workforce.
These arrangements are mutually beneficial: employers secure trusted workers, while newly arrived migrants gain
immediate access to income and accommodation. In some cases, employers also receive a payment from relatives in

5.2.3 Italy as an alternative to Gulf migration

For many migrants, Italy's appeal lies less in the concrete promises of specific jobs or earnings and more in the
impression that it can offer these things. After decades of labour migration to the Gulf, stories of long hours, low
wages, and harsh conditions have become deeply embedded in collective memory. These experiences have shaped
a widespread perception that Gulf migration offers survival but not dignity or long-term mobility. By contrast, Italy is
imagined as a place where personal freedom, fairer labour conditions, and the possibility of regularisation are at least
attainable, even if uncertain. As one migrant explained: "In my village, many people went legally to Saudi Arabia or
Qatar, but I knew the stories. I did not want that life. Italy seemed like the only door left open to me.

It is important to note that this is an aspirational view rather than a reflection of actual conditions for irregular
migrants in Italy. Interviews conducted in Italy show that many encounter precarity, underemployment, and ongoing
vulnerability upon arrival. Nevertheless, the perception of Italy as offering a pathway, however difficult, toward
stability and social mobility continues to differentiate it sharply from the Gulf experience and plays a significant role
in shaping migration decisions.

5.2.4 Social mobility and family aspirations

Italy's pull is also reinforced by the social prestige attached to migration to Europe. Success abroad translates into
elevated family status at home, influencing marriage prospects and social standing. Families frequently express pride
in having sons in Europe, and young men note that "Europe migrants" are considered the most eligible bachelors in
their communities.

Moreover, candidates often cite the possibility of family reunification and citizenship in Europe as decisive
advantages over the Gulf, where migration is strictly temporary and excludes dependents. The impression that Italy
provides the ability to settle, gain permanent residence, and eventually bring one's family to Europe amplifies its
long-term appeal and frames it as a pathway to transformation rather than mere employment.

5.3 Journey planning

For many Bangladeshi migrants interviewed, the process of planning a journey was rarely self-directed. Rather,
it evolved through interactions with brokers, relatives, or community members who presented migration as both
achievable and desirable. Social networks, like friends, neighbours, and returnees, were central conduits for information
and access to dalals6 and travel agents.

5.3.1 From regular intentions to irregular pathways

Several respondents initially sought legitimate means of travel, often approaching licensed recruitment agencies
in search of employment visas for Gulf countries or Europe. In numerous accounts, brokers or agency representatives
informed them that obtaining a visa through regular channels was "not possible," before offering an alternative
irregular route. Through this pivot, the broker effectively transformed a regular migration aspiration into an irregular
one, framing the latter as the only viable pathway to reach Europe. These conversations reveal how irregular migration
is often introduced under the guise of professional recruitment, exploiting the limited availability of legal options and
the widespread trust placed in local agents.

5.3.2 Social influence and opportunistic recruitment

In many cases, migration plans began with casual encounters rather than deliberate search. Some migrants were
first approached by intermediaries in informal community settings such as tea stalls, mosques, or local markets. These
brokers observed and identified potential clients, often young men facing unemployment or financial difficulty, and
used persuasive narratives to encourage migration. By presenting migration as an attainable escape from hardship,
brokers tapped into pre-existing frustration and social aspiration. The line between suggestion and recruitment was
thin: what started as a conversation over tea could evolve into a commitment involving thousands of dollars and a
perilous transcontinental journey.

In some cases, the approach came not from a local broker but from a friend already living in Italy who had
previously made the journey. Initially, this contact appeared to be offering advice or assistance, but it later became
clear that such friends were in fact working with smugglers and had become part of the broader facilitation network.
Their role was to build trust and legitimacy by presenting themselves as successful migrants, while in reality they were
serving as intermediaries connecting new clients to the same networks that had arranged their own journeys.

5.3.3 "Shopping around" and the engineered use of "success
stories"

Certain localities in Bangladesh, such as Shariapour, Madipour, Poritpour, and Kishervans; are known as migration
hubs where movement to Italy has become common. In these areas, there are multiple brokers operating side by side,
giving prospective migrants several options to choose from. Within this environment, migrants often "shop around",
seeking advice from friends and relatives already in Italy or Libya about which smugglers they used and how their
journeys unfolded. They compare prices, routes, and reputations before deciding which broker to trust.

When migrants do not have relatives or friends abroad, they rely instead on neighbourhood networks, such as local
returnees, acquaintances, and community figures, to identify brokers with a track record of success. This collective
process of verification has become a defining feature of how journeys are planned in many migration-prone districts.

Smugglers have learned to anticipate this verification loop, and it now directly shapes their recruitment methods.
Knowing that potential clients will seek proof of successful outcomes, they deliberately curate and deploy "success
stories." They put would-be migrants on phone or video calls with people who have recently reached Italy, send
photos and voice messages from Europe, or claim, "my own son is in Italy," to create a sense of credibility and personal
connection. These narratives are designed to pre-empt doubt and inspire confidence at the exact moment when
migrants are comparing options. In this way, the "success story" has become a marketing tool- an engineered device
to convert verification into persuasion.

5.3.4 Trust, familiarity, and local legitimacy

A recurring theme in the interviews was the degree of trust migrants placed in brokers from their own locality.
Many respondents emphasised that they were more inclined to believe someone from their village or neighbourhood
than an "official" or government representative. This familiarity, often reinforced by kinship or long-standing social
ties, endowed brokers with local legitimacy and made their promises more persuasive. Even when migrants had
doubts about the legality of the journey, the broker's personal connection or community reputation often outweighed
these concerns.

5.3.5 Family involvement

Families play a decisive role in the planning and organisation of migration journeys. Many migrants reported that
their fathers, rather than themselves, were the primary negotiators with smugglers. Fathers often handled the
financial arrangements, collected information, and made final decisions about which broker to engage. This reflects
both the family's collective investment in migration and the intergenerational nature of migration decision-making in
rural Bangladesh. Migration is rarely an individual choice; it is a family project undertaken in the hope of improving
household welfare.

5.3.6 Online recruitment and the digital marketplace

Sometimes, initial contact is made online. A minority of respondents described finding facilitators through
Facebook pages or posts such as "a boat is going on this day, call us," and then moving to private messaging to discuss
prices and logistics. These exchanges often take place on `Imo', a popular messaging platform among Bangladeshi
communities. Smugglers use it to communicate directly with prospective migrants, share updates, and negotiate
arrangements. Although online facilitation is not widespread, this digital channel offers smugglers a discreet and
low-risk way to reach potential clients and transition conversations into offline negotiations.

5.3.7 Duration and logistics of journey preparation

Once the decision to migrate has been made, the organisation of the journey tends to move quickly. Most
respondents reported that it takes between one week and one month from the time they meet the smuggler
and agree to proceed until their actual departure from Bangladesh. This short preparation period reflects the
high level of organisation within smuggling networks and the efficiency with which logistics are arranged once
payment is secured.

Typically, the migrant already possesses a valid passport, which is handed over to the smuggler as soon as
an agreement is reached. The smuggler then manages all travel arrangements, including the visa acquisition
and purchase of plane tickets, often through links with travel agents or facilitators who handle the necessary
documentation. During this period, communication between the smuggler and migrant usually continues on Imo,
allowing for coordination of departure dates, flight details, and handover points. This streamlined process, which can
take place in as little as a few days, gives migrants the impression of a highly functional and reliable operation, further
reinforcing their confidence in the smuggler's capacity to deliver on promises.

6. Routes of travel and smuggling
dynamics

The UN Protocol against the Smuggling of Migrants by Land, Sea and Air (2000) defines smuggling as "the
procurement, in order to obtain, directly or indirectly, a financial or other material benefit, of the illegal entry of a person
into a State Party of which the person is not a national or a permanent resident."

This definition highlights three core elements:

1. Facilitation by a third party;

2. Irregular entry into a state; and

3. The procurement of a financial or material benefit.

However, the mechanics of Bangladeshi migration to Libya and onward to Europe require a broader analytical
lens. In line with MMC's approach, this report adopts an expanded understanding of smuggling that reflects how
migrants themselves perceive the actors and activities that shape their journeys. MMC uses the terms smuggler and
smuggling to describe a wide range of services, paid for or otherwise compensated, that facilitate irregular migration.
These include the irregular crossing of international borders or internal checkpoints, but also the provision of services
such as documentation, transport, accommodation, and logistical coordination. Importantly, MMC's interpretation is
deliberately broader than the UN Protocol's legal definition, and does not imply that all such activities constitute
criminal offences. Rather, it captures the practical realities and facilitation arrangements as experienced and described
by migrants.

Applying this dual lens is critical in the Bangladeshi context. In practice, not all journeys involve illegal border
crossings, and not all facilitators engaged in the process fit the legal definition of a smuggler. Within Bangladeshi
migration systems, there exists a spectrum of actors: smugglers who profit directly from organising irregular travel,
including onward maritime movement; and facilitators who operate within broader network to arrange tdocumentation,
flights, accommodation, or handovers, without personally orchestrating irregular entry.

This distinction is essential to understanding how Bangladeshi migration to Libya and Europe functions. The route
is characterised by layered facilitation and shifting operational roles, in which actors move fluidly between licit and
illicit activities depending on opportunity, demand, and control at different points along the journey.

Among the nationalities using the Central Mediterranean Route, Bangladeshis have one of the most organised
and structured migration systems to and through Libya, comparable only to the Eritreans. Bangladeshi networks are
well established in both eastern and western Libya, with particularly strong footholds in the east, where the hybrid
system of semi-formal entry and irregular onward movement is concentrated.

6.1 Recruitment process in Bangladesh

Migration from Bangladesh to Libya operates through a hybrid system that combines both formal and informal
elements. While the journey is ultimately irregular, it often begins within the framework of the formal labour migration
system, using the same actors, procedures, and documentation that govern legal recruitment in Bangladesh. This
overlap between licit and illicit channels makes it difficult to distinguish where formal recruitment ends and smuggling
begins, and it explains why understanding the recruitment process in Bangladesh is essential to analysing irregular
migration routes to Libya and beyond.

expanding opportunities for rent-seeking, corruption, and exploitation. In this way, Bangladesh's formal migration
infrastructure provides both the entry point and the veneer of legitimacy through which irregular and exploitative
practices flourish.

6.2 Departing Bangladesh

The journey from Bangladesh marks the point where formal labour migration systems and irregular networks
begin to overlap. Most Bangladeshi migrants who travel to Libya do so through pre-arranged "packages" that include
air travel, visa processing, and entry clearance. Yet despite these formal appearances, these arrangements are
typically embedded in informal brokerage chains that extend from village-level recruiters to transnational facilitators.
The routes are therefore hybrid in nature, combining legal documentation and commercial air travel with the irregular
practices that ultimately deliver migrants into Libya's informal economy.

Once a migrant decides to proceed, the dalal collects the passport and coordinates all subsequent steps, including
visa acquisition and flight bookings. Migrants are instructed to appear at Dhaka airport on a set date, often joining a
small group of others travelling the same route. These individuals usually do not know one another personally; they are
linked through the same facilitation chain. This stage represents the transition point between Bangladesh's regulated
migration system and the transnational smuggling networks that take over beyond national borders.

Facilitators employ a range of strategies to ensure smooth passage through Dhaka airport. In some cases, a
network representative accompanies the group through immigration and security checks. Others reported travelling
alone but being given a code number or instruction to present at migration control, which allowed them to pass
without questioning.

In other cases, migrants pay extra to obtain a BMET "smart card", an official document required for regular labour
migration. This allows them to pass through normal immigration channels without drawing attention, even when
their destination is Libya. Nonetheless, airport authorities occasionally detain passengers en route to Libya for brief
questioning, particularly when they suspect that travellers may intend to continue irregularly by boat to Italy. These
interrogations are meant to distinguish regular labour migrants from those pursuing onward movement to Europe.
Migrants typically respond that they are travelling to Libya for work, which is an explanation that aligns with the
documentation they carry. They are therefore released and allowed to board their flights.

Many respondents described a recruitment process in which they never met the primary smuggler in person.
Instead, communication took place entirely via Imo, a messaging platform widely used in Bangladesh. The smuggling
chain is deliberately fragmented: one intermediary collects payments, another receives passports, and another
delivers them once the visa is ready. This layered handover system protects the network by ensuring that no single
actor manages the full operation, maintaining both secrecy and operational efficiency.

6.3.1 Dubai as a transit hub

Among the identified routes, the Bangladesh­Dubai­Egypt­Benghazi corridor emerged as the most common.
Migrants typically fly from Dhaka to Dubai before continuing either directly to Benghazi or onward via Egypt. Yet the
duration of the stop in Dubai varies widely. Some migrants report moving through the airport within hours, escorted
by a facilitator directly onto the connecting flight. Others are instructed to remain in Dubai for days or even weeks
before continuing. This variation illustrates the uneven and adaptive nature of the facilitation system. While the exact
reasons differ across cases, migrant testimonies indicate that the pace of transit often depends on how prepared the
onward travel arrangements are when the journey begins.

In most cases, these differences stem from logistical, bureaucratic, and network-related factors. Migrants who
move swiftly through Dubai are likely to have their Libyan entry permissions or sponsor arrangements finalised before
departure from Bangladesh. Those who are held in transit, by contrast, are often waiting for pending visas, security
clearances, or flight coordination managed by facilitators in Dubai or Cairo. Stopovers therefore act as buffer zones,
giving facilitators time to finalise paperwork, confirm payments, and synchronise travel schedules. The decision to
route migrants through Dubai, rather than directly to Egypt, may also reflect pragmatic considerations: UAE transit
visas are easy to obtain, and Dubai hosts a well-established Bangladeshi brokerage network that allows facilitators
to manage timing and logistics discreetly.

This system is sustained by a chain of semi-independent facilitators operating across legs of the journey: origin
recruiters in Bangladesh, transit facilitators based in Dubai, and arrival facilitators in Libya. These actors are linked
through informal communication, hawala or other payment mechanisms, and client handovers. Each controls a
discrete phase but depends on the others for timing and documentation. Stopovers therefore serve a practical function
within this chain, allowing handover between networks, verification of payments, and consolidation of groups before
the next departure.

On arrival in Dubai, migrants are typically met by transit facilitators based in the UAE. They arev often Bangladeshi
nationals embedded in the local brokerage scene. These facilitators escort migrants to holding locations, typically
rented apartments or villas, where others are already waiting for onward travel. These sites bring together people
recruited by different dalls in Bangladesh but channelled into a single Dubai-based transit network that maintains
direct links with facilitators in Libya. Some migrants reported being given USD 500 in cash before departure, with
instructions to hand it to the facilitator in Dubai--an informal mechanism for cross-border payment confirmation
within the same network.

Migrants generally remain in these locations until the next leg of their journey is confirmed. Dubai-based brokers
coordinate with their counterparts in Bangladesh and Libya to align group sizes, flight schedules, and document
readiness. Departures usually occur in small groups, not necessarily composed of people who began their journeys
together, reflecting how Dubai functions as a consolidation hub within a broader facilitation economy.

In this way, Dubai operates as more than a transit point. It is a logistical and financial hinge connecting the
recruitment infrastructure in Bangladesh with the smuggling economy that moves people into Libya. Whether
migrants transit quickly or remain for weeks depends less on personal choice than on the operational dynamics of
this decentralised network, where bureaucratic timing, financial flows, and inter-network coordination determine the
rhythm of movement.

6.3.2 Alternative transit through India and Sri Lanka

An alternative pathway identified in this study follows the Bangladesh­India­Sri Lanka­Libya sequence, usually
extending through Dubai, Kuwait, or Egypt before reaching Benghazi. This route tends to emerge when restrictions
tighten at Dhaka airport or when Bangladeshi authorities increase scrutiny of passengers suspected of travelling
irregularly to Libya. In such moments, smugglers reroute migrants overland to India, where entry requirements have
traditionally been easier to navigate. Until recently, tourist visas for India were simple to obtain and did not require
formal registration, allowing recruiters to move clients discreetly across the border.

Upon arrival in India, migrants typically remain for several days while facilitators arrange Sri Lankan tourist
visas. Sri Lanka then serves as a temporary staging ground, where migrants stay in guesthouses or rented rooms for
a few days to several weeks, awaiting the finalisation of their Libyan entry visa. Once the documentation is secured,
the onward journey proceeds by air into Libya--most commonly via Dubai and Egypt, or in some cases, Kuwait and
Egypt. Testimonies indicate that stopovers in Sri Lanka are primarily logistical: they allow facilitators to synchronise
visa issuance and flight bookings while avoiding detection by Bangladeshi or Indian authorities.

The growing use of this route reflects the adaptability of smuggling networks to policy shifts at origin. However,
informants noted that following the political transition in Bangladesh in 2024, visa procedures in India have tightened
and surveillance of Bangladeshi travellers has increased, reducing the accessibility of this channel. Consequently, the
India­Sri Lanka route now functions as a secondary pathway, activated primarily when direct departures from Dhaka
become too risky or costly.

6.3.3 Transit through Syria

A smaller number of migrants reported travelling via the Bangladesh­Dubai­Syria­Benghazi route. This pathway
relied on Cham Wings Airlines, which operated regular commercial flights from Damascus to Benghazi until 2023.
In these cases, migrants described being met upon arrival in Damascus by a Syrian facilitator, who handed them
their boarding passes and escorted them through the airport directly to the connecting gate. The same individual
reportedly boarded the Cham Wings flight with the migrants and accompanied them through immigration upon
arrival in Benghazi.

documents, and group management are handled with precision to avoid detection. The suspension of Cham Wings

6.4 Smuggling through Eastern Libya

The emergence of an organised migration corridor through eastern Libya has reshaped movement along the
Central Mediterranean route since 2020, beginning with the LNA's introduction of a new entry-visa system and
evolving into a politically leveraged, commercially operated pathway distinct from the dynamics in western Libya.
Syrians were the first to make this system viable, travelling via Cham Wings Airlines under a broader alignment
between Haftar and the Assad regime, before Bangladeshi migrants increasingly came to dominate the route from
late 2021. By 2023, eastern Libya had become a high-throughput entry point feeding directly into rising arrivals
in Italy, even as sanctions on Cham Wings and selective crackdowns prompted networks to adapt--shifting some
operations toward departures from Crete and, later, transporting migrants westward to Zuwara. Together, these
developments illustrate how political incentives, commercial interests, and smuggling networks have converged to
create a flexible, rapidly evolving system that now plays a central role in shaping migration flows from Libya to Italy.

Syrians

Through this system, travel agencies in countries of origin began selling all-inclusive "migration packages" covering
the entire route: airfare, entry clearance for eastern Libya, airport pick-up, accommodation, overland transport to the

in the transfer of Syrian mercenaries, arms trade, narcotics trafficking, and money-laundering, which supports the
activities of the Syrian regime."15

Since 2020, Khalifa Haftar's forces in eastern Libya and Bashar al-Assad's regime in Syria have maintained
a pragmatic alliance grounded in shared opposition to Turkey and reinforced by Russian and UAE backing. The
partnership included military training for Haftar's pilots in Syria, intelligence coordination, and logistical cooperation.
Russia and Assad facilitated the movement of Syrian militias to Libya in support of Haftar (transported by Cham
Wings), reflecting a wider geopolitical alignment. While the relationship provided Assad with financial and diplomatic
lifelines and strengthened Haftar's international links, the fall of Assad in 2024 reportedly disrupted smuggling
networks between Syria and Libya.16

6.5 Movements from Eastern Libya to Western Libya

The movement of migrants from eastern to western Libya follows a structured but highly fragmented system
shaped by local sponsorship arrangements at Benghazi airport, a chain of regionally controlled smuggling hubs, and
negotiated passage across territory held by different armed actors. While eastern authorities impose registration
and health screening procedures, this framework dissolves once migrants cross into western Libya, where control
shifts to long-established coastal groups. The final handover into the so-called "game houses" marks both the end of
the east­west journey and the beginning of a far more unpredictable and often violent phase dominated entirely by
western Libyan smugglers.

6.5.1 Arrival into Eastern Libya

When migrants arrive at Benina International Airport in Benghazi, they are required to have a local sponsor from
the city to enter. The sponsor system is an integral part of the process, and smugglers have established networks of
local contacts who fulfil this role. Several migrants described being directed into a special queue for Bangladeshis, an
arrangement that reflects how well established and institutionalised the Bangladeshi network has become through
eastern Libya.

Upon exiting the airport, a car is typically waiting for each migrant. The driver is always reported to be Libyan,
and carries a list of names and photographs of the individuals he is assigned to collect. Migrants consistently reported
that multiple cars are lined up outside the airport, each designated for a different nationality. Every car corresponds to
a different smuggling ring: even Bangladeshi migrants who travelled together from Dubai are separated into different
vehicles at Benghazi airport, depending on which network facilitated their journey from Bangladesh.

The migrants are then transported to a first holding location, typically located in or near Benghazi, where they
stay temporarily before being moved onward. These initial holding locations are usually segregated by nationality,
with Bangladeshi migrants housed together. Once settled in the first holding location near Benghazi, arrangements
are made for the migrants' onward movement from eastern to western Libya.

6.5.2 A network of regional hubs

Available evidence suggests that Libya's smuggling economy is hub-based and locally embedded, rather than
controlled by coast-to-coast organisations. It functions through a chain of regional hubs across the south, east, and
western coastal areas. These hubs are influenced by local power structures, including armed actors and commercial
interests operating within conflict economies. Outsiders cannot simply move through or conduct operations without
local partnerships. This structure means that actors from the east cannot directly manage western coastal points
such as Zuwara or Zawiya; instead, they must negotiate, subcontract, or hand over migrants to the established local
networks that dominate these areas.

6.5.3 Routes of travel from east to west

After leaving Benghazi, convoys travel westward, either along the coastal highway from Benghazi to Ajdabiya,
then Sirte, Misrata, Tripoli, eventually arriving in Zuwara. Since the outbreak of conflict in Sudan, the Ajdabiya
corridor has become heavily congested and more tightly controlled due to increased Sudanese displacement through
eastern Libya and LNA security measures in response.18 As a result, eastern networks have increasingly shifted away
from the Ajdabiya coastal route, preferring desert tracks through Shwerayif and the western mountain region. From
there, they will move northwards toward the coast.

As migrants travel westward from Benghazi, they encounter multiple checkpoints operated by a range of security
actors. In some cases, vehicles and drivers were changed as migrants moved between areas of control; in other
cases, accompaniment was reported. Overall, these accounts illustrate how transit through the corridor is shaped by
fragmented authority and locally negotiated arrangements between smugglers and various other actors.

6.5.4 Registration and health controls under the LNA

In eastern Libya, migrants described a structured system of registration and medical screening overseen by the
Libyan National Army (LNA). At checkpoints, they were required to undergo blood tests to screen for communicable
diseases. Those who tested positive were deported, while those cleared were issued a security registration card and
permitted to continue their journey. Migrants also reported that they were required to show this card at subsequent
checkpoints along the route, suggesting a coordinated and standardised process across LNA-controlled territory.

This system represents a form of migration management established by the LNA, blending security oversight with
rudimentary public health controls. It allows the eastern authorities to monitor and regulate movement through their
territory while maintaining the appearance of order and control. However, once migrants cross west of Sirte into areas
under the Government of National Unity (GNU), this registration ceases to hold validity. Beyond that point, movements
occur entirely through informal channels, facilitated by smuggling networks and negotiated passage across multiple
checkpoints to reach the western coast.

6.5.5 Cooperation, not expansion

Once migrants reach western Libya, they are taken to what they call the "game house"--the final holding site
before departure for the coast. The term "game" is widely used among Bangladeshi migrants to describe the irregular
boat crossing of the Mediterranean, and the "game house" is thus the place where the "game" begins. These sites
are always managed by Libyan smugglers and accommodate mixed nationalities, marking a clear transition from
the Bangladeshi-run holding locations in the east. This point represents the handover, where Bangladeshi networks
relinquish control to the Libyan groups that dominate the coastal departure points.

The game house is consistently described by migrants as the most dangerous part of the journey. Up to this point,
their Bangladeshi smugglers maintain some control and can offer limited protection. But from the moment they enter
the game house, the Libyan smugglers take over--and are widely described as brutal and unpredictable. Whether a
person passes through quickly or suffers prolonged abuse depends largely on chance. Some migrants reported being
extorted in the game house, forced to pay additional sums despite assurances from their Bangladeshi facilitators that
the entire journey had already been covered.

Bangladeshi intermediaries decide which Libyan smuggler, or game operator, to hand each migrant to, often
based on the amount of money paid in Bangladesh. Migrants noted that their fate depends heavily on the honesty
of the dalal: in some cases, those who paid less, or dealt with dishonest brokers, were never even given a chance to
attempt the "game" at all.

6.6 Boat departures from the western coast

The majority of migrants in our sample departed from Zuwara, while a smaller number departed from Zawiya,
and had travelled after October 2023. The data indicates that the choice of departure site is opportunistic rather
than hierarchical, shaped by local political dynamics and security conditions rather than by a fixed coastal structure.
In other words, traffic flows rotate between coastal nodes according to who enjoys short-term protection and who is
being pressured at a given time.

Operationally, the final leg mirrors the hub-and-handover model described earlier. From second-stage holding
sites around Tripolitania, mixed-nationality groups are moved after midnight in closed lorries to minimise detection
and manage ransom risk. The coast-side interface is highly compressed: a military or militia figure often supervises
embarkation; groups are rushed aboard with little sequencing; and crews include a handful of migrants pre-selected
and briefed to steer, handle fuel, or troubleshoot engines. Vessels range from repurposed fishing boats to larger

32  Navigating new corridors: the evolving route of Bangladeshi migration to Italy through Libya
wooden craft; loads of several hundred people are not unusual. The crossing to Lampedusa can be direct and fast,
typically 24 to 36 hours in fair weather, but remains precarious. Mechanical failures and dehydration are common, and
even the "smoothest" journeys involve severe physical risk rather than reduced danger.

Search-and-rescue and interception patterns further underscore these dynamics. Bangladeshis were among
the most intercepted and returned nationalities between 2019 and 2022, with IOM Libya's Search and Rescue data
recording 4,451 Bangladeshi interceptions in 2022, the highest of any nationality that year. In 2023, however, that
figure fell sharply to 1,668--a 63 per cent decrease. This decline likely reflects the same selective enforcement
described above: negotiated tolerance for certain operators, temporary shutdowns of others, and altered embarkation
points that reduced patrol predictability. In short, fewer interceptions do not necessarily mean fewer departures; they
may instead indicate a more coordinated and politically managed coastal economy.

For those intercepted, disembarkation rarely marks the end of the ordeal. As detailed in previous sections,
Bangladeshi migrants report being rapidly handed from coastal forces to Bangladeshi intermediaries who demand
additional payments for release or onward travel. This indicates that interception itself is embedded in the same
economy of extraction as embarkation. Consular constraints further perpetuate this cycle. The Bangladeshi Embassy
in Tripoli reports ongoing difficulty obtaining exit authorisations from Libyan authorities, leaving migrants who wish to
return home effectively trapped. In 2023, the embassy sought to repatriate approximately 2,000 individuals but was
unable to do so due to bureaucratic and logistical obstacles.

6.7 Arrivals into Western Libya

Some Bangladeshi migrants reported arriving directly into western Libya, travelling on flights from Dubai to
Mitiga International Airport in Tripoli. Upon arrival, the process mirrored that of Benghazi: cars were lined up outside
the terminal, each assigned to collect migrants of a specific nationality. As in the east, the drivers were Libyan and
carried lists with names and photographs of those they were to receive, indicating the same level of coordination
between airport authorities, local sponsors, and smuggling facilitators.

Like arrivals in the east, migrants entering through Tripoli were transported from the airport to a first holding
location, typically accommodating only Bangladeshi nationals. These holding locations served as temporary
holding sites while onward arrangements were organised. From there, the migrants were moved to a game house, the
final holding point before departure, which was operated by Libyan smugglers and hosted mixed nationalities.

However, those arriving directly in western Libya often faced longer waiting times before departure compared to
those who entered through the east. Some reported being held for several months (up to six) before being taken to
the coast. This suggests that the Bangladeshi smuggling networks operating in eastern Libya may be more structured
and organised.

6.8 The economics of smuggling

The economics of the Bangladesh­Libya­Italy route are shaped by a set of broadly understood price tiers that
vary according to migrants' intentions, levels of risk, and the circumstances they face along the way. Standard
packages, higher-tier options, and additional costs incurred inside Libya all reflect how payments adjust to the realities
of the journey, while those who travelled to Libya for work follow a different cost structure altogether. Using these
price patterns and verified arrival figures, this section also outlines a scenario-based approach to estimate the scale
of the smuggling market, providing a grounded indication of its overall size without overstating precision.

6.9 Prices

The cost of travelling from Bangladesh to Italy follows a relatively standardised structure that is well understood
in many migration-prone districts. For those who leave Bangladesh with the intention of reaching Italy, the journey is
commonly priced at USD 10,000, divided into two instalments: an initial payment of USD 3,000 made in Bangladesh
before departure, followed by USD 7,000 after the migrant arrives in Libya.

Alongside this standard package, some families opt for an "all-inclusive" version, typically costing around USD
14,000. Marketed as a higher-tier option, it is presented as covering the full journey as well as any contingencies that

Navigating new corridors: the evolving route of Bangladeshi migration to Italy through Libya  33
may arise inside Libya. Its purpose is essentially reassurance: a premium product for households seeking a greater
sense of security in an inherently uncertain environment.

However, even with these predefined packages, many migrants report that the overall cost of the journey increases
once inside Libya. Additional payments, arising at various points and for a range of reasons (as described in
the section on risks), often raise the total expenditure to USD 15,000­17,000. These higher amounts tend to
correspond to journeys in which circumstances become more constrained and migrants have less control over their
onward movement.

In contrast to Italy-bound travellers, migrants who go to Libya for the purpose of work encounter a different
pricing model. Their entry, which takes place through semi-formal labour channels, is typically priced at around
USD 3,000 in total. This amount is usually split between a modest payment in Bangladesh (typically $500) before
departure and the remainder upon arrival in Libya.

A separate cost structure applies to those who originally migrated to Libya for work but later decide to continue
to Italy after their circumstances change. For these migrants, the price for the Libya­Italy leg alone is approximately
USD 5,000. In these cases, the payment finances a late, and often urgent transition out of Libya rather than a
pre-planned, long-distance journey from Bangladesh.

Taken together, these different pricing tiers illustrate how the economics of smuggling map onto the varied
mobility trajectories captured in the profile typology. Each cost structure reflects not only the route taken but
also the level of choice, constraint, and predictability that shapes the migrant's position along the continuum of risk.

6.10 Estimating the size of the Bangladesh­Libya­Italy
smuggling market

To estimate the size of the smuggling economy that facilitates movement from Bangladesh to Italy via Libya,
this report adopts a transparent, scenario-based method grounded in the evidence generated by the study. The
objective is not to produce a precise valuation, which would be neither possible nor credible given the opacity of
the system, but rather to establish a plausible order-of-magnitude estimate based on known flows and consistently
reported price points.

The estimate draws on three main elements. First, it uses the official figure of 14,000 Bangladeshi arrivals to Italy
from Libya in 2024, which provides a conservative proxy for the number of migrants who successfully completed the
full payment cycle. This approach avoids overestimation by counting only those who reached Italy, while recognising
that a proportion of those who paid for the journey did not arrive and therefore remain outside this lower-bound
calculation.

Second, it incorporates the distinct price tiers documented through interviews: a standard package of
USD 10,000 for the full journey from Bangladesh to Italy; a higher-tier "all-inclusive" option of USD 14,000; and
significantly higher total expenditures of USD 16,000­17,000 among those who incurred additional costs inside
Libya. These three tiers form the backbone of the model. Journeys undertaken by individuals who had originally
travelled to Libya for work and later decided to continue to Italy, typically priced at USD 5,000 for the Libya­Italy
leg alone, are treated as a separate category, acknowledging that they represent a minority within the overall
Italy-bound flow.

Third, because there is no representative dataset that indicates how many migrants fall into each price category,
the estimate relies on three scenario-based distributions: conservative, central, and upper-bound. The conservative
scenario assumes that most migrants paid the lower end of the price spectrum; the central scenario reflects the
balance suggested by interview data, in which a substantial proportion reported higher-than-expected payments; and
the upper-bound scenario models a context in which cost escalation inside Libya was widespread. These scenarios
provide a structured way to reflect uncertainty without imposing unwarranted precision.

Taken together, these elements allow for a credible, transparent estimate of the scale of the market. By anchoring
the model in official arrival figures, consistently documented payment structures, and clearly stated scenario
assumptions, the analysis provides a realistic lower-bound range for the revenue generated by smuggling networks
facilitating movement from Bangladesh to Italy via Libya, without overstating the accuracy of figures that are, by
nature, indicative.

7.1.1 Opportunistic extraction along the route

At the most basic level, opportunism is routine. Drivers, guards, and escorts frequently ask migrants to surrender
their cash or phones "for safekeeping," claiming that carrying valuables on the road is dangerous. Once handed over,
these possessions are rarely returned. Such small-scale thefts are common because facilitators are often left alone
with migrants for extended periods, creating moments of unchecked control.

7.1.2 Exploitation in holding locations

Facilitators managing holding locations also exploit delays in movement. When journeys stall, because of
checkpoints, fighting, or changing agreements, conditions deteriorate in predictable stages. Food is first reduced,
then withheld altogether, until migrants are told to pay more if they wish to resume eating or continue travelling. These
practices are part of a broader business logic: each actor along the route expects to be compensated for additional
time and risk. In the absence of formal agreements, coercion becomes the mechanism through which new payments
are enforced.

7.1.3 Kidnapping and Insecurity

Beyond everyday opportunism, Bangladeshi migrants are also targeted by armed groups who understand
their ransom value. Abductions occur during transport between holding locations or while travelling to and from
workplaces. Migrants describe constant fear and restricted mobility, often confined indoors or moved only under
escort. Those who do work outside leave before dawn, hidden inside goods trucks, and return by midday to avoid
ambushes. Despite these precautions, kidnappings remain frequent. Some migrants reported armed men breaking
into their accommodation at night to seize them for ransom.

7.1.4 Extortion in the game houses

At the final stage, in the game houses, which are the last holding sites before departure, Libyan smugglers
routinely demand further payments, even from those who were told that their journey was fully paid in Bangladesh.
When families hesitate, smugglers escalate pressure through violence and threats. Migrants recounted beatings,
mock executions, and warnings of organ trafficking. Families in Bangladesh receive calls, photos, or videos showing
their relatives in distress, accompanied by urgent instructions to send money through informal channels.

7.1.5 Orchestrated kidnappings

The same system that exposes migrants to kidnapping by Libyan groups also enables staged
kidnappings orchestrated jointly by Bangladeshi and Libyan smugglers. These scenarios are designed
to simulate abduction, forcing families to make new payments. In some cases, smugglers distinguish
between "premium" and "normal" journeys. Those who pay for the "premium option" are promised protection. They
are told that even if they are kidnapped or extorted, the smuggler will cover the costs and no further payment
will be required. Those on the "normal option" are warned that if anything happens to them, they will have to
pay for their own release. This distinction often sets the stage for manipulation: migrants who choose the normal
option frequently experience kidnappings or detentions that appear to have been deliberately arranged to generate
additional payments. In such cases, the original smuggler often appears at the end of a chain of extortion as the
"saviour" and the migrants pay an additional sum to resume their journey.

7.1.6 Dynamics at disembarkation

Migrants in this study consistently reported that after being intercepted at sea by the Libyan Coast Guard, they
were not taken into formal detention facilities, they were handed over to Bangladeshi networks operating along
the western coast. These networks demanded additional payments for the migrants' release or onward travel, often
contacting the migrants' families directly in Bangladesh. In most cases, the migrants' original smuggler also became
involved, either by advising the families to pay or coordinating subsequent movements. It is not clear whether this

Navigating new corridors: the evolving route of Bangladeshi migration to Italy through Libya  37
process reflects active collusion between different Bangladeshi groups or an informal procedure through which the
Coast Guard routinely transfers custody of Bangladeshi migrants to these facilitators. What is evident, however,
is that interception does not mark the end of the journey; it initiates a new round of financial negotiation between
Bangladeshi intermediaries, Libyan actors, and the families at origin.

7.2 Trafficking dynamics

Patterns observed among Bangladeshi migrants in Libya reveal how smuggling and trafficking frequently
overlap, forming part of the same continuum of exploitation. Across cases, the elements of trafficking are evident:
recruitment through deception, the abuse of economic vulnerability, and exploitation through debt, coercion, or control
of movement and identity documents. Whether presented as a repayment arrangement, temporary employment, or
a means to cover ransom costs, the underlying mechanism is the same: migrants' labour and bodies are commodified
to generate profit. Within Libya's fragmented economy, these practices have become a normalised extension of the
smuggling system, where the distinction between facilitator and trafficker depends less on role and more on timing
and intent.

7.2.1 Debt and bondage

The most common form of trafficking identified involves debt-based coercion. Migrants who cannot afford to pay
for the journey upfront are told by facilitators that they can settle the cost after arriving in Europe. Believing they are
entering into a credit arrangement, they effectively begin their journey already indebted. When they reach Libya,
instead of continuing to Italy, they are informed that they must work to repay their travel debt. This work takes place
under exploitative conditions: migrants have no freedom to choose their employer, wages are withheld, and the debt is
inflated to ensure continued dependency. What begins as a smuggling arrangement transforms into labour bondage,
with the debt serving as a mechanism of control.

7.2.2 Deceptive recruitment for work

Another pattern involves migrants who are told they are travelling to Italy through regular labour channels. These
individuals enter Libya legally by air and are promised temporary work while waiting for their "next visa" to Italy. On
arrival, their passports are confiscated under the pretext of processing paperwork, which is often the first sign of
trafficking. They are put to work immediately, frequently in construction or service sectors, but receive no payment.
Over time, they are informed that they have been "sold" to another employer or broker. In one reported case, seven
migrants were collectively sold for USD 17,000; the new handler demanded USD 12,000 per person to secure their
release. Such transactions demonstrate a market of human resale, where migrants are treated as tradable assets
within Libya's informal labour economy.

7.2.3 Bonded labour during detention and kidnapping

A further form of exploitation occurs when migrants are kidnapped or detained for ransom. While families at
home negotiate payments, the captives are forced to work, often in agriculture, construction, or manual labour, in
order to cover their living costs or as punishment for delays in payment. This practice blurs the line between detention
and labour exploitation, embedding trafficking practices within Libya's wider system of ransom-based extortion.

7.2.4 Broker-controlled labour system

For some migrants, the intention to travel to Libya is framed around finding work rather than reaching Europe.
Smugglers in Bangladesh often assure them that employment will be arranged upon arrival, promising to
"introduce them to someone who can help." However, this introduction rarely leads to direct or stable work. Instead,
migrants enter a broker-controlled labour system that traps them in a cycle of debt and dependency. Even Bangladeshi
migrants destined for Europe will sometime start looking for work in Libya when their onward travel becomes delayed
and become introduced into this system.

38  Navigating new corridors: the evolving route of Bangladeshi migration to Italy through Libya
Once in Libya, access to employment is mediated entirely by local brokers who control hiring, wages, and housing.
Migrants must pay fees to obtain short-term jobs, and when these end, they are required to pay again to access new
opportunities. In some cases, brokers withhold all earnings, claiming deductions for food, accommodation, or "service
fees." When work is unavailable, migrants are obliged to remain in broker-managed housing, paying for subsistence
even as they earn nothing. This arrangement strips them of financial autonomy and ties their survival to the broker's
control.

Employment for Bangladeshi migrants in Libya is concentrated in low-wage, high-risk sectors that rely heavily on
informal labour. Common areas of work include construction, housekeeping in hotels, bakeries, restaurants, cleaning
services, domestic work, and carwashes. These sectors depend on irregular workers with little bargaining power and
are therefore easily integrated into the broker system.

Although overt violence is not always used, the structure of this system constitutes coercion. Migrants have already
invested heavily to reach Libya and cannot simply abandon the arrangement without losing everything they have
paid. Their irregular status leaves them without legal protection or the ability to seek alternative work. The brokers
exploit this vulnerability to maintain control over their labour and extract continuous payments.

This pattern reflects the core elements of trafficking for the purpose of labour exploitation as defined under the
Palermo Protocol: recruitment through deception, movement across borders under false promises, and exploitation
through abuse of vulnerability and control of labour. The deception at origin (promising employment that does not
exist) coupled with economic coercion and the withholding of wages at destination, transforms what begins as
smuggling into a trafficking relationship. The absence of physical restraint does not diminish the exploitative nature of
the system; control is exercised through dependency, indebtedness, and the constant threat of destitution.

7.2.5 Other ways of securing employment in Libya

Some migrants access employment through existing Bangladeshi networks rather than brokers. In several cases,
a relative already working in Libya organised the paperwork and arranged for the migrant to join them, using
personal or employer connections to secure authorisation. Others reported that they arrived in Benghazi through the
same facilitated travel system as smuggled migrants but were channelled into temporary work instead of transit. On
arrival, they were accommodated in a holding location for around fifteen days while a work permit was arranged.
Once the permit was obtained, they were transferred to their workplace and began receiving wages.

While these examples may reflect lawful or semi-formal employment, they nonetheless occur within the same
brokerage system that enables exploitation. The intermediaries who arrange these opportunities often operate
across both licit and illicit economies, facilitating documentation for some workers while withholding wages or
confiscating passports for others. This overlap means that legality does not necessarily protect migrants from coercion;
the mechanisms that enable legitimate work can easily be repurposed to sustain trafficking-like control.

8.1 A Multi-layered system of actors

Smuggling networks are best understood not as monolithic "cartels" but as flexible, modular structures in which
different actors specialise in particular segments of the journey. In the Bangladesh-to-Libya route, this segmentation
is clearly visible: recruitment and documentation in Bangladesh; transit via the UAE, Kuwait, Egypt or Syria; entry into
Libya; movement across Libya; and finally maritime departure. Each segment is handled by actors with particular
geographic, linguistic and institutional advantages.

8.1.1 Operators at origin: the recruitment chain in Bangladesh

At the origin end, the network is heavily pre-organised by Bangladeshi intermediaries:

· A smuggler ("dalal") in Bangladesh recruits the migrant, negotiates the journey, collects the payment (either fully
    in advance or in part via instalments) and arranges onward documentation.

· Recruitment sometimes began through casual encounters in community spaces such as tea stalls or
    mosques, where brokers identified vulnerable young men and used persuasive narratives to draw them into
    the migration process.

40          Navigating new corridors: the evolving route of Bangladeshi migration to Italy through Libya
· Respondents described being accompanied by a facilitator during parts of the departure process, including support
    through immigration and boarding. Such accounts point to the use of informal facilitation practices within airport
    environments and the high level of coordination involved in organising onward movement.

· Migrants are incentivised: some brokers offer discounts if the migrant brings friends with them, thereby increasing
    volume and reducing per-person cost.

· Families in Bangladesh often transfer funds to Bangladeshi bank accounts of the broker; sometimes payment
    occurs after arrival in Libya upon confirmation of safe arrival. This origin architecture underscores how the legal
    labour-migration apparatus is repurposed for irregular flows, leveraging Bangladesh's recruitment agencies,
    diaspora networks, and informal trust systems.

8.1.2 Transit facilitators and cross-border coordination

Between Bangladesh and Libya, transit facilitators play a crucial role. Examples include:

· Flights through the UAE, Kuwait, Egypt, and Syria where a local contact meets migrants at the gate, takes
    passports, and returns with boarding passes. In Syria, migrants reported that the facilitator even boarded the
    onward flight and travelled with them to Benghazi.

· In cases where migrants stopped in the UAE rather than transiting, Emirati facilitators managed the Dubai
    segment independently. Bangladeshi brokers subcontracted to these Emirati operators, who handled local
    logistics such as accommodation, visa processing, and airport clearance.

· Communication mechanisms were maintained throughout the journey via WhatsApp or Imo, allowing brokers to
    stay in contact with migrants, reinforce trust, and ensure compliance with payment and routing instructions.

This segment shows that medium-level facilitators coordinate handovers and transit flows to connect origin and
destination segments.

8.1.3 The Libyan segment: embedded partnerships

Upon arrival in Libya, the network morphs into a hybrid structure embedded within local conflict-economies. The
network needs someone in the east to obtain the entry clearance and receive the migrants in Benina International
Airport and someone in Zuwara to organise departure:

· Authorities in Benghazi airport require a sponsor to receive those arriving on the entry clearance. Bangladeshi
    facilitators, either those that travelled with migrants or facilitators based in Libya handle the airport arrival,
    transport to initial holding locations, and liaison with origin brokers. These facilitators often speak Arabic and have
    close ties with Libyan actors.

· Holding locations immediately after arrival are usually nationality-segregated (Bangladeshi exclusively)
    and managed by Bangladeshi smugglers; but once migrants reach the "game house" stage, they are moved to
    Libyan-run facilities that host mixed nationalities and Libyan smugglers are in charge.

· Libyan drivers dominate transportation between holding locations, across checkpoints, and to the final coastal
    departure points. They often change cars along the route between east and west Libya, and in some cases secure
    police or military escorts.

· Additional actors include Sudanese facilitators working with Libyan groups (in the game house or at the coast),
    Pakistani facilitators working with Bangladeshi groups, and local militia or security forces who might be paid to
    allow passage.

· Migrants reported a uniformed military officer firing three gunshots at the coastal departure point just before
    boarding. While the exact purpose is not clear, the act could serve as a signal to coast-guards or militia to allow
    passage, a display of authority to ensure compliance, or a timing cue for embarking crews.

The cooperation between Bangladeshi and Libyan actors illustrates how, in fragmented conflict settings like Libya,
smuggling networks rely on negotiated access rather than hierarchical control.

8.2 Financial and communication networks

Payment and communication channels are integral to system functioning:

· Payment flows: migrants pay their origin broker in Bangladesh (often before travel). After arrival in Libya, some
    pay additional amounts in the first holding location or at the game house. Migrants report being extorted by Libyan
    smugglers who demand more money despite the origin payment covering "the whole journey."

· "Game operator" selection: Bangladeshi brokers select a Libyan game-operator based on how much money
    the migrant has paid. Migrants emphasise that their success (or being abandoned entirely) often depends on this
    selection and the honesty of the Bangladeshi broker.

· Trust and reputation: Returning migrants often become brokers (dalals), signalling that the network reinvests its
    own clientele into its operator base.

· Communication: a "friend" in Italy often calls the migrant during the journey, presenting themselves as a trusted
    adviser but in reality acting on behalf of the smugglers. Their role is psychological: encouraging migrants to trust
    the network, comply with instructions, and make additional payments when requested. This function mirrors the
    pattern of returning migrants who later become brokers: both demonstrate how the network reinvests its own
    clientele into its operator base, turning former clients into facilitators who perpetuate the system.

These financial and communication practices reflect the business model of a modular smuggling "service", where each
actor profits from their segment and the migrant's dependency on onward movement enhances leverage.

8.3 Coordination, risk, and trust

While the network thrives on decentralisation, several themes stand out:

· Coordination: The segmentation requires coordination across countries and segments: for example, the
    Bangladeshi broker must liaise with the transit facilitator, who connects with the Libyan operator. Smugglers
    operate across origin, transit and destination nodes.

· Risk distribution: Each actor bears a different risk. Bangladeshi brokers risk reputation and client loss; transit
    facilitators risk detection at airports; Libyan operators risk interception by coastguards or rival militias. Migrants
    bear the highest risk of being abandoned, abuse, extortion or even death.

· Trust: Reputation matters. Migrants assess brokers based on peer networks. Brokers rely on performance (safe
    arrival, onward movement) to maintain clientele.

· Adaptive business model: As enforcement pressures shift, so do routes, actors and fees (for example, eastern to
    western movements through Libya). The network adapts, reinforcing the point that smugglers are highly responsive
    to border policy changes.

9.1.3 Secondary routes

A smaller but persistent flow of Bangladeshi migrants travels through the Eastern Mediterranean route, entering
Europe via Türkiye and Greece before continuing along the Western Balkans corridor. The route typically begins
in India, with migrants crossing on foot into Pakistan, here they join Pakistani migrants, continuing through Iran and
Türkiye, and eventually moving through Greece, North Macedonia, Serbia, and Slovenia towards Italy or other EU
Member States. Migrants on the Eastern Mediterranean route tend to demonstrate greater onward mobility within the
EU, although Italy is still a key destination for this flow also.

Another emerging pathway involves entry through Romania, facilitated by a bilateral Memorandum of
Understanding (MoU) between Bangladesh and Romania allowing for legal labour migration. Some Bangladeshi
migrants obtain Romanian work visas under the pretext of formal employment and then travel onwards to other
EU Member States once in Europe. Several key informants reported that certain migrants paid intermediaries for
employment and travel packages to Romania, only to discover upon arrival that no job existed. These individuals were
left stranded and subsequently moved irregularly towards Italy, often with the assistance of secondary smugglers
operating across Central Europe.

A number of European governments have expressed concern not about regular migration channels themselves, but
about the way intermediaries in Bangladesh may distort or exploit them. In 2024, for example, Denmark announced
tighter screening of student visas for Bangladeshi applicants following indications that some visa applications
were being facilitated by brokers for purposes other than genuine study. This development underscores a broader
governance challenge: vulnerabilities within origin-country recruitment ecosystems can affect visa categories that
are otherwise legitimate.

9.2 Regular v irregular migration

The boundary between regular and irregular migration is increasingly blurred in the Bangladesh­Libya­Italy
corridor. Many migrants begin with the intention of migrating legally, but limited knowledge, systemic barriers,
and reliance on intermediaries expose them to manipulation within formal migration frameworks. Legal pathways
such as Italy's Decreto Flussi and bilateral labour schemes are frequently repurposed by brokers, generating debt,
misinformation, and false expectations. As high costs, low success rates, and weak accountability undermine trust
in legal migration, irregular routes come to be seen not as unlawful choices but as rational responses to systems that
are inaccessible in practice.

9.2.1 Lack of knowledge

Interviews reveal that most Bangladeshi migrants do not clearly distinguish between regular and irregular
migration. For many, "legal" migration simply means travelling by plane with a valid passport, without full awareness
of the administrative processes involved. This limited understanding, combined with widespread misinformation,
leaves migrants vulnerable to manipulation. In the context of the hybrid system described earlier, where travel to Libya
often begins through legal channels, the boundary between regular and irregular migration becomes particularly
blurred. Several respondents reported only discovering that they were travelling irregularly once they reached Libya,
where they were informed that the final leg to Italy would take place by boat.

9.2.2 Manipulation of legal migration systems

Italy's Decreto Flussi framework is the main legal mechanism through which non-EU nationals can enter Italy for
work. It sets annual quotas for different sectors, such as agriculture, construction, and services, and applications are
processed online during designated "click days" on a strictly first-come, first-served basis. Employers, or accredited
intermediaries, must pre-compile applications on the government's ALI portal, and once the system opens, submissions
are accepted in chronological order until the quota is filled. In practice, this creates a race to submit applications within
seconds of the portal opening, and most slots are exhausted almost immediately.

While the system was designed to match labour demand with controlled migration, in reality it has become highly
exclusionary and vulnerable to exploitation. The technical and bureaucratic complexity of the process, combined with
the dependence on Italian employers to initiate applications, means that very few workers in Bangladesh can access
it directly. Instead, intermediaries and private agencies act as gatekeepers. They present themselves as authorised
facilitators of the decreto flussi, claiming they can secure nulla osta (work permits) or employer sponsorships for a fee.

These intermediaries frequently misuse or fabricate employer quotas. Some advertise non-existent Italian
companies or claim to have secured quotas that were already filled, while others resell the same limited number of
permits to multiple clients.

In Bangladesh, this system of deception has become deeply entrenched. Local recruitment agencies and dalals
advertise the decreto flussi as a guaranteed opportunity for legal migration, circulating fake job offers, falsified
company letters, and photocopies of genuine-looking Italian documents to reassure clients. Many set up temporary
offices during click-day periods, claiming direct connections with Italian employers or "inside contacts" at embassies.
Migrants, unable to verify the authenticity of these claims, often pay large sums in good faith, sometimes mortgaging
land or borrowing heavily. In some cases, families receive fabricated receipts or screenshots of online submissions,
giving the illusion that their applications are being processed in Italy. When months pass without updates, brokers
blame embassy delays or administrative backlogs, sustaining the illusion for as long as possible.

The scale of the problem is reflected in Italy's own data. In 2024, only 7.8 per cent of assigned places under the
decreto flussi resulted in completed work permits and stable jobs, and just 29 per cent of nulla osta led to visas
being issued.32 This demonstrates that there are serious structural issues within the system itself, which is creating
the fertile ground for manipulation and exploitation.

For Bangladeshi workers, these dynamics mean that even ostensibly legal migration routes operate through
opaque, profit-driven intermediaries. The line between authorised and unauthorised recruitment has effectively
collapsed, leaving migrants to navigate a formal system that, in practice, mirrors the logic of smuggling: pay an
intermediary, wait in uncertainty, and hope that a place within the quota materialises.

9.2.2.1 MOU with Malaysia

The exploitation of legal migration systems is not unique to Italy; the Bangladesh­Malaysia labour corridor shows
similar patterns. Under the 2012 G2G MoU, which was designed to cap worker fees at USD 420 and eliminate
private intermediaries, migrants instead paid USD 3,900­6,000, facilitated by dalals who misrepresented jobs and
wages. A 2023 Winrock study found extensive deception by recruitment agencies, while investigations uncovered
fake employers, bribery-based approval of job orders, and widespread passport confiscation, with 72 per cent of
workers reporting retention of documents. By 2025, Bangladesh had launched inquiries into agencies and former
lawmakers implicated in collusion and embezzlement, demonstrating how state-to-state migration schemes can be
hijacked by brokers, employers, and complicit officials.

9.2.2.2 Forced into irregular pathways

The other dimension that is relevant is that exploitation of regular migration systems can cause migrants to
accumulate serious debts that become another driver for irregular migration. Most migrants borrow heavily to
pay these fees, taking loans from local moneylenders, mortgaging family land, or selling livestock. They do so in
the belief that once in Italy they will earn enough to repay the debt within months. When they later discover that
their applications were fraudulent, or that no visa ever existed, they find themselves deeply indebted and socially
disgraced, having already told their families and communities that they were migrating to Europe. At this point, many
see no option but to pursue irregular channels, often turning to smugglers who promise to "complete" their journey
through Libya.

Interviews reveal how these debts are structured at the community level. In rural areas, migrants often borrow
from a village leader who functions as an informal moneylender, advancing funds specifically for migration-related
expenses. The loans are given against the deed of the borrower's house or family land, and interest begins to accrue
immediately. One respondent explained that he had borrowed from such a lender to pay both the migration fees and
a later ransom payment. By the time of the interview, he had already repaid 500,000 taka (approx. USD 5 000) but
still owed over 1 million (approx. USD 10 000), continuing to pay both interest and principal simultaneously. Crucially,
these lenders only extend credit to people who are migrating, viewing migration as collateral; they would not lend to
someone who stayed behind.

This dynamic creates a structural push factor toward irregular migration: once debt has been incurred on the
assumption of departure, the borrower must migrate by any means to repay it. For many, therefore, irregular movement
becomes not a choice but a financial necessity.

9.2.2.3 Accountability gaps

Within Bangladesh, the formal recruitment framework provides limited protection for workers who are defrauded
or whose applications fail. By law, recruitment agencies must compensate clients when employment arrangements
collapse, but in practice such complaints rarely reach the BMET. Most agencies settle disputes privately to avoid losing
their licences or reputation, leaving the government with incomplete visibility over the scale of abuse. This lack of data
makes it difficult to identify patterns of malpractice or to design effective regulatory responses.

9.2.3 When regular migration systems discourage legal migration

Even when no fraud is involved, the structure of legal migration systems itself can discourage migrants from
pursuing regular pathways. As seen above through the Italian example, formal visa procedures, whether through
embassy programmes, labour recruitment schemes, or bilateral agreements, are characterised by high administrative
costs, long processing times, and very low success rates.

Applicants are required to pay substantial non-refundable fees when submitting their visa applications. These
fees must be paid regardless of the outcome, and most applications are ultimately rejected. The process is also
lengthy and opaque. Securing the necessary paperwork, such as invitation letters or sponsorship confirmations, can
take months, followed by additional delays for verification and approval. Applicants receive little feedback on the
status of their case, and many are never formally notified of rejection.

Because of these realities, even fully legitimate channels can leave migrants financially depleted and disillusioned.
Many conclude that legal migration is not only expensive but also unattainable for ordinary workers. Smugglers and
brokers exploit this perception, contrasting the slow, bureaucratic nature of official procedures with the speed and
apparent certainty of irregular routes. They tell prospective migrants: "If you apply through the embassy, you will pay
and wait forever; if you come with me, you will reach Italy in days."

In this context, irregular migration becomes not a deliberate act of illegality but a rational response to systemic
inefficiency and exclusion. The very design of formal migration systems, which are costly, complex, and inaccessible,
creates conditions in which irregular routes appear faster, simpler, and more reliable, even for those who initially set
out to migrate lawfully.

10. Perspectives and key take-aways

10.1 Migration from Bangladesh to Italy via Libya is a
continuum, not a rupture

Migration from Bangladesh to Libya and Italy is best understood as the adaptive extension of long-standing labour
migration systems rather than a rupture from them. The same brokers, documentation agents, and social trust
structures that facilitate legal labour migration are repurposed to sustain irregular routes when regular pathways
become inaccessible or unprofitable. This continuity underscores that irregular migration is not born of chaos but of
structural limitations within formal systems, including costly and restrictive visa processes that leave aspiring workers
few alternatives. The Libya corridor reflects how a well-established migration infrastructure can mutate to serve
parallel, informal functions in response to both opportunity and constraint.

Implication: This suggests that protection-sensitive migration management should build on, rather than isolate
from, these existing systems, improving oversight, integrity, and fairness in recruitment and documentation channels
instead of approaching irregular migration as a detached criminal phenomenon.

10.2 Protection risks are embedded in formal systems

The report highlights that exploitation and deception occur not only along the smuggling route but within the formal
recruitment system itself. Licensed intermediaries, sub-agents, and financiers in Bangladesh often replicate the same
exploitative practices attributed to smugglers, such as inflated fees and false promises. Migrants' vulnerability is
therefore institutionalised before departure, reinforced through debt and dependence that bind them to their brokers.
Irregular movement becomes a continuation of exploitation rather than its starting point.

Implication: Efforts to enhance migrant protection may be most effective when focused on the recruitment and
financing stage: by regulating intermediaries, ensuring full contract transparency, introducing targeted pre-departure
counselling that demystifies hybrid routes, and strengthening financial literacy to reduce debt-based coercion.

10.3 Migrants' agency is real but constrained by systemic
inequities

The decision to migrate irregularly emerges not from ignorance but from constrained rationality, a negotiation between
aspiration, family pressure, and limited opportunity. Migrants are active decision-makers navigating systems stacked
against them, weighing known risks against the perceived dignity of progress. Their choices are social, collective, and
morally charged: refusing to migrate may mean disappointing family expectations or failing to repay communal loans.
Within this structure, agency exists but operates under structural duress.

Implication: Migration management frameworks might become more effective if they acknowledge this constrained
agency. Outreach and information efforts should not portray migrants as deceived or naïve but recognise the logic of
their choices, supporting them through safer, affordable, and legitimate options that preserve dignity and social mobility.

10.4 Libya's hybrid migration system reflects governance gaps,
not chaos

Eastern Libya's semi-formal visa and sponsorship regime demonstrates how migration management emerges in
fragmented governance contexts. The system's functionality, with entry authorisations issued through the Military
Investment Authority and local sponsors managing arrivals, shows a structured, revenue-generating model that fills
the vacuum of absent national coordination. Far from anarchy, this represents an adaptive form of governance rooted
in local authority and economic pragmatism.

Implication: Constructive engagement with Libyan authorities may therefore benefit from recognising and
strengthening these emergent governance mechanisms. Rather than focusing solely on interdiction, partnerships
could support standardisation, oversight, and human rights compliance within these local systems to promote stability
and accountability.

10.5 The protection economy thrives on predictability and trust

Smuggling networks depend on the social and financial reliability of migrants' families, who act as predictable payers.
Trust, in the broker, the process, and the promise of arrival, sustains the system. Families often guarantee repayment
through social collateral and emotional obligation, making trust itself a tool of control. This "protection economy"
thrives not through anonymity but through community legitimacy.

Implication: To reduce vulnerability, protection responses could target this trust-based architecture: expanding access
to transparent credit and ethical lending, supporting family-level risk education, and developing safe community
finance systems that reduce dependence on exploitative brokers.

10.6 Libya's conflict economy turns migrants into commodities

The Libyan context transforms migration into an economy of control. Fees for security clearance, passage, and
detention releases flow directly into the coffers of armed groups and intermediaries. Migrants' presence is monetised
at every stage, from recruitment to sea departure, thereby sustaining local economies of power. This dynamic blurs
the line between state and militia, as control is exercised through formal procedures enforced by informal actors.
Exploitation becomes systemic rather than incidental.

Implication: Migration partnerships and donor engagement in Libya could be strengthened by incorporating political
economy and conflict analysis into programme design. This would ensure that migration management does not
unintentionally reinforce the very power structures that profit from migrant vulnerability.

10.7 Smuggling and trafficking exist on a continuum

The boundaries between smuggling and trafficking in this corridor are porous. Migrants frequently move between
voluntary facilitation and coercive exploitation depending on payment failure, capture, or debt escalation. These
transitions are fluid and often shaped by circumstance, rather thannot intent. The legal separation between smuggling
and trafficking thus obscures how exploitation actually unfolds on the ground.

Implication: Protection and legal frameworks could become more effective by adopting continuum-based approaches.
This would involve linking anti-smuggling and anti-trafficking responses under shared coordination and ensuring that
assistance is based on vulnerability and harm rather than rigid classifications.

10.8 The family is both protector and vector

Family networks sustain migration emotionally, financially, and logistically, but they can also amplify risk. Debt taken
in the name of family honour or survival becomes a powerful coercive tool, compelling migrants to continue even when
conditions deteriorate and risks increase. The same kinship systems that provide trust and solidarity can entrench
cycles of pressure and silence around abuse.

Implication: Protection strategies could therefore incorporate family-oriented interventions, combining psychosocial
counselling, remittance management guidance, and awareness of intergenerational pressures, to empower families
as informed decision-makers rather than passive enablers of risk.

10.9 Diaspora networks shape both risk and resilience

The Bangladeshi diaspora in Italy operates as both an aspirational reference and a practical support structure.
Success stories shared through social media reinforce migration aspirations, while established community members
provide employment connections, shelter, and advice upon arrival. The diaspora thus forms a dual mechanism, driving
mobility but also cushioning integration.

Implication: Migration management could more effectively engage diaspora communities as allies in protection and
ethical recruitment. This could include structured mentorship programmes, support for community-based reintegration
initiatives, and partnerships that channel remittances toward safe and legal mobility options.

10.10 Route-based protection requires multi-country
governance

The Bangladesh­Libya­Italy corridor functions as an interdependent system spanning multiple jurisdictions, where
interventions at one point reverberate across the chain. Crackdowns in Libya or new visa restrictions in the Gulf simply
displace risk geographically. Uncoordinated national responses therefore perpetuate circular harm.

Implication: Comprehensive migration management could benefit from a route-based framework that connects
origin, transit, and destination states through shared data, protection mechanisms, and mobility pathways. Such
cooperation would integrate labour, humanitarian, and security dimensions to balance control with protection.

10.11 Information and empowerment, not deterrence

The study finds that deterrence campaigns fail because migrants are guided by social credibility, not official warnings.
Trust flows through personal networks, and perceived success outweighs risk narratives. Deterrence alone cannot
counteract the social legitimacy of migration.

Implication: Information strategies may be more effective when they focus on empowerment and credibility, using
trusted voices such as returnees, community elders, or diaspora figures to communicate realistic costs and conditions,
and framing migration choices in terms of agency rather than fear.

10.12 Governance must integrate economic, social, and
humanitarian dimensions

Irregular migration persists because governance systems remain fragmented: labour, border, and protection
institutions operate in silos, leaving space for intermediaries to thrive. The lack of cross-sector coordination reproduces
vulnerability and erodes accountability.

Implication: Comprehensive migration management could advance by bridging these divides: aligning labour, security,
and social protection frameworks through shared oversight and joint planning. Integrated governance would enable
a more holistic approach that balances development, regulation, and rights.

Three interlocking systems underpinning the Bangladesh­
Libya­Italy corridor

The findings of this study demonstrate that irregular migration from Bangladesh to Italy through Libya is not a
disorderly or fragmented phenomenon. It is the outcome of three interlocking systems that together shape migrants'
decisions, enable their movement, and produce the patterns of exploitation observed along the route. Understanding
these systems as connected structures, rather than isolated events, offers a clearer view of why this corridor has
expanded and why it has proven so resilient to policy interventions.

System 1. Bangladesh's migration­debt­intermediary
ecosystem

Bangladesh's economy is structurally reliant on overseas employment, and migration is embedded in household
strategies, community expectations, and national economic stability. Within this environment, a dense network of
recruitment agencies, sub-agents, travel intermediaries, and informal lenders has emerged to facilitate mobility.
These actors do not operate separately from irregular migration; rather, the same brokers who organise regular labour
migration often work with irregular facilitators, using the infrastructure of legal mobility, such as passports, visas, and
commercial flights, to initiate journeys that ultimately lead to Libya. High recruitment costs, widespread indebtedness,
and limited transparency create the conditions in which migrants become dependent on intermediaries, making them
more vulnerable to deception, coercion, and irregular pathways when regular options fail or become inaccessible. This
system establishes the vulnerability profile that travels with migrants across borders.

System 2. The transnational facilitation network connecting
Bangladesh to Libya

Movement out of Bangladesh is not characterised by illegal border crossings but by a modular chain of facilitators
operating through Dubai, Egypt, Kuwait, India, Sri Lanka, Turkey, and formerly Syria. Each node manages one segment
of the journey, such as documentation, airport passage, accommodation, or transit coordination, before handing
migrants to the next. The route is highly adaptive: when scrutiny increases over flights to Dubai, brokers redirect
travellers through India or Sri Lanka. This is not a single organisation but a distributed service economy responding to
enforcement pressures and opportunities. The legality of the early stages of travel, the use of visas, flights, and formal
documentation, obscures the point at which migration becomes irregular, blurring the line between authorised travel
and broker-managed movement. This system ensures a reliable flow into Libya regardless of changing controls at
origin or transit points.

System 3. Libya's hybrid governance­smuggling economy

Libya functions simultaneously as a destination for labour migrants, a transit point for those moving onward to Europe,
and a political economy in which mobility itself is monetised. Since 2020, entry permissions issued in eastern Libya
have allowed migrants to arrive legally at Benina Airport, where Bangladeshi and Libyan facilitators manage reception,
registration, and movement across the east­west divide. Armed groups, local authorities, and coastal smuggling
networks operate through a territorial handover model: eastern actors facilitate entry and westward passage;
western coastal groups manage maritime departures. These are not competing structures but coordinated hubs in
a larger economy in which each actor extracts revenue at its node. Shifts in enforcement; for example, crackdowns
on eastern departures in late 2023, lead not to disruption but to internal re-routing, such as the transfer of migrants
from Benghazi to Zuwara and Zawiya. Within this system, the boundary between smuggling and trafficking is fluid,
shaped less by intent than by payment failure, delay, or capture. Extortion, debt bondage, and coercion are structural
features, not aberrations.

Together, these three systems form a coherent architecture. Bangladesh's migration and debt dynamics create
the conditions that drive people into the corridor; the transnational facilitation system moves them efficiently across
borders; and Libya's hybrid governance economy absorbs them into a revenue-generating structure that channels
them toward Italy. Each system reinforces the others, producing a high-throughput migration corridor that cannot be
understood or addressed through interventions targeted at a single stage of the journey. Efforts to strengthen regular
migration pathways, reduce debt-driven vulnerability, improve oversight of intermediaries, or support route-based
protection mechanisms will only be effective if they recognise this systemic interdependence.

Ultimately, the Bangladesh­Libya­Italy corridor is not sustained by irregular actors alone, but by structural
conditions, institutional gaps, and economic incentives that span origin, transit, and destination. A systems-based
understanding of these dynamics is essential for developing migration policies that are both effective and protection-
sensitive, and for designing responses that address not only the symptoms of irregular migration but the deeper
structures that sustain it.
