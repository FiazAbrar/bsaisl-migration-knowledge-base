When Dalia returned to Bangladesh, she had to use a temporary travel document because her passport had been confiscated in Saudi Arabia.

During her 14 months there, she had been admitted to hospital and moved to a shelter in Riyadh after what she described as repeated sexual abuse and beatings.

For more than a decade, Al Amin Noyon has met women like Dalia at Dhaka's international airport. As manager of the BRAC Migration Welfare Centre, which shelters workers who have suffered abuse abroad, he has received women bearing injustices and others showing signs of psychological distress. His work includes finding their families.

"Our state, our ministry, or the airport authority — none of them has the actual statistics on how many women are returning, whether as victims of torture or fraud, or for whatever reason they come back," he said.

Netra News obtained from the centre a list of 138 women who returned from domestic work in Saudi Arabia between March 2018 and August 2024, and interviewed 48 returnees. Their accounts included beatings, sexual abuse, unpaid wages and transfers between employers without their consent.

Their accounts, and interviews with officials, point to gaps in the help available to women trying to leave abusive households. The problems ranged from a helpline that could not be reached to safe homes without a psychosocial counsellor.

Other women described being confined and then sold from one household to another.

Saudi Arabia introduced reforms in 2021 intended to make it easier for some private sector workers to change jobs and obtain exit and re-entry visas. Domestic workers were excluded from those changes, Al-Kheetan said.

At the centre of that dependence is kafala, a sponsorship system that ties a migrant worker's legal status to an employer. Despite successive reforms, its central controls remain in place, according to migrant rights organisations.

Bangladesh funds two safe homes for women in Saudi Arabia through its expatriates' welfare ministry, Noyon said. But, he added, "there are no women workers or officials of ours there who would support these women."

"At times, the embassy's safe house in Riyadh has sheltered as many as 300 workers at once."

Between 2021 and June 2026, the bodies of 250 Bangladeshi women who had worked as domestic workers in Saudi Arabia were brought back to Bangladesh, according to the Wage Earners Welfare Board. Thirteen were repatriated in the first six months of 2026.

In a separate tally covering 2021 to July 2025, the board recorded at least 64 deaths by suicide among Bangladeshi women working as domestic help in Saudi Arabia.

Those figures do not establish how many deaths resulted from abuse. But rights groups have repeatedly questioned how migrant workers' deaths in the country are recorded and investigated.

In its World Report 2026, Human Rights Watch said Saudi authorities had failed to protect workers from preventable deaths, investigate workplace safety incidents or ensure adequate compensation for families. It said many deaths were wrongly classified as natural, leaving them uninvestigated and uncompensated.

One case that did reach a Saudi court concerned Abiron Begum Ansar. When her body was returned to Bangladesh in 2019, her family said she was "unrecognisable". Relatives told The Daily Star that, before her death, she had complained of beatings and of her head being pressed against a hot iron grille.

In 2021, a Riyadh court sentenced her employer, Ayesha al-Jazani, to death for her murder, Middle East Eye reported. Her family had requested the death penalty rather than financial compensation.

Between 1991 and 2021, 372,192 Bangladeshi women went to Saudi Arabia for domestic work, according to the Bureau of Manpower, Employment and Training.

Other countries sending domestic workers to the kingdom have imposed conditions or restricted recruitment over concerns about their treatment. India has required minimum pay, a minimum age and a financial guarantee from employers. The Philippines and Indonesia have suspended recruitment at different times.

On 6th October 2025, the two countries signed their first agreement governing the recruitment of general workers.

Researchers who reviewed the 2025 pact said it did not specify working hours, a minimum wage, workplace standards or a clear way for workers to seek redress for abuse. They also criticised what they described as a failure to hold employers accountable.

Munir said recruitment under the agreement would go through private agencies rather than Bangladesh's state recruitment agency, BOESL, making independent oversight harder. Agreements should be public, he argued, and drafts should be shared with workers and advocates before they were finalised.
