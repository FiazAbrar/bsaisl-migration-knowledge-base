Tasattur in Saudi Arabia: The Five Arrangements That Count as Concealment, and the Legal Way to Do Each

SF

By Syed Farasat, founder and editor

Published October 24, 2026 · Updated November 5, 2026 · 8 min read

Tasattur in Saudi Arabia (التستر التجاري) is a Saudi letting a non-Saudi run a business for the expat's own account under the Saudi's licence or commercial register. The Anti-Concealment Law, issued by Royal Decree M/4 of 1/1/1442H (20 August 2020), makes both sides criminals: up to five years in prison, a fine of up to SAR 5 million, confiscation of the proceeds and deportation of the non-Saudi.

Most expats in a concealment case did not set out to break the law. They bought a running shop from a friend, or agreed a "partnership" on a handshake. Below are the five arrangements the Ministry of Commerce treats as concealment and the legal structure that does the same job.

What the Anti-Concealment Law actually says

The law (نظام مكافحة التستر) was approved by Council of Ministers Decision 785 and Royal Decree M/4 in August 2020, published in Umm Al-Qura on 28 August 2020, and under Article 20 came into force 180 days later, in late February 2021. It replaced the 2004 law (Royal Decree M/22 of 1425H), which had carried a maximum of two years and SAR 1 million. The Ministry of Commerce enforces it, the Public Prosecution investigates and the Criminal Court tries the cases (Article 5).

Three definitions carry the whole law:

  • Economic activity (Article 1) is any profit-seeking activity that needs an approval or licence, whether commercial, investment, service, professional, industrial or agricultural.
  • Concealment (Article 2) is any agreement or arrangement that lets a non-Saudi practise an economic activity they are not licensed for, using the licence or approval issued to the concealer.
  • The crimes (Article 3) are: (a) the Saudi enabling it, including by lending his name, licence, commercial register or trade name; (b) the non-Saudi practising the activity for his own account through that person; (c) anyone who knowingly incites, assists or advises; and (d) obstructing inspectors or giving them false information.

Article 4 adds three lesser violations that are punished administratively rather than criminally: an establishment giving a non-Saudi, irregularly, instruments that amount to absolute control over it; a non-Saudi holding or using such instruments; and an establishment running its business through a bank account that does not belong to it.

The five arrangements that count as concealment

In September 2021 the Ministry of Commerce listed seven tell-tale practices. They collapse into five arrangements, each with a lawful equivalent.

Intent is not the test. The law looks at who controls the activity and who takes the profit. For an expat on a work iqama, the Labor Law already bars working for your own account, as our guide to working for another employer explains, so arrangement 1 breaks two laws at once.

Deportation under Article 11 is written into the conviction, not decided later. It comes after the sentence is served and after fines, Zakat and tax are settled. Expats who imagine the worst case is "being sent home" have the order of events backwards.

The correction programme, and why it is over

Royal Decree M/4 itself ordered a one-time way out. Item Two of the decree ordered a mechanism for violators of the old law who were still in breach when the new one took effect. Anyone who applied to the Ministry of Commerce within 180 days of the law coming into force was exempted from the penalties of both laws and from paying income tax retroactively. The exemption excluded anyone who began a violation under the new law, anyone already caught, and anyone already referred to the Public Prosecution or the court.

The National Program to Combat Commercial Concealment opened that window in early 2021. Arab News reported on 4 March 2021 that it would run six months to 23 August 2021. In September 2021 the ministry extended it to 16 February 2022, and in February 2022 it confirmed the period had ended on that date, publishing examples of manufacturers and retailers that had legalised through it.

What options did the programme offer?

The ministry listed them consistently: bring in the non-Saudi as a licensed partner; register ownership in the non-Saudi's name once he met the legal requirements; keep the business going by adding a new Saudi or licensed foreign partner; let the Saudi sell, assign or dissolve the business; let the non-Saudi obtain Premium Residency and regularise; or let the non-Saudi leave the Kingdom permanently after giving up his claims. Those same six routes, minus the amnesty, are still the only ways to unwind an arrangement today.

No new correction period has been announced since February 2022. Posts claiming an open "tasattur amnesty" in 2026 are not backed by any Ministry of Commerce announcement we could find.

Legal ways for an expat to own a business

Investment registration with MISA

The Investment Law (Royal Decree M/19 of 16/1/1446H, 22 July 2024) took effect in February 2025 and replaced foreign investment licensing with registration. Article 7(2) requires a foreign investor to register with the Ministry of Investment before investing; after that the investor obtains a commercial register and any sector licences like a local business, and Article 4 promises equal treatment of local and foreign investors. MISA says registration is issued to legal entities, foreign companies and individuals who hold Premium Residency, with an entrepreneur track for start-ups and a review period of up to ten days. The investor care line is 8002449990. Steps and costs are in our business set-up guide.

Premium Residency

Premium Residency holders can obtain investment registration in their own name and are exempt from submitting a foreign commercial register and financial statements, which is exactly the gap that pushes ordinary iqama holders into concealment. The products, fees and eligibility are covered in our Premium Residency guide.

A registered foreign company

If you own a company abroad, it can register with MISA and open a Saudi entity or branch. The business belongs to the company, the bank account is in the entity's name, and you can be its registered manager.

Being a well-paid manager instead of a hidden owner

Not every expat needs to own the business. A Qiwa contract as general manager with a salary and a documented commission, paid through the Wage Protection System, is legal, accrues end-of-service (see the end-of-service calculator) and leaves the Saudi owner genuinely in charge. What it cannot include is the takings landing in your personal account or you holding blank cheques.

What people get wrong about tasattur

"I only hold a power of attorney, so I am the victim if anything goes wrong." The opposite. Holding instruments of absolute control is an Article 4 violation in its own right, and if you are trading for your own account through them you are the Article 3(b) offender, with deportation attached.

"A small grocery or a mobile shop is below the radar." Article 1 covers any licensed profit-seeking activity. Inspection powers in Article 6 extend to any site where the activity is carried on and to CCTV footage, and officers from municipalities, HRSD and ZATCA share them, so a routine municipal visit can start a concealment file. In October 2026 alone the National Program to Combat Commercial Concealment carried out 3,562 inspection rounds, covering restaurants, groceries and nut, coffee and spice shops, and referred 96 suspected cases (Al-Jazirah).

"My kafeel agreed, so it is a private matter." Consent of the Saudi is the crime, not a defence. Article 15 also voids the private contract between you, so the money you put in cannot be recovered through the courts if the arrangement collapses.

"If I get caught I will just be deported." Deportation comes last, after prison, fines, confiscation and settlement of Zakat and tax. Accounts are frozen while that happens; our guide to frozen bank accounts explains what a freeze looks like from the customer's side.

If you are inside an arrangement now

There is no amnesty, but there are better and worse ways out. The cleanest is to convert the business into a registered structure through MISA or Premium Residency, which usually means the Saudi party formally selling or admitting a partner. The second is to wind it down: the Saudi closes or sells the register and the expat exits, if leaving the country on a final exit visa with his affairs settled. Article 13 rewards self-reporting only when it leads to arrests or seizures, so it suits someone who was drawn in, not the person who ran the scheme.

Whichever route, move the money first. Revenue running through a personal account is the evidence that turns suspicion into a case, and under Article 8 the Public Prosecution can freeze it for 60 days before anyone is charged.

Frequently asked questions

What is tasattur in Saudi Arabia?

Tasattur (commercial concealment) is defined in Article 2 of the Anti-Concealment Law as any agreement or arrangement through which a person enables a non-Saudi to practise an economic activity in the Kingdom that the non-Saudi is not licensed for, using the licence or approval issued to the concealer. The classic case is a Saudi citizen whose commercial register covers a shop, workshop or contracting firm that an expat actually owns and runs, with the citizen paid a monthly or yearly sum. Both the Saudi and the non-Saudi commit a crime under Article 3.

What is the penalty for tasattur in Saudi Arabia?

Article 9 of the Anti-Concealment Law sets imprisonment of up to five years and a fine of up to SAR 5 million, or either, with the penalty doubled for a repeat offence within three years. On conviction the court also confiscates the proceeds (Article 10), publishes a summary of the judgment (Article 11), deports the non-Saudi after the sentence and bans re-entry (Article 11), dissolves the establishment, strikes its commercial register and bans the Saudi from business for five years (Article 12). Zakat, tax and fees owed by the business are collected from both jointly.

Can an expat legally own a business in Saudi Arabia?

Yes, through the Ministry of Investment. Under the Investment Law of 2024, in force from February 2025, a foreign investor registers with MISA before investing and then obtains a commercial register and sector licences in the normal way; registration replaced the old foreign investment licence. MISA says it issues registration to legal entities, foreign companies and individuals who hold Premium Residency, and that applications are reviewed within up to ten days. An expat on an ordinary work iqama without a foreign company behind them generally needs Premium Residency or a registered foreign entity to own the business.

Is the tasattur correction period still open?

No. Royal Decree M/4 ordered a one-time correction mechanism giving violators 180 days from the law taking effect to apply to the Ministry of Commerce and be exempted from penalties and from retroactive income tax. The window closed on 23 August 2021, was extended once, and finally ended on 16 February 2022. Anyone still in a concealment arrangement today has no amnesty to rely on. The only mitigation left in the law is Article 13, which lets the court reduce or waive penalties for a participant who reports the crime before it is discovered.

Is the non-Saudi also punished for tasattur, or only the Saudi?

Both. Article 3(a) criminalises the Saudi who enables the activity and Article 3(b) criminalises the non-Saudi who practises an economic activity for their own account through that person. Article 3(c) adds anyone who incites, assists or advises with knowledge. The same prison term and fine apply to all of them, and the non-Saudi is additionally deported after serving the sentence and paying what the court orders, then barred from re-entering the Kingdom under Article 11.

How do I report commercial concealment in Saudi Arabia?

The Ministry of Commerce is the body that receives reports, inspects and seizes under Article 5 of the law, working with officers from the municipalities, HRSD, agriculture and ZATCA who have judicial-seizure powers under Article 6. Reports go through the ministry's official channels on mc.gov.sa. Article 18 requires the identity of the person reporting to be kept in a confidential register and out of the case file, and allows a reward of up to 30 percent of the fine collected once the judgment or decision becomes final.
