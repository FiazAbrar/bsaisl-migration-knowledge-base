    ABSTRACT
    Saudi Arabia’s Labor Reform Initiative represents an important step toward
    a more flexible, competitive labor market with greater worker autonomy.
    Introduced in March 2021 as part of Vision 2030 and the National Trans-
    formation Program, the reform eased key restrictions on expatriate work-
    ers’ mobility by allowing eligible workers to change employers and manage
    exit and re-entry procedures with greater autonomy. This report examines
    how the reform has shaped labor mobility, worker outcomes, and employer
    behavior in Saudi Arabia’s private sector, drawing on administrative data,
    surveys, and qualitative interviews. The findings indicate that removing ob-
    stacles to expatriate mobility increased job-to-job transitions, particularly
    among low-skilled expatriate workers, and was associated with improved
    earning prospects for expatriates who changed jobs. Survey and interview
    evidence further suggests that the reform has strengthened workers’ bar-
    gaining position and increased competition among firms for expatriate la-
    bor, prompting some employers to adjust retention practices and working
    conditions. The analysis also points to opportunities to strengthen the re-
    form’s impact by helping smaller and labor-intensive firms adapt to a more
    mobile labor market, including through better workforce planning, stronger
    human resource practices, improved access to skills, and incentives to invest
    in training and worker retention. Overall, the Labor Reform Initiative offers
    important lessons for advancing labor market flexibility, worker protection,
    and Saudi Arabia’s long-term economic transformation.

    INTRODUCTION


    Labor mobility refers to the ability of workers to move freely between
    jobs, occupations, industries, or geographic locations in search of em-
    ployment opportunities. Several factors determine the extent of a country’s
    labor mobility, including economic incentives, social conditions, individual
    circumstances, and regulatory environments. For example, areas with strong
    economic growth and high labor demand can attract more workers. Family
    and friends can either encourage relocation through connections in new
    areas or act as a constraint. Additional considerations like climate, health
    care, and access to education may affect the decision to relocate. Finally,
    restrictive policies may also influence employers’ or employees’ readiness
    and ability to move.

    In March 2021, the Saudi Arabian government introduced the Labor Reform
    Initiative (LRI), removing some legal restrictions on expatriate workers’
    labor mobility. As part of the National Transformation Program and Vision
    2030, the policy aimed to attract high–skilled foreign talent, improve the
    working environment, and boost productivity, which is crucial for achieving
    economic goals. The LRI enables expatriate workers to change jobs and exit
    or re–enter the country without their current employer’s prior consent, sig-
    nificantly improving their autonomy and working conditions. The LRI was
    introduced alongside a series of policy reforms, including initiatives to attract
    and retain talent in the country, such as visas for highly skilled individuals
    and investors under the premium residency system.

    International evidence suggests that enhanced labor mobility for expatri-
    ate workers could profoundly improve Saudi Arabia’s economic landscape
    by benefiting both employees and firms. The literature demonstrates that
    reallocating workers to sectors and regions with higher demand increases
    competitiveness and strengthens economic resilience (Castellanos and
    Heutel 2024). For expatriates, the freedom to switch employers under the
    LRI enables them to find positions that better match their skills and exper-
    tise, resulting in higher productivity and greater job satisfaction (Boschma,
    Eriksson, and Lindgren 2014). This flexibility also motivates workers to invest

     in their professional development, cultivating a more dynamic and skilled
     workforce. As workers gain more autonomy in choosing their employment
     opportunities, the labor market becomes more competitive, driving higher
     wages and improved working conditions for all participants (Danninger
     2016). For businesses, enhanced labor mobility serves as a catalyst for great-
     er efficiency and innovation (Braunerhjelm, Ding, and Thulin 2020). With
     access to a larger, more competitive talent pool, firms can better align their
     workforce with their specific needs, particularly in specialized sectors.
     Empirical evidence shows that the shortage of specialized labor is one of
     the main barriers to adopting more efficient technologies (Carroni, Delogu,
     and Pulina 2023). By intensifying competition for talent, firms are pushed to
     attract and retain the specialized workers needed to implement these tech-
     nologies, thereby encouraging modernization of their operations. As firms
     overcome labor constraints and adopt advanced technologies, they achieve
     higher productivity levels and enhance the long–term competitiveness of
     Saudi Arabian industries.

     Although the LRI is in place and international studies suggest that greater
     labor mobility can improve worker and firm outcomes, there is limited
     empirical evidence establishing a causal relationship between the reform
     and foreign workers’ mobility and wages. This paper aims to address this
     empirical gap and contribute to the literature by providing novel causal
     evidence on the subject. The paper uses administrative data1 to explore the
     labor mobility trends in Saudi Arabia over the past few years and analyze the
     impact of the LRI on worker mobility and wages. It also complements the
     findings from the administrative data analysis with evidence from employer
     and employee surveys and qualitative interviews.2

     The paper applies the difference–in–differences approach3 to isolate
     the impact of the LRI on mobility and wages. It compares labor market
     outcomes before and after the reform for two distinct groups: those affect-
     ed by the reform and those not influenced due to ineligibility (specifically,
     workers who have been in the Kingdom for less than one year). By ensuring
     that both groups are similar in other relevant characteristics, any observed
     changes from the pre–LRI to post–LRI period (such as differences in labor
     mobility) can be attributed directly to the reform itself via the difference–
     in–differences approach.



     1. The administrative data used in this study include microdata on workers and firms from the General Organization for Social Insurance (GOSI) and
     data from the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development’s (MHRSD) Nitaqat initiative for the period 2017—2024.
     2. A survey of private–sector employers who experienced expatriate mobility was conducted in May 2024. Out of 438 respondents who initiated the sur-
     vey, 234 provided responses. The survey captured insights from both origin and destination firms. Additionally, an online survey targeting employees
     who transferred under the new policy was conducted by the Qiwa platform in July 2024, receiving responses from over 49,000 participants. Finally, 48
     in–depth interviews with employers were carried out in June 2024 to provide further qualitative insights.
     3. The paper applies a difference–in–differences approach to isolate the impact of the reform on mobility and wages. While the strategy follows
     standard practice and includes robustness checks, the estimates should be interpreted with the usual caution regarding potential unobserved factors.
     Throughout the paper, we refer to these estimates as impacts of the reform.

     The paper presents several conclusions. It shows that the rate at which
     nationals and expatriates switch jobs increased over the years. Job–to–job
     transitions for expatriate workers over the past five years were most frequent in
     microfirms and among low–skilled workers. Although most workers remained
     in the same region after transitioning jobs, many found employment across
     different economic sectors. The paper also concludes, based on the impact
     evaluation, that the LRI had its greatest positive effect on labor mobility
     among expatriate workers with lower skill levels. Additionally, regression
     analysis shows a positive relationship between the LRI and the wages of
     workers experiencing job transitions. Finally, qualitative analysis supports the
     quantitative results. Interviews indicated that expatriate workers can explore
     better opportunities with the LRI and that they are asking for higher pay and
     negotiating with their employers. This has created more competition among
     firms for expatriate workers. Firms also highlighted the increased flexibility
     to hire relevant talent with local experience at lower recruitment costs and
     shorter waiting times, providing indictive evidence that the LRI may have
     facilitated the retention of skilled expatriate talent within the Saudi market,
     though these insights might be shaped by respondent selection or changing
     labor–market conditions.

     The paper is organized into seven sections. This section provided an intro-
     duction to labor mobility in Saudi Arabia. Section two explores the literature
     on the economic effects of labor mobility. Sections three and four provide a
     comprehensive overview of the Saudi Arabian private–sector labor market
     as well as the LRI and the legislative changes it introduced. Section five
     portrays labor market trends as well as the characteristics of workers who
     are more likely to switch jobs and their labor market outcomes. Section six
     presents the estimated causal effects of the policy, section seven sums up the
     authors’ conclusions.

      EXPLORING
     THE EVIDENCE
          LITERATURE INSIGHTS ON LABOR MOBILITY
                AND ITS ECONOMIC EFFECTS
     Empirical and theoretical studies have identified multiple positive effects
     of labor mobility on workers and the economy. Understanding the intricate
     mechanisms driving labor mobility, as well as its underlying causes and effects,
     is crucial not only for comprehending the functioning of labor markets but
     also for formulating effective policies to enhance economic efficiency and
     individual welfare. This section presents empirical evidence on the effects
     of labor mobility on economic growth, productivity, innovation, resilience,
     and wages.

     Labor mobility facilitates economic growth and productivity. It allows
     workers to move to regions or industries where their skills are in higher
     demand, thereby enabling a more efficient use of human resources. This
     improved labor allocation can enhance economic output and productivity.
     Estimates for Indonesia show a 22 percent increase in labor productivity gain
     if all barriers are removed and an 8 percent increase if the migration costs
     are reduced to the United States level (Bryan and Morten 2019). However, the
     effects on productivity are not homogeneous. In Sweden a study has found
     that intraregional labor mobility between skill–related industries increases
     labor productivity and employment growth more than intraindustry mobility
     and mobility across unrelated industries (Boschma, Eriksson, and Lindgren
     2014). In addition, a study of 12 European Union (EU) countries revealed that
     workers moving from high–tech and medium–tech industries tend to create
     positive productivity effects for the receiving industry. However, those coming
     from low–tech industries have no significant impact, or may even produce
     negative results (Foster–McGregor and Pöschl 2016).

     Labor mobility boosts creativity and technological advancements by accel-
     erating the exchange of diverse skills and ideas. As workers relocate, they
     bring unique experiences and perspectives, which can lead to the development
     of new solutions and innovative practices. Braunerhjelm, Ding, and Thulin
     (2020) used a matched Swedish employer—employee dataset and firm–level
     econometric models to show that when skilled workers move between firms,
     especially innovative ones, patenting significantly increases through knowledge


13   Saudi Arabia’s Labor Mobility Reforms: A New Era for Foreign Workers and the Labor Market
     transfer. Similarly, Kaiser, Kongsted, and Rønde (2015) demonstrated that
     mobility between patent–active firms substantially raises total innovation,
     as incoming workers from such firms are far more productive in generating
     new patents. These findings are supported by Xu et al. (2024), who used a
     sample of Chinese workers to emphasize the role of knowledge diffusion as
     a mediator through which labor mobility enhances firm innovation. Finally,
     Jia et al. (2022) studied the effect of top management’s labor mobility experi-
     ence on firm innovation. Their findings suggest that job mobility experience
     is positively associated with a firm’s engagement in exploratory innovation
     strategies that generate new knowledge.

     Labor mobility enhances country resilience to both external and internal
     shocks. The recent financial crisis, which erupted in 2008, allowed scholars
     to investigate whether labor mobility could influence a country’s ability to
     withstand and recover from economic shocks. Håkansson and Bejaković (2020)
     studied the role of labor mobility’s in reducing labor market bottlenecks and
     increasing resilience in Croatia. They find that due to low levels of residential
     mobility, the Croatian labor market adapts poorly not only to recession, but
     also an economic boom. In Latvia, Fadejeva and Opmane (2016) found that
     internal labor market mobility supported postcrisis recovery by enabling rapid
     worker reallocation across jobs, sectors, and contract types. This flexibility
     helped restore competitiveness without currency devaluation, cushioned
     unemployment through part–time and temporary work, and strengthened
     the economy’s capacity to adapt to future internal and external shocks.

     Finally, a mobile labor market has crucial implications for resilience not
     just to external but also to internal shocks, such as specific policies, par-
     ticularly in the context of climate change. Castellanos and Heutel (2024) used
     a computable general equilibrium model of the United States to study the
     effects of a carbon tax on aggregate unemployment. They found that under
     the perfect immobility assumption, a US$45–per–ton carbon tax increases
     unemployment in the coal sector by 13 percentage points. Therefore, labor
     mobility is a key factor in mitigating the adverse impacts of both external
     and internal economic adjustments.

     Studies generally agree that workers who switch jobs experience wage
     increase (Bernard, Finnie, and St–Jean 2008; Campbell 2001; Danninger
     2016; Pavlopoulos et al. 2007; Perticara 2004; Yankov 2022). However,
     numerous challenges arise for scholars when analyzing mobility for various
     reasons. Mobility is a dynamic process involving workers who are often self–
     selected; movers tend to be more ambitious, more willing to take risks, and
     possess better skills and abilities than nonmovers. Ideally, researchers should
     isolate the impact of mobility by examining changes in the situation of the
     same individuals before and after they move and comparing these changes
     with those of “stayers” who have similar observable characteristics, which
     is rarely an option. Despite these challenges, scholars have made significant

     progress in understanding labor mobility’s positive effects on wages and
     welfare due to advanced statistical methods and longitudinal data. However,
     it is important to highlight that these positive effects on wages are stronger
     for early–career workers than for mid– and late–career workers (Lam, Ng,
     and Feldman 2012). Moreover, Davia (2005), who studied the effect of labor
     mobility on the wages of young workers across Europe, found that job mo-
     bility pays, even for involuntary movers. The effects also depend on the job
     and sector. In a study conducted in the United States, Gius (2004) found that
     changing jobs within the same industry or occupation leads to an increase
     in a person’s earnings. However, a job change that involves both a shift in
     industry and occupation results in a reduction in earnings. Munich, Terrell,
     and Svejnar (2002), who studied the effect of labor mobility on wages in the
     Czech Republic, also found that the relationship between mobility and wages
     varies by type of separation and the movers’ sectors of destination. Finally,
     studies suggest that workers who move experience gains not only in earnings,
     but also in education and health (Abella 2013).

                          The labor market in Saudi Arabia has undergone a significant transfor-
                          mation in recent years. The labor force participation rate rose from 42 per-
                          cent in 2018 to 51 percent in 2024, primarily due to increased participation
                          among women. During the same period, unemployment rates decreased by
                          around 6 percentage points. This translated into an expansion of the private
                          sector workforce. The number of workers in the private sector increased from
                          approximately 9 million in 2018 to almost 11 million in 2024. The share of
                          expatriates among private–sector workers declined slightly after COVID–19
                          but remained stable over the 2018—2024 period at around 80 percent. The
                          representation of Saudi women increased, rising from 6.5 percent in 2018 to
                          8.7 percent in 2024, while the share of Saudi men decreased from 13.8 percent
                          to 12.4 percent. In absolute terms, both groups experienced growth, with
                          around 100,000 more Saudi men and 350,000 more Saudi women employed
                          in the private sector in 2024 compared to 2018 (see figure 3.1).

     Overview of the Labor ­Reform ­Initiative




     The LRI also introduced important changes regarding exit and re–entry, as
     well as final exit visas for expatriate workers. Under this reform, expatriates
     can independently apply for exit and re–entry visas through the Absher portal
     during the validity of their employment contract, allowing them the flexibility
     to leave and return to Saudi Arabia without relying on employer approval.
     Additionally, the LRI enables expatriate workers to submit an electronic ap-
     plication for final exit visas through Absher, granting them the freedom to
     exit the country at the end of their contract. However, both services require
     workers to meet certain conditions to qualify for these options.5

     The process for transition to a new employer has also been significantly
     enhanced under the LRI. Previously, transferring employment required the
     new employer to initiate the process in person at labor offices. However, under
     the LRI, the transfer request is now initiated through a platform called Qiwa.
     Moreover, the current employer now only receives a notification and no longer
     has the authority to reject or object to the request. Before the implementation
     of the LRI, the transfer process was contingent on the original employer’s
     explicit approval, which had to be submitted with the request. Labor office
     decisions were based primarily on this approval. Now, the process places
     greater emphasis on worker autonomy. The employee accepts the offer from
     the new employer and begins the notice period, unless waived by the current
     employer. Once the notice period is complete, the new employer finalizes the
     residency transfer via the Absher platform, enabling the employee to start
     their new job.




     5. To obtain the automatic exit and re–entry visa service, the worker must: be an expatriate who is working under the Saudi labor
     system; have a valid residency visa; have a duly attested employment contract; pay the requisite fees; not have any outstanding fees
     or fines; and declare through the Absher portal that they fully understand the measures entailed by their failure to return before the
     leave period approved by their employer ends. To obtain the automatic exit visa service, the worker must meet the same criteria,
     except for a different declaration in the Absher portal: the worker must declare that they understand the measures entailed by their
     decision to exit the Kingdom before fulfilling their contract or applying through the platform (MHRSD 2020).

HOW DID THE LABOR REFORM INITIATIVE AFFECT
EMPLOYERS AND EXPATRIATES?

       As evidenced in the literature, higher labor mobility in Saudi Arabia is
       associated with higher wages. Administrative data reveal that an increasing
       share of expatriates has experienced wage increases upon job transitions over
       the past few years. The percentage more than doubled between January 2018
       and 2022 (figure 5.13). By the end of 2022, there was a noticeable reduction in
       the share of workers experiencing no wage adjustment upon job transition,
       which may be linked to electronic contracts being made mandatory through
       Qiwa in May 2022. When focusing solely on workers who received wage raises,
       those who transitioned generally gained more than those who remained with
       the same employer. The difference was most pronounced for medium–high–
       skilled workers (see figure 5.14), but they are only a small share of those who
       received wage raises (figure 5.15).

       Employer interviews and employee and employer surveys also report a pos-
       itive effect of the LRI on wages, indicating that workers gained enhanced
       negotiating power with the policy. The interviews reveal that the increased
       negotiating power of expatriate workers led to heightened market competition,
       which, in turn, has motivated expatriate talent to remain in Saudi Arabia and
       seek improved opportunities. As one employer aptly stated:

         “We strive to retain them by addressing their concerns and,
          on occasion, offering salary increases. For instance, last
          month, I successfully retained an employee by implementing
          a salary adjustment.”

       A survey of origin firms (firms experiencing a workforce outflow) and
       destination firms (firms experiencing a workforce inflow) corroborates
       these interviews. Notably, 45 percent of destination firms reported offering
       higher salaries, while 91 percent of origin firms actively sought to retain their
       workers by providing pay raises. An online survey of over 49,000 expatriate
       workers revealed that 52 percent transferred for favorable opportunities, such
       as higher pay or a promotion, while 32 percent transferred due to negative
       experiences, like misalignment with their employer or dissatisfaction with
       their role.

     The LRI policy was perceived to offer numerous significant advantages of
     recruiting expatriates. Interviews and surveys conducted with firms revealed
     a diverse array of reasons for hiring expatriate workers through the mobility
     policy rather than recruiting from overseas. Key advantages included better
     skills among expatriates with Saudi labor market experience, a faster and
     more streamlined process, and lower overall costs for recruitment. As one
     employer noted:

        “ The main one being the ease of transferring labor
         internally within the Kingdom, saving time, and the effort
         that HR might exert in recruiting talents from within
         and outside the Kingdom.”

     The enhanced mobility of the workforce has also provided employers with a
     greater ability to evaluate talent before hiring, as prospective employees are
     conveniently located within the country. In a 2025 3LMS study,7 65 percent of
     employers reported that the LRI had a positive impact on their organization,
     while only 2 percent perceived it as having a negative effect. Furthermore, 58

       ­INITIATIVE
     Descriptive statistics are essential for understanding broad patterns and
     differences among groups. They help highlight trends and provide context
     for the data. However, while useful, these summaries alone cannot determine
     whether the LRI directly caused the observed changes. For example, differ-
     ences in wages or job transitions may result from other factors unrelated to
     the reform.

     To estimate the specific impact of the LRI, the paper employs a differ-
     ence–in–differences (DiD) approach, comparing changes in labor mobility
     between groups affected by the reform and those that were not. The LRI,
     implemented in March 2021, applied only to expatriates who had been residing
     in Saudi Arabia for at least one year, thereby excluding newer expatriates.
     This eligibility rule allows for a clear identification strategy, defining the
     treatment group as expatriates with more than one year of tenure in Saudi
     Arabia and the control group as expatriates with less than one year of resi-
     dence. The paper measures job–to–job transitions over the 2018—2024 period,
     which covers three pre-reform years (2018—2020) and four post-reform years
     (2021—2024). The DiD estimator captures how these outcomes changed for
     long–term expatriates (treatment group) relative to short–term expatriates
     (control group) after the reform’s introduction. By contrasting these trends
     before and after March 2021, the paper isolates the causal effect of the LRI
     on expatriate labor mobility, controling for broader labor market dynamics
     and year–specific shocks (box 6.1). Since randomized controlled trials are
     not feasible in this setting, the LRI provides a valuable natural experiment
     enabling credible causal inference.

        CONCLUSION

     With the LRI, the Saudi Arabian government has taken a significant step
     toward a flexible labor market. The results of this study show that remov-
     ing the obstacles for expatriate mobility has increased workers’ job–to–job
     transitions and improved their earning prospects. As a result of the policy,
     firms are changing their planning practices and improving working condi-
     tions to retain employees. All of this was also recognized by the international
     community and led to the improvement of the Kingdom’s ranking in labor
     market indices. Since 2020, Saudi Arabia has improved its overall Internation-
     al Institute for Management Development (IMD) Competitiveness ranking
     from 24th to 16th place (IMD 2024). Moreover, its Labor Rights Index score
     increased from 58 to 62.5.9 In 2024, Saudi Arabia also ranked second in the
     InterNations Working Abroad Index.10

     As a result of the heightened competition for workers, expected changes
     will not only benefit employees but also strengthen the broader econo-
     my. Higher wages and improved benefits to retain workers will boost their
     purchasing power, driving greater consumer demand and fueling economic
     growth. Enhanced investments in training and skill development could lead
     to a more capable and innovative workforce, raising productivity across
     sectors. The potential adoption of flexible work arrangements and emphasis
     on well–being might create a healthier, more satisfied labor force, reducing
     turnover and fostering stability. Over time, these adjustments could promote
     a more dynamic labor market in which economic efficiency and innovation
     thrive. If these trends continue, the economy could become more equitable,
     resilient, and better equipped to sustain long–term growth in an increasingly
     competitive global landscape.

     The LRI has also received widespread international recognition, contrib-
     uting significantly to Saudi Arabia’s improved global competitiveness.
     Since its introduction, the Kingdom has achieved notable progress across
     several key indicators. Saudi Arabia’s ranking in the Labor Regulation Index
     has risen 23 places, while the Skilled Labor Index ranking has improved by 22
     places. Additionally, the Labor Force Productivity Index ranking has climbed
     18 places, and the Immigration Laws Index advanced by 12 places. These
     advancements highlight the success of the LRI in fostering a more dynamic
     and competitive labor market.

     Nonetheless, some challenges persist. Smaller firms are disproportionately
     affected by the LRI and may thus experience more adjustment burdens than
     larger firms, as well as concerns about retaining their labor force. To remain
     competitive, these firms can adopt labor–saving technologies and automation,
     thereby reducing their dependence on manual labor for repetitive, low–skilled
     tasks. In addition, investing in workforce upskilling fosters resilience and ad-
     aptability. For example, artificial intelligence—powered platforms can deliver
     personalized training modules to new hires, reducing the time and cost of
     onboarding. Furthermore, such technologies not only enhance operational
     efficiency but also empower employees with relevant skills, contributing to
     long–term organizational stability. Further improvements in recruitment
     support and access to talent may also gradually ease hiring pressures.

     While the LRI has successfully enhanced labor mobility, some employers
     have raised concerns about its potential negative impacts. Many are frus-
     trated by the unexpected nature of transfers, often discovering an employee
     has left only when they are urgently needed for a project. Others argue that
     the ease of transfer undermines contractual agreements, making it difficult
     to retain skilled workers. As a result, companies are hesitant to invest in
     employee training, fearing that workers may transfer to other companies
     after just a year or two. Dissatisfaction is most common among businesses
     with weaker human resources practices and less attractive environments;
     however, low–skill, labor–intensive sectors are particularly affected due to
     high transfer volumes. Policies that help microfirms hire needed skills can
     turn this dissatisfaction into increased value added and profitability. These
     policies reduce hiring costs, mitigate employment risk, improve matching
     efficiency, and alleviate administrative and financial constraints. In terms of
     training, the government began to incentivize firms to train their employees.
     A policy was implemented in 2023 that required firms with more than 50
     employees to report their annual training activities through Qiwa (that is,
     training hours, number of trainees, and training budgets). Providing firms
     with information is important, as many undertrain because they are unsure
     about the returns on their investment.

     Conclusion




     Finally, evidence suggests that low–skilled expatriates have experienced
     the greatest impact from the LRI compared to other groups. The policy has
     primarily reshaped employment dynamics in labor–intensive sectors, with
     relatively less influence on middle–skilled professions. Further exploration is
     needed to fully understand the reasons behind these findings, which could be
     due to limited formal job intermediation services for these skills. Interviews
     suggest thus far that workers are relying on personal networks or directly
     applying to firms when seeking new opportunities: “Laborers often come to
     the industrial area, especially industrial employees, they come and apply to every
     factory.” To facilitate intermediation, skills credentialling systems can be put
     in place. A skills passport can allow job seekers to indicate which skills they
     have acquired and which qualifications they have obtained to better stream-
     line job applications.
