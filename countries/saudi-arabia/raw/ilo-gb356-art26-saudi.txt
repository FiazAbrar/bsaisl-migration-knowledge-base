Fifteenth item on the agenda

Complaint submitted under article 26 of the ILO
Constitution alleging non-observance by Saudi
Arabia of Conventions Nos 29, 95 and 111
     1.       At its 355th Session (November 2025), the Governing Body examined a report of the Officers
              on a complaint concerning non-observance by the Government of Saudi Arabia of the Forced
              Labour Convention, 1930 (No. 29), the Protection of Wages Convention, 1949 (No. 95), the
              Discrimination (Employment and Occupation) Convention, 1958 (No. 111), the Migrant Workers
              (Supplementary Provisions) Convention, 1975 (No. 143), and the Occupational Safety and
              Health Convention, 1981 (No. 155), submitted under article 26 of the ILO Constitution by
              several Workers’ delegates to the 113th Session (2025) of the International Labour
              Conference. 1
     2.       Taking into account the conditions established in article 26 of the ILO Constitution, the
              Governing Body: (a) decided that the complaint was receivable in relation to Conventions
              Nos 29, 95 and 111, and not receivable in relation to Conventions Nos 143 and 155;
              (b) requested the Director-General to transmit the complaint to the Government of Saudi
              Arabia, inviting it to communicate its observations on the complaint by 30 January 2026;
              (c) acknowledging the importance of dialogue between the parties, encouraged them to
              maintain their constructive engagement and requested the Director-General to provide any
              support needed to facilitate that process and to report to the Governing Body at its next
              session in March–April 2026; (d) decided to place the item on the agenda of the 356th Session
              (March–April 2026) of the Governing Body. 2

2.     THE KINGDOM OF SAUDI ARABIA HAS A STRONG LEGAL FRAMEWORK FOR
       PROTECTING MIGRANT WORKERS
     11.   An international organisation should analyse a complaint based on facts and evidence, not
           innuendo that is not grounded in proof. In this regard, the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia already
           has strong, codified protections for workers (citizens and foreign residents alike) under the
           Labour Law. 20 The Labour Law prohibits forced labour and unlawful wage withholding and
           requires respectful treatment; 21 sets binding limits on working time and rest, including
           daily/weekly caps and a minimum break (no less than half an hour) after five consecutive
           hours; 22 and imposes employer duties for occupational safety and health (“OSH”). 23 This
           framework is complemented by the Anti-Harassment Law 24, which obliges government and
           private entities to adopt workplace measures to prevent and combat harassment, 25 and by the
           Anti-Trafficking in Persons Law 26, which mandates assistance and protection for victims –
           including medical care, shelter, and where necessary, permission for non-Saudi victims to
           remain or work during proceedings. 27 Enforcement mechanisms include the “Wage Protection
           Program” that electronically monitors on-time, full wage payments for Saudis and non-
           Saudis, 28 the pre-litigation amicable settlement process administered by the Ministry of
           Human Resources and Social Development (the “MHRSD”), 29 and proceedings before
           specialised Labour Courts with jurisdiction over wage, termination and other employment
           disputes, 30 with e-litigation via the Najiz portal enabling electronic filing, exchange of
           memoranda, viewing all case filings, digital sessions, delivery of judgements, and appeals. 31
           Additionally, labour inspection is carried out under the Labour Law Implementing Regulations
           for Labour Inspection 32, which sets clear inspection procedures, defines inspectors’ powers
           and employers’ obligations, and provides corrective measures and penalties – thereby
 GB.356/INS/15(Rev.2)                                                                                6




         strengthening enforcement and ensuring procedural fairness. These protections are further
         operationalised through official policies and guides, including the National Policy for the
         Elimination of Forced Labour, a government policy articulating the Kingdom’s commitment,
         governance arrangements and cross-agency coordination to eradicate forced labour; 33 the
         Guide to Services Provided for Expatriates, a consolidated reference that signposts expatriate
         workers to rights, key procedures and complaint channels; 34 the Controls to Improve the
         Contractual Relationship of Domestic Workers and their Equivalents, ministerial controls
         setting contractual standards, obligations and dispute-handling rules for domestic work
         relationships; 35 and the Guide to the Rights and Obligations of Domestic Workers in the
         Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, an official explanatory guide outlining rights, duties and recourse
         pathways for domestic workers. 36 In addition, employment contracts are documented
         electronically through the MHRSD/Qiwa ecosystem; employers create and document contracts
         online; employees can view, approve, reject or request amendments; and once both parties
         agree, the contract is certified by MHRSD, providing an authoritative, time-stamped record
         accessible to each party. 37 The Executive Regulations of the Labour Law and its Annexes
         mandate the use of a unified employment-contract model with mandatory clauses, ensuring
         completeness and transparency of terms. 38 Furthermore, the Contractual Relationship
         Improvement Initiative modernised key aspects of the employment relationship, most notably
         job-mobility and exit procedures, strengthening workers’ ability to exercise their rights. 39

   12.   Further, the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia recognises the right of workers, both Saudis and non-

         agencies to protect the rights of workers to form associations:
         a.   Rules of Formation of Labour Committees issued pursuant to Council of Ministers
              Resolution no. 12 dated 2 April 2001, together with the Implementing Regulation issued
              pursuant to Ministerial Resolution No. 1691 dated 10 April 2002. The main purpose of
              these committees is to represent workers, improve workplace conditions, and advocate
              for worker rights. 41 These rules marked a significant development in promoting social
              dialogue and collective labour representation in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia.
         b.   Law on Civil Associations and Foundations issued pursuant to Royal Decree No. R/8 dated
              01 December 2015, together with the Implementing Regulation issued pursuant to
              Ministerial Resolution No. 73739 dated 20 March 2016. The law provides a legal
              framework for the formation, activities, and management of non-profit organisations,
              promoting civil engagement and social welfare activities;
         c.   Employer Compliance Guide issued by the MHRSD, which consolidates employers’
              obligations under the Labour Law (covering internal regulations, discipline, payroll, OSH,
              and complaint handling) to support establishments in implementing compliant internal
              procedures consistent with the Labour Law and the rules governing workers’
              committees; 42 and
         d.   The Labour Affairs Agency (one of the agencies affiliated with the MHRSD), which confirms
              the Ministry’s mandate to oversee policies and systems that improve labour relations
              within establishments. 43

3.     THE KINGDOM OF SAUDI ARABIA’S LEGAL FRAMEWORK HAS ALREADY MADE
       SIGNIFICANT PRORESSS AND CONTINUES TO MAKE FURTHER PROGRESS
     13.    The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia understands that progress can always be made, even when the
            existing worker protections are robust and legally supported. The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia
            has, since 2015, undertaken an extensive reform of its labour laws – in close coordination with
            the ILO. For example, the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia has entered into a Programme of
            Cooperation with the ILO. 44 The ILO lauded the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia’s reforms, noting
            that:
                     [a]mong other achievements, the Saudi government has made good progress in recent years
                     on the ratification and implementation of international labour standards. The new phase of
                     cooperation will enable the Government to build and expand upon these achievements. 45

     14.    That reform programme continues. For example, the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia has
            implemented the following policies and legislation, each of which further strengthens worker
            protections in the Kingdom:
       1)    Labour Regulatory Framework
            a.   Labour Law issued pursuant to Royal Decree No. m/51 dated 27 September 2005, which
                 lays out the basic rights and responsibilities of workers and employers;
            b.   Executive Regulations of the Labour Law and its Annexes issued pursuant to Ministerial
                 Resolution No. 70273 dated 18 December 2018, which clarify the details of the Labour
                 Law, promote a safe workplace, protect the rights of all workers, and help advance and
                 modernize the Kingdom’s labour market; 46
            c.   Labour Law amendments issued pursuant to Royal Decree No. M/44 dated 12 August
                 2024 (came into effect on 19 February 2025); The amendments seek to improve the labour
                 market, enhance job stability, safeguard the rights of all parties in the employment
                 relationship. Among other, the amendments include expanding provisions on leave and
                 contracts; revising grievance procedures; imposing penalties for unlicensed recruitment
                 activities; and requiring employers to adopt training and qualification policies to upskill
                 employees. these changes followed an extensive study that involved benchmarking
                 against other countries’ labour laws and global best practices, with input from more than
                 1,300 participants via the Public Consultation Platform. They also included consultation
                 with private sector entities, government agencies, worker committees, and HR experts; 47
            d.   Table of Violations and Penalties issued pursuant to Ministerial Resolution No. 75913
                 dated 03 December 2023 and updated pursuant to Ministerial Resolution No. 44558 dated
                 06 October 2024, which updates the schedule of fines/penalties under the Labour Law
                 and its Implementing Regulations to improve market compliance; 48 The table also serves
                 as a regulatory tool to identify and classify violations that constitute potential indicators
                 of forced labour, enabling such cases to be addressed through a parallel assessment track
                 to verify suspected forced labour situations and trigger appropriate preventive or
                 protective measures, in addition to administrative enforcement.
       2)    Labour Inspection and Safety & Health
            e.   Executive Regulations for the Control and Organisation of Labour Inspection issued
                 pursuant to Ministerial Resolution No. 120279 dated 27 February 2025, which establishes
                 the framework for labour inspections by setting inspectors’ powers and duties, organizing
                 proactive and complaint-based inspections, detailing employers’ cooperation obligations
                 and inspection procedures, and replacing the 2007 inspection regulation; The framework

     17.    The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia expresses its surprise and regret regarding the aggressive, non-
            diplomatic tone of the Complaint, as well as the public remarks made by the Complainants

                     Workers are being treated as disposable in Saudi Arabia. They leave alive and return in

     18.    The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia considers such rhetoric to be a departure from the principles of

            supervisory mechanisms.

5.1.3. The Complaint is inadmissible because it is premature and does not take into account the
       Kingdom of Saudi Arabia’s ongoing reforms
   43.   The Complaint does not relate to ongoing breaches of the relevant ILO Conventions. It cannot
         as the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia is currently in the process of reforming its labour law system.
         The Complaint is therefore inadmissible, because it is extremely premature.
   44.   The Complaint procedure under Article 26 is triggered where an ILO Member State has failed
         to “secur[e] the effective observance” of the ILO conventions it has ratified. Moreover, a
         Commission of Inquiry under Article 26 is the highest-level investigative procedure, reserved
         for accusations concerning “persistent and serious violations” that a State has “repeatedly
         refused to address”. 105 The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia is currently in the process of securing
         “effective observance” of these conventions, as further discussed in Section 2 and Section 3
         above. It is thus premature for an investigation to take place in respect of the Kingdom of Saudi
         Arabia’s alleged non-compliance with the ILO conventions it has ratified.
   45.   As a matter of international law, a breach of an obligation cannot occur on the basis of what
         might happen or what might be. A breach arises upon an act committed by or on omission by
         the State. 106 This is implicit, for example, in the notion of “breach” in the Articles on the
         Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts (the “Articles on the Responsibility
         of States”). Articles 12 to 15 of the Articles on the Responsibility of States provide as follows:
                  Article 12.   Existence of a breach of an international obligation
                        There is a breach of an international obligation by a State when an act of that State is not
                  in conformity with what is required of it by that obligation, regardless of its origin or character
                  …
                  Article 14.   Extension in time of the breach of an international obligation
                       1. The breach of an international obligation by an act of a State not having a continuing
                  character occurs at the moment when the act is performed, even if its effects continue.
                       2. The breach of an international obligation by an act of a State having a continuing
                  character extends over the entire period during which the act continues and remains not in
                  conformity with the international obligation.
                       3. The breach of an international obligation requiring a State to prevent a given event
                  occurs when the event occurs and extends over the entire period during which the event
                  continues and remains not in conformity with that obligation.
                  Article 15.   Breach consisting of a composite act
                       1. The breach of an international obligation by a State through a series of actions or
                  omissions defined in aggregate as wrongful occurs when the action or omission occurs which,
                  taken with the other actions or omissions, is sufficient to constitute the wrongful act.
 GB.356/INS/15(Rev.2)                                                                                        17




                       2. In such a case, the breach extends over the entire period starting with the first of the
                  actions or omissions of the series and lasts for as long as these actions or omissions are
                  repeated and remain not in conformity with the international obligation. 107

   46.   Articles 12 and 14 (and Article 15 in relation to composite acts constituting a breach) impose a
         temporal condition on the existence of a breach: the breach exists only when an act is unlawful
         under international law and only continues to occur when the act and international law remain
         incongruent. That is, a breach does not exist when the behaviour of the State is or may be in
         conformity with international law.
   47.   Moreover, the procedure in Article 26 of the ILO Constitution should only be relied on where a
         Member State has made “persistent and serious” breaches of the convention(s) it has ratified.
         Article 26 states that a Complaint can be made where a Member State has failed to “secur[e]
         the effective observance” of one the conventions it has ratified. A Commission of Inquiry will
         then be established “when a member state is accused of committing persistent and serious
         violations and has repeatedly refused to address them”. 108 There are, for example, many
         instances of the ILO Governing Body deferring the decision on whether to appoint a
         Commission of Inquiry until after the State has had the opportunity to engage with the
         complaint by, for example, preparing legislation to address the issues raised in the complaint,
         receiving technical assistance from the ILO, and consulting with domestic labour
         organisations. 109
   48.   The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia is currently in the process of undertaking substantial reforms of

5.2. The whole Complaint is not legally receivable because it fails to meet the legal threshold

5.2.1. The Complaint is not receivable in full because it alleges violations of conventions the
       Kingdom of Saudi Arabia has not ratified and general international labour standards
   55.   The Governing Body has already recognised that the Complaint is not receivable in relation to
         two conventions mentioned in the Complaint that the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia has not ratified,
         namely Convention No. 143 and Convention No. 155. 116
   56.   For the avoidance of doubt, for the same reasons, the Complaint is not receivable also in
         relation to the other unratified conventions and any international labour standard that the
         Complaint mentions, 117 if the Complaint is purporting to initiate the procedure under Article
         26 of the ILO Constitution for allegations of non-observance of those instruments.
   57.   Complaints under Article 26 of the ILO Constitution are only receivable when they concern
         allegations under conventions that the relevant State has ratified. Article 26(1) of the ILO
         Constitution states:
                  Any of the Members shall have the right to file a complaint with the International Labour Office
                  if it is not satisfied that any other Member is securing the effective observance of any
 GB.356/INS/15(Rev.2)                                                                                  19




                  Convention which both have ratified in accordance with the foregoing articles. 118
                  (Emphasis added.)

   58.   The wording of the ILO Constitution is clear: a complaint under Article 26 of the ILO
         Constitution must concern a ratified convention and not either unratified ILO conventions or
         general labour standards.
   59.   In addition, ILO guidance states that one of the “receivability criteria” of Article 26 complaints
         is that “the member State against which the complaint is filed must have ratified the
         Convention that is the subject of the complaint”. 119
   60.   To the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia’s knowledge, the ILO has not to date deemed receivable a
         complaint under Article 26 of the ILO Constitution against a State which has not ratified a
         convention.
   61.   The ILO’s decisions show that it would not have competence over a complaint concerning an
         unratified convention. For example, the ILO Governing Body decided a complaint against Malta
         was not receivable in circumstances where it had not ratified one of the conventions in the
         complaint, although the Governing Body cited other grounds for why the complaint was not
         receivable in its entirety. 120 By way of another example, in a complaint procedure concerning
         Zimbabwe, an ILO Commission of Inquiry underlined that it “was competent only to examine
         Zimbabwe fulfilment of the obligations it undertook upon ratifying Conventions Nos 87 and
         98” and therefore other matters “would be outside its competence”. 121
   62.   Similarly, the ILO deems not receivable allegations against States in other supervisory
         procedures before the ILO concerning unratified conventions. 122 For example, the ILO did not
         set up a tripartite committee to examine allegations under Convention No. 155, 123 even
         though a complainant made allegations under Convention No. 155 against the Kingdom of
         Saudi Arabia in 2024.
   63.   It would be contrary to international law to allow procedures against the Kingdom of Saudi
         Arabia in relation to an unratified convention. This is textbook international law: a State is not
         bound to answer to a treaty that it has not ratified. 124 The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia is not
         bound to apply these conventions upon adoption by the ILO. It is only bound when it ratifies
         and registers its ratification with the Director-General of the ILO. 125
   64.   Opening a complaint procedure under Article 26 of the ILO Constitution concerning
         conventions unratified by ILO Member States would also be contrary to the ILO Constitution.
         As previously stated, the ILO Constitution expressly provides that the Complaint procedure
         under Article 26 only applies with respect to “any Convention which both have ratified in
         accordance with the foregoing articles”. 126 In addition, Article 5 of the ILO Constitution,
         concerning “obligations of members in respect of conventions”, clarifies that where convention
         is not ratified by a member State, “no further obligation shall rest upon the Member except
         that it shall report to the Director-General of the International Labour Office, at appropriate
         intervals as requested by the Governing Body, the position of its law and practice in regard to
         the matters dealt with in the Convention”. 127 Applying an Article 26 complaint procedure to
         unratified conventions would thus be incompatible with the ILO Constitution.
   65.   The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia has ratified many ILO conventions. However, allegations in the

5.2.2. The Complaint is not receivable in full as it raises allegations that do not allege a violation

   66.   The Complaint raises matters that are not accompanied by any assertion that the Kingdom of
         Saudi Arabia has failed to secure effective observance of any ILO convention at all. Those parts

   67.   A complaint procedure under Article 26(1) of the ILO Constitution is only validly initiated if it
         alleges violations of specific ILO conventions that a State has ratified. Article 26(1) requires that
         a complaint states that “it is not satisfied that any other Member is securing the effective

         one of the “receivability criteria [that] need to be met for the Governing Body to initiate its
         consideration of the complaint” is that:
                  the complaint must provide an indication that the complainant ‘is not satisfied’ that the
                  member State against which the complaint is filed is ‘securing the effective observance’ of a
                  Convention it has ratified. 129

   68.   The ILO Governing Body therefore deems complaints non-receivable when they do not allege
         a violation of a particular ILO convention in a Member State. In the complaint concerning
         Austria and other European Union Members States, the complainant alleged that European
         Union trade restrictions had negative consequences for the economy of Belarus. Although
         referring to two ILO conventions in the complaint, the ILO Governing Body noted that that
         complaint “does not allege that any of the 27 EU Member States has failed to secure the
         effective application of Conventions Nos 111 and 122 within its jurisdiction”. 130 The ILO
         Governing Body thus considered that the complaint “fail[ed] to state a valid cause of action
         under article 26(1)” of the ILO Constitution. 131 It concluded that “[a]s a consequence, the
         complaint does not appear to fulfil the requirements set out in article 26 of the ILO Constitution
         for receivability”. 132

   70.   None of the above-mentioned parts of the Complaint satisfy the legal threshold of indicating

6.1.     The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia is engaging with the ILO and the ITUC to address the
         allegations raised in the Complaint
       72.   As discussed above, the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia had already entered into a Programme of
             Cooperation with the ILO over the last few years before the Complaint was made
             (see Section 3). In December 2022, the programme entered its second phase 141 and it is
             currently in its third phase as of June 2025. This constitutes the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia
             securing the effective observance of the relevant ILO conventions and the ILO Governing Body
             should close the Complaint procedure.
       73.   In addition, separately, on the ILO’s request, after the filing of the Complaint , the Kingdom of
             Saudi Arabia has in good faith engaged in discussions with the ILO and the ITUC to resolve the
             matters raised by the Complaint. All parties have been engaged in facilitated and good faith
             dialogue with the aim of resolving fully, or providing a blueprint to resolve, all issues in the
             Complaint. Through this process, the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia is securing the effective
             observance of the relevant ILO conventions and the ILO Governing Body should close the
             Complaint procedure.
       74.   It is the practice of the ILO Governing Body to close Article 26 complaint procedures when
             either (i) the State engages with a complainant (such as by engaging in good faith negotiations
             concerning a complaint’s subject matter) or (ii) the ILO considers that progress has been made
             toward addressing matters raised in the complaint or are supervised by the ILO (such as
             through a technical cooperation programme with the ILO). This is even if the matters are not
             yet fully resolved. For example, the ILO Governing Body discontinued the complaint procedure
             concerning Nigeria after ILO supervisory bodies noted that “some substantial progress” was
             made by Nigeria following its cooperation with an ILO direct contact mission. 142 Notably, the
             ILO’s supervisory bodies considered that “there were discrepancies between the law and
             ratified Conventions which still had to be remedied” but the “matters considered ... to remain
             outstanding have now been referred to the Committee of Experts within the framework of its
             regular supervision of ratified Conventions”. 143

             where they are concerned that a State is not “securing the effective observance of any
             Convention” that State has ratified. 144 Where a Commission of Inquiry is appointed, its role is
             to draft a report on the complaint “containing such recommendations as it may think proper
             as to the steps which should be taken to meet the complaint”. 145 However, where a
             government, the complainant and the ILO are already engaged in good faith dialogue and are
 GB.356/INS/15(Rev.2)                                                                                 22




         taking steps to resolve the matter, continuation of the procedure serves no useful purpose.

         concerning Qatar, Guatemala and Bahrain. 146 It closed Article 26 procedures concerning those
         States precisely because engagement with the complainants and technical cooperation
         between the State and the ILO made a Commission of Inquiry unnecessary.
   76.   The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia is already engaging with the ITUC and the ILO. Separately, it is

6.2. The allegations in the Complaint are not sufficiently evidenced or proved
   78.   The Complaint does not establish that the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia failed to comply with any

         procedure, reserved for accusations concerning “persistent and serious violations” that a State
         has “repeatedly refused to address”. 147 It cannot be used as a fishing expedition into

   79.   The allegations made in the Complaint do not meet the required standard to hold the Kingdom

         applied by international judicial or quasi-judicial bodies, is generally that of preponderance of
         evidence or reasonable grounds to believe. 150 The ILO Governing Body must therefore be

         failed to “secur[e] the effective observance of” any of the Conventions that it has ratified. 151

6.3.1. Alleged violation of Convention No. 29: the Forced Labour Convention
   119. The Complaint alleges that the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia has failed to comply with Convention
        No. 29. As explained above, these allegations are factually unsubstantiated. They also do not
        rise to a violation of Convention No. 29.

   120. It is unclear precisely which allegations in the Complaint are meant to establish a breach of

   121. The substantive obligations of States under Convention No. 29 are established in Article 1 and
        Article 25. Under Article 1, States are required to “suppress the use of forced or compulsory
        labour in all its forms within the shortest possible period.” Article 25 further requires that the
        “illegal exaction of forced or compulsory labour shall be punishable as a penal offence, and it
        shall be an obligation on any Member ratifying this Convention to ensure that the penalties
        imposed by law are really adequate and are strictly enforced.”
   122. The committee set up to examine the representation alleging non-observance by Qatar of
        Convention No. 29 explained the obligations on States arising from Article 1 as follows:
                  [b]y ratifying the Convention, States are under the obligation to adopt measures, in law and
                  practice, with a view to ensuring that no form of forced labour is tolerated on their territory. To
                  this end, it is important for the Government to adopt appropriate legislation and to establish
                  legal safeguards to prevent any de facto coercion to perform work, and to ensure that, when
                  forced labour is exacted, adequate penalties are imposed on perpetrators. 242

   123. It further explained that a committee responsible for considering allegations under Article 1
        must:
                  evaluate the manner in which the Government is discharging its duty, pursuant to Article 1 of
                  the Convention, “to suppress the use of forced or compulsory labour in all its forms”. To this
                  end, the committee will review the legal framework regulating the situation of migrant
                  workers, in so far as it relates to their protection from forced labour, as well as the manner in
                  which this framework is applied in practice. 243

   124. Thus, it is a State’s legal framework and the measures adopted by the State for regulating the

        comply with its obligations under Article 1 and Article 25. The Complaint, however, does not
        engage with, let alone demonstrate, that the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia’s legislative framework
        is non-compliant with Convention No. 29. While the Complaint makes some reference to the
        Kafala system, 244 it does not show how the Kafala system, now replaced by an employment
        contract system, fails to comply with Convention No. 29. In fact, as detailed legislative
        framework in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia more generally is fully compliant with Convention
        No. 29. Moreover, the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia continues to work towards the reform of its
        legislative framework to ensure that it is fully aligned with the Convention No. 29 or any of the
        other ILO conventions referenced in the Complaint. As explained below, the Kingdom of Saudi
        Arabia has in place a strong legal framework and enforcement measures to prevent deception

6.3.2. Alleged violation of Convention No. 95: the Protection of Wages Convention
   139. The allegations in the Complaint also do not give rise to a violation of Convention No. 95
        concerning the protection of wages.
   140. The Complaint sets out some unparticularised instances where groups or individuals in the
        Kingdom of Saudi Arabia have purportedly experienced “wage theft” or “wage denial”. 266 As
        demonstrated above in Section 6.2.2 above, these allegations are highly speculative and
 GB.356/INS/15(Rev.2)                                                                                        35




        completely unsupported by evidence. These allegations also do not rise to a violation of
        Convention No. 95.
   141. Obligations under Convention No. 95 with respect to “wage theft” or “wage delay” 267 are set
        out in Article 12. Article 12 specifies that:
                  1. Wages shall be paid regularly. Except where other appropriate arrangements exist which
                  ensure the payment of wages at regular intervals, the intervals for the payment of wages shall
                  be prescribed by national laws or regulations or fixed by collective agreement or arbitration
                  award.
                  2. Upon the termination of a contract of employment, a final settlement of all wages due shall
                  be effected in accordance with national laws or regulations, collective agreement or arbitration
                  award or, in the absence of any applicable law, regulation, agreement or award, within a
                  reasonable period of time having regard to the terms of the contract.

   142. As established by an ILO committee of experts, a State’s obligations under Article 12 are
        comprised of three essential elements: (i) efficient control; (ii) appropriate sanctions; and
        (iii) the means to redress the injury caused (including not only the full payment of the amounts
        due, but also fair compensation for the losses incurred by the delayed payment). 268
   143. The factual allegations in the Complaint concerning some unparticularised instances 269 where
        certain groups 270 or individuals 271 purportedly had their wages delayed or denied while
        working in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, even if true, quod non, do not alone give rise to non-
        compliance with Article 12. They do not show that the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia failed to
        establish “efficient control”, “appropriate sanctions” or “means of redress” for wage delay or
        denial from employers. 272 In fact, the Complaint does not at all address the measures that are
        in place in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia to prevent wage theft and denial.
   144. The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia has in place and is effectively implementing a robust legal
        framework which ensures compliance with Convention No. 111. This is evidenced by:
        a.   in 2020, the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia introduced a wage insurance policy, ensuring that
             the Government intervenes by allocating state funds to cover unpaid wages for up to
             six months, along with a return ticket to the worker's home country. This applies in cases
             where employers are unable to meet their obligations and have delayed more than 80%
             of workers’ wages. 273 In 2024, the MHRSD issued binding guidelines that operationalise
             this scheme. 274 Furthermore, in accordance with Article 19 of the Labour Law, wages
             owed to workers – or their heirs – are classified as first-rate privileged debts in bankruptcy
             situations. This means that, in the event of an employer’s bankruptcy, owed wages are
             prioritised for payment over other debts, including judicial and bankruptcy-related
             expenses. Specifically, one month’s wages are to be paid on an expedited basis before any
             other expenses; 275 in addition, under the Preventive Justice Initiative jointly advanced by
             the Ministry of Justice and the MHRSD, a documented employment contract (including the
             wage clause) may be treated as an enforceable instrument, allowing direct recourse to
             enforcement procedures without separate litigation where wage obligations are not
             honoured; 276
        b.   the MHRSD has made significant efforts to ensure the applicability and enforcement of
             wage protection laws in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. The following data highlights the
             Kingdom of Saudi Arabia’s commitment to safeguarding workers’ rights through the
             ”Wage Protection Program”:
             i.    88% of companies in the Kingdom are fully compliant with the Wage Protection
                   Program; 277
 GB.356/INS/15(Rev.2)                                                                                         36




             ii.     91% of establishments upload their payroll files within 30 days of the due date (after
                     the upload window was shortened to tighten enforcement);
             iii.    in the first half of 2024, inspections recorded 59,891 violations for failing to submit

        c.   the program safeguarded wages of more than 8.5 million employees in 2024. 279 Between

6.3.3. Alleged violation of Convention No. 111: the Discrimination (Employment and Occupation)
       Convention
   146. The allegations in the Complaint do not rise to a violation of the Convention No. 111.
   147. The allegations made in the Complaint regarding discrimination are sparse. The entire section
        on “racial discrimination and marginalisation” in the Complaint is as follows:
                    The racial discrimination experienced by African migrant workers in Saudi Arabia violates
                    Convention No. 111, which stipulates that all workers be treated fairly and without
                    discrimination based on race, colour, or national origin. The differential treatment of African
                    workers in terms of salaries, working conditions and access to fundamental rights not only
                    violates the concept of equal opportunity but also sustains a system in which African workers
                    are considered inferior and disposable.

   148. The Complaint then notes generally that migrant workers “face discrimination not only on
        account of their status as migrant workers but also because of their race and economic
        background”. 282 As outlined above, these sweeping allegations are speculative and
        unsupported by evidence. They also do not rise to a violation of Convention No. 111.
   149. The substantive obligations of States under Convention No. 111 are established in Articles 2
        and 3 of the Convention. Article 2 requires the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia to “declare and pursue
        a national policy designed to promote, by methods appropriate to national conditions and
        practice, equality of opportunity and treatment in respect of employment and occupation, with
        a view to eliminating any discrimination in respect thereof.” 283 Article 3 requires the Kingdom
        of Saudi Arabia to undertake “by methods appropriate to national conditions and practice” to:
        (a) seek the co-operation of employers and workers’ organisations and other appropriate
        bodies in promoting the acceptance and observance of the policy; (b) “enact such legislation
        and to promote such educational programmes … to secure the acceptance and observance of
        the policy”; (c) “repeal any statutory provisions and modify any administrative instructions or
        practices which are inconsistent with the policy”; (d) “pursue the policy … under the direct
        control of a national authority”; (e) “ensure observance of the policy in the activities of
        vocational guidance, vocational training and placement services under the direction of a
 GB.356/INS/15(Rev.2)                                                                                37




        national authority”; (f) “indicate in its annual reports on the application of the Convention the
        action taken in pursuance of the policy and the results secured by such action.” 284
   150. The Complaint does not establish a breach of either Article 2 or Article 3. The allegation that
        African workers in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia experience “discrimination” is wholly
        unsubstantiated, as explained in Section 6.2.3 above. It also does not itself establish a breach
        of the substantive obligations in Convention No. 111. The fact that “African migrant workers”
        in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia “experience” racial or other forms of discrimination, even if
        proven, quod non, does not alone give rise to non-compliance with Convention No. 111. The
        Kingdom of Saudi Arabia is not required to guarantee that no one working in the Kingdom of
        Saudi Arabia experiences discrimination. It is required to have in place a “national policy” and
        take other specific measures “with a view to eliminating discrimination [in respect of
        employment and occupation].” 285 The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia has, indeed, upheld these
        obligations.
   151. The Complaint does not at all engage with the “national policy” that is in place in the Kingdom
        of Saudi Arabia to promote ... equality of opportunity and treatment in respect of employment
        and occupation” and eliminate “any discrimination in respect thereof.” 286 Indeed, the Kingdom
        of Saudi Arabia has adopted significant reforms and has in place a robust legal framework to
        ensure compliance with Convention No. 111. Discrimination is explicitly prohibited under
        Article 8 and 26 of the Basic Law of Governance and Article 61 of the Labour Law in the
        Kingdom. 287 Since 1994, various Council of Ministers resolutions have reinforced the right to
        equal wages for male and female workers performing the same work under equivalent
        conditions. 288 The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia has also introduced a national policy aimed at
        promoting equality of opportunity and treatment in employment and occupation. 289 The policy
        will be implemented in phases, with a comprehensive evaluation planned after five years to
        assess its effectiveness and make necessary amendments. The primary goal of this policy is to
        eradicate discrimination by strengthening labour regulations and enforcing measures against
        discriminatory practices.
   152. To ensure the effective implementation and compliance with this policy, the Kingdom of Saudi
        Arabia has also put in place mechanisms to assess the impact of regulations and amendments,

     tribunals, “addresses the question of whether, in light of all the relevant circumstances, that court or tribunal
     should entertain the case” (Shabtai Rosenne, “International Courts and Tribunals, Jurisdiction and
     Admissibility of Inter-State Applications”, Max Planck Encyclopedias of International Law, March 2006,
