CMS POLICY BRIEF 11 — Future of Bangladesh–Malaysia Labour Migration: Policy Priorities and Strategic Opportunities — August 2026
Center for Migration Studies (CMS), South Asian Institute of Policy and Governance (SIPG), North South University

### Existing Syndicates and High Migration Costs

The migration cost for Bangladeshi workers in Malaysia is significantly affected by
persistent syndicates in the recruitment system, resulting in limited transparency and
accountability within agencies. It was reported that, between 2022 and 2024, around
450,000 to 476,672 Bangladeshi workers entered Malaysia through 101 recruiting
agencies. However, this journey resulted in high migration costs, irregularities, and
exploitation of migrant workers. Hence, without solving these issues, the reopening of
the Malaysian labour market could further exacerbate the entire situation. (The Daily
Star, 2026)

Earlier, the Bangladeshi government fixed recruitment costs for prospective migrant
workers willing to work in Malaysia. Although the official costs were relatively low, set
by BMET and both the governments of Bangladesh and Malaysia, many workers were
reportedly forced to pay higher amounts, around Tk. 300,000 to Tk. 400,000, to secure
employment. Usually, these migrants pay this huge amount through loans, escalating
debt dependency and put the workers in vulnerable positions to forced labour, wage
theft, employer abuse, and acceptance of exploitative working conditions (Khan &
Hasan, 2026). Therefore, without prioritising workers' benefits through strict monitoring
of employers and agencies, reopening the Malaysian labour market to Bangladesh would
benefit recruitment agencies more than migrant workers.

### Weak Institutional and Consular Support

One of the main issues Bangladeshi workers working in Malaysia face is the sudden
change of their legal status. While working, workers often face sudden job loss, job
switching, termination, and manipulation of work, contracts, etc. Hence, it can be said
that workers' legal status is often dependent on employers, leaving them at risk of
exploitation. Unfortunately, there are no designated and dedicated helplines for the
struggling migrant workers in Malaysia. At the same time, some tend to avoid legal
channels to avoid detention, deportation and other kinds of harassment.

The Bangladesh High Commission in Malaysia aims to assist Bangladeshi migrants in
Malaysia for passport renewal, legal and documentation support, emergency and
employer-related services. However, these essential services are reported to be
inaccessible and slow.

## Policy Recommendations (extract)

Streamline the recruiting agencies by keeping records of their performance to uproot
the syndicate in the recruitment process. Employ strict punishments and penalties for
malpractice by recruiting agencies.

Develop a system to verify jobs before working for Malaysian employers by tracking
employers' details, workplace addresses, medical insurance, accommodation, and other
relevant information.

Implement a strict monitoring and evaluation mechanism for recruiting agencies and
create a transparent, well-designed ranking system based on their performance.
