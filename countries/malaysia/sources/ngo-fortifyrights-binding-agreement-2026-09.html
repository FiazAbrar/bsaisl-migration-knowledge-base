Malaysia/Bangladesh: Establish Binding Labor Agreement to Protect Migrant Workers

Malaysia, Bangladesh
September 30, 2026

Migrant workers in Malaysia face abuses, including excessive recruitment fees, wage theft, and poor working conditions

(KUALA LUMPUR, September 30, 2026)—Bangladesh and Malaysia should establish a binding bilateral labor agreement governing migrant-worker recruitment that provides meaningful and enforceable protections for workers, Migrant Welfare Network (MWN) and Fortify Rights said today.

Previous bilateral memorandums of understanding (MOUs) have included safeguards for Bangladeshi migrant workers, yet workers continue to report excessive recruitment fees, debt bondage, wage theft, passport confiscation, unsafe working conditions, and other abuses. Unlike a non-binding MOU, a bilateral labor agreement should establish binding obligations between the two governments and provide a stronger basis for ensuring that worker protections are implemented and enforced.

Any bilateral labor agreement must include safeguards against excessive recruitment fees, debt bondage, wage theft, passport confiscation, unsafe working conditions, and other abuses.

“Migrant workers make an enormous contribution to Bangladesh’s economy, sending billions of dollars home to their families every year, but too many are still exposed to exploitation and abuse,” said Abdullah al Mamun, President of Migrant Welfare Network. “Recruitment should resume only if the abuses workers face in the past are not repeated.”

“Before restarting formal migrant flows, Malaysia and Bangladesh must first put effective protections in place to safeguard migrant workers’ rights,” said John Quinley, Director at Fortify Rights. “The Malaysian and Bangladeshi governments must ensure that workers have access to effective remedies when rights are violated.”

Malaysia stopped accepting new migrant workers from multiple countries in 2024. However, after the Bangladesh Nationalist Party took office in February this year, senior Bangladeshi officials have made concerted efforts to resume the recruitment of Bangladeshi workers, making at least three high-level visits to Malaysia, including Prime Minister Tarique Rahman’s official visit in June.

Previously, in December 2021, Bangladesh and Malaysia signed an MOU governing the employment of Bangladeshi migrant workers in Malaysia, replacing earlier MOUs signed in 2003, 2012 and 2016 which had been repeatedly suspended over allegations of labor abuse and corruption. The 2021 agreement reportedly included protections requiring employers to cover airfare, immigration-related expenses, housing, and health and medical insurance; to provide workers with a weekly day of rest and overtime pay for work exceeding eight hours per day; and to provide compensation for workplace injuries. The agreement also provided for annual leave under Malaysian law, as well as compensation and other benefits in the event of a permanent workplace injury or death.

On September 16, 2026, Bangladesh’s Expatriates’ Welfare and Overseas Employment Secretary Md Mokhtar Ahmed said the government was preparing to reopen Malaysia’s labor market to Bangladeshi workers through a transparent recruitment process. He said several ministerial-level meetings between Bangladesh and Malaysia had already taken place and that authorities were developing a mechanism for sending workers once recruitment resumes.

In July 2026, MWN and Fortify Rights interviewed 18 migrant workers from Bangladesh who previously worked in Malaysia and experienced abuses, including rampant wage theft, unsafe working conditions, and excessive fees paid to recruitment agencies. The workers are from the districts of Jamalpur, Feni, Noakhali, Cumilla, and Cox’s Bazar in Bangladesh.

On September 24, 2026, MWN and Fortify Rights sent letters to the governments of Bangladesh and Malaysia summarizing our research findings, making specificrecommendations to safeguard the rights of Bangladeshi migrant workers and to upgrade the previous MOU to a legally binding bilateral labor agreement.

According to the Malaysian Ministry of Home Affairs, more than 800,000 Bangladeshis currently hold active work permits in Malaysia, positioning Bangladesh as the largest source of migrant labor in the country. Migrant workers in Malaysia are routinely employed in labor-intensive and hazardous sectors, including construction, manufacturing, and plantation work.

A 23-year-old migrant worker from Noakhali District returned to Bangladesh after suffering a severe spinal injury while working for a construction firm. Although he had no prior work experience, the company failed to provide formal training. “The other workers taught us,” he said. “[T]he work was very dangerous.” 

After repeatedly lifting heavy pipes, “I injured my spine … I am still not fully recovered,” he said. His medical record states that “pain radiates” down his leg and he is “unable to lift heavy weight.” Copies of his medical records are on file with MWN and Fortify Rights.

Although he sought medical treatment, he said the company provided no financial support, paid none of his medical expenses, and stopped paying his full wages.

Furthermore, the firm still owes him 16,000 ringgit [approximately US$4,000] in unpaid wages, after his monthly pay was cut to 500 ringgit for several months. “The company gave me no compensation [for my injuries]. They did not even pay my salary.” To secure the job, he paid 550,000 taka [approximately US$4,456] to a recruitment agency, funded through a bank loan and money borrowed from relatives. 

The recruitment process and the excessive and illegal fees charged can leave workers in substantial debt. Our research found that Bangladeshi recruitment agencies routinely charge workers for airfares and visa fees, costs which, under the previous MOU, should be covered by the Malaysian employer. This debt increases workers’ dependence on brokers and their employers, making it harder for them to challenge abuses.

Another Bangladeshi former migrant worker, 22, said he paid a broker from a recruitment agency 570,000 taka (approximately US$4,600)—more than seven times the Bangladeshi government-set official recruitment fee—to secure employment in Malaysia in 2023. The broker explained that the amount covers his visa, airfare, and medical expenses, although the MOU between Bangladesh and Malaysia specifies that such fees should be paid by the employer.

Before traveling to Malaysia, the recruitment agency made him and other migrant workers record a video at its Dhaka premises, falsely stating that the workers paid 79,000 taka (US$640)—the official recruitment fee set by the government—to go to Malaysia. The man said, “I didn’t want to lie, but they told me, ‘You need to tell them, or you can’t get on the flight.’” Fearing that outcome, he gave in.

After he arrived in Malaysia, his employer confiscated his passport, a common practice many migrant workers in the country face. The company also failed to provide personal protective equipment: “We didn’t have helmets or masks. There were chemicals [we worked with].”

Soon after, the firm stopped paying wages on time, prompting a group of workers to stage a protest. The company retaliated. “I was leading the strike, and they canceled my visa after I made a complaint,” he said. “I joined the strikes and helped lead the protest because the company was not treating us well from the beginning.” He returned to Bangladesh in March 2026.

Migrant workers in Malaysia also routinely face excessive working hours, unlawful wage deductions, threats, and violent abuses from employers.

A 24-year-old former migrant worker from Jamalpur District arrived in Malaysia in 2023 to work for a waste management company. He returned to Bangladesh in February 2026. He regularly worked from 8 a.m. into the evenings, seven days a week, without overtime pay, holidays, or leave.

Furthermore, the company deducted 100 ringgit (approximately US$25) from his salary each month for accommodation and required him to pay 500 ringgit (US$125) for uniforms and safety equipment, although the MOU specifies that the employer is responsible for these charges. When the equipment wore out, his supervisor directed workers to pay for replacements themselves.

When workers demanded overtime pay, as mandated by Malaysian law, Malaysian supervisors responded with threats and physical violence, he said. “Once, we asked for overtime pay. They brought me to a room, and they pushed my face and slapped me,” he said. “They warned that if we continue like this, they will send us back.”

Migrant workers are sometimes pushed into undocumented status when employers fail to obtain or renew their work permits, exposing them to arrest by immigration authorities and subsequent deportation, and making them even more vulnerable to exploitation at work.

A former Bangladeshi migrant worker said his employer failed to renew his work permit and later denied employing him when contacted by immigration officials following his arrest. He spent 46 days at the Muar immigration detention center while awaiting deportation.

He said he was never brought before a court. “When we first arrived at the detention center, we were stripped naked,” he said. “We were asked to hold our ears and squat up and down fifty times. I was humiliated because we were naked and being punished without reason.”

He described overcrowding, unsanitary conditions, and frequent physical punishment by immigration authorities. “The detention place had a capacity for 70 people, [but] 140 people were placed there,” he said. “We slept on the hard flooring. We had to sleep almost on top of each other because there was no space.”

Fortify Rights has previously documented severe overcrowding, collective punishment, medical neglect, and inadequate food and drinking water in Malaysia’s immigration detention centers. Fortify Rights has calculated that there are at least 140 deaths in immigration detention since 2024.

Both Bangladesh and Malaysia have obligations under international and domestic law to protect migrant workers from forced labor, unsafe working conditions, wage theft, and other forms of labor exploitation. However, because Malaysia has not joined many major human rights instruments, a binding bilateral labor agreement is essential to legally guarantee these rights.
 
Malaysia is not a party to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) or the International Convention on the Protection of the Rights of All Migrant Workers and Members of Their Families (ICRMW). However, certain rights reflected in the ICCPR are recognized under customary international law to which all nations need to abide. Article 8 of the ICCPR prohibits forced labor and Article 22 protects freedom of association, including the right to form and join unions.

Malaysia and Bangladesh have both ratified the International Labour Organization (ILO) Forced Labour Convention, 1930 (No. 29), as well as the 2014 Protocol to the Forced Labour Convention. Malaysia is bound by the ILO Occupational Safety and Health Convention, 1981 (No. 155), which aims to “prevent accidents and injury to health arising out of, linked with or occurring in the course of work, by minimising, so far as is reasonably practicable, the causes of hazards inherent in the working environment.” 

The ILO’s General Principles and Operational Guidelines for Fair Recruitment provide that governments should ensure migrant workers have meaningful access to grievance and dispute-resolution mechanisms. The guidelines state: “Governments should take steps to ensure the availability and operation of grievance and other dispute resolution mechanisms that are accessible in practice, rapid and affordable.”

In November 2025, U.N. human rights experts said in a statement: “We are deeply troubled that fraudulent recruitment and the exploitation of migrants remain widespread and systematic in Malaysia.” The experts continued: “We have also been informed that a small number of recruitment agencies operate as a closed syndicate sustained by corruption, lack of transparency, and systemic exploitation.”

In September 2025, Bangladesh’s Anti-Corruption Commission (ACC) filed 13 cases against 31 individuals associated with overseas recruitment agencies for allegedly embezzling 1,159 crore taka (approximately US$95 million) in connection with excessive fees charged to Bangladeshi workers seeking employment in Malaysia. The ACC alleged that agencies charged workers as much as five times the government-fixed recruitment fee of 78,990 taka (approximately US$645).

A senior representative of the ACC in Chattogram, Bangladesh, told Fortify Rights, “We investigate fraud and money laundering. … If we find or hear about migrant worker abuse cases we transfer it to the police. Sometimes to the Ministry of Labor [and Employment].” ACC said there were currently no investigations into recruitment agencies in Chattogram related to migrant workers going to Malaysia. 

Remittances from migrant workers are critical for Bangladesh’s economy and an important contributor to the country’s foreign-exchange reserves. Between July 2025 and April 2026, Bangladesh reportedly received US$29.33 billion in remittances, an increase of 19.5% compared with the same period the previous year. In April 2026, migrant workers and other Bangladeshis abroad reportedly sent home US$3.13 billion, marking the fifth consecutive month in which remittances exceeded US$3 billion. 

On July 31, 2026, Bangladesh Prime Minister’s Adviser Mahdi Amin, on a trip to Malaysia, said that new migrant workers will start arriving in Malaysia the “last week of August.”

“Bangladesh and Malaysia have an opportunity to reform a recruitment system that has left migrant workers in debt and vulnerable to abuse, by negotiating and signing a legally binding bilateral labor agreement,” said Abdullah al Mamun, President of Migrant Welfare Network. “Workers should be able to migrate for decent jobs without paying excessive fees, losing their wages, or risking their health and safety. The previous MOUs lacked these legally binding obligations, and led to widespread abuses.”

“Previous MOUs have failed to protect labor rights and exposed massive corruption, leading to their repeated suspension,” said John Quinley, Director at Fortify Rights. “What Bangladesh and Malaysia need is not another flawed MOU, but a legally binding bilateral labor agreement to avoid a repeat of such labor abuses and corruption.”
