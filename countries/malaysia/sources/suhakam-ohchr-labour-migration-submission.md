# SUBMISSION TO WORKING GROUP ON BUSINESS AND HUMAN RIGHTS

# BY HUMAN RIGHTS COMMISSION OF MALAYSIA (SUHAKAM)

## INPUTS FOR THE REPORT ON “LABOUR MIGRATION, BUSINESS AND HUMAN RIGHTS” TO THE 80TH SESSION OF THE UN GENERAL ASSEMBLY

## 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 Transnational migrant workers form the backbone of Malaysia’s labour-intensive sectors, including construction, manufacturing, plantations, domestic work, and medical supply industries. As of September 2024, there are approximately 2.4 million registered foreign workers in Malaysia, nearly 14% of the national workforce.<sup>1</sup> They contribute significantly to Malaysia’s economic growth and integration into global supply chains. However, despite their economic importance, migrant workers continue to face widespread discrimination, exploitation, and abuse throughout the migration cycle—from recruitment in countries of origin to employment and eventual repatriation.

1.2 Migrant workers, particularly those from countries such as Nepal, Bangladesh, and Indonesia, remain at heightened risk of human rights violations, including forced labour, wage theft, unsafe working conditions, and lack of access to justice. These risks are further compounded for women workers, undocumented persons, and those in informal or isolated work environments such as domestic work or remote plantations.

1.3 Malaysia’s migration governance system, combined with reliance on third-party outsourcing and opaque recruitment networks, creates structural conditions that allow exploitative practices to flourish. Despite international commitments and domestic policy reforms, significant protection gaps persist, undermining the rights and dignity of migrant workers.

## • Malaysia’s commitment

1.4 At the global level, normative frameworks have evolved positively. The endorsement of the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (UNGPs), the ratification of key ILO instruments such as Forced Labour Convention, 1930 (C29) and the Protocol of 2014 to the Forced Labour Convention (P029), and global development targets like Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 8.7, which calls for the eradication of forced labour, all represent major milestones. However, the implementation of these standards remains uneven, particularly in cross-border contexts. Migrant workers continue to be excluded from effective protection systems and are seldom included in policymaking processes that directly impact their rights, recruitment pathways, and access to remedy.

1.5 At the regional level, declarations such as the ASEAN Declaration on the Protection and Promotion of the Rights of Migrant Workers 2007 (Cebu Declaration)<sup>2</sup> and the ASEAN Consensus on the Protection and Promotion of the Rights of Migrant Workers 2017$^3$ provides a cooperative framework but remains non-binding, limiting its enforceability. Institutional mechanisms to uphold migrant rights across the region remain underdeveloped, and national implementation varies significantly in both scope and substance.

1.6 Nationally, the Government has taken several positive steps in recent years especially in addressing forced labour issues in Malaysia. These include the launch of the first National Action Plan on Forced Labour (NAPFL) 2021–2025$^4$, aimed at eradicating forced labour by 2030 and promoting safe migration practices; key amendments to labour legislations such as the Workers' Minimum Standards of Housing and Amenities Act 1990 [Act 446], the Employment Act 1955 [Act 265], which now includes Section 90B on Forced Labour; amendments to the Anti-Trafficking in Persons and Anti-Smuggling of Migrants Act 2007 (ATIPSOM); and the ratification of the International Labour Organisation’s Protocol No. 29 on Forced Labour. In addition, the Government introduced the “Working for Workers” (WFW) mobile application as a digital platform to facilitate the lodging of labour complaints by foreign and local workers and submitted Malaysia's letter of intent to join Alliance 8.7 as a Pathfinder Country under the SDGs. Malaysia is also in the process of drafting its first National Action Plan on Business and Human Rights (NAPBHR), with the National Baseline Assessment on Business and Human Rights (NBA) launched in August 2024.<sup>5</sup>

## • Report of the Independent Committee on the Management of Foreign Workers$^7$

1.9 As part of broader policy efforts, in 2018, the Government commissioned an Independent Committee on the Management of Foreign Workers, tasked with proposing comprehensive policy reforms to manage the deployment of foreign workers in a holistic and sustainable manner. Although the Committee completed its report in 2019, it was only released publicly in 2023. The report stands as one of the most comprehensive national assessments of Malaysia’s foreign worker management system to date.

1.10 The Committee’s findings revealed deep-rooted structural issues in the management of migrant labour, including fragmented institutional responsibilities, lack of transparency in recruitment processes, weak enforcement mechanisms, corruption risks, inadequate database, and policy incoherence. It presented a wide-ranging set of recommendations aimed at addressing overall national policies, recruitment policies, employment policies, repatriation policies, the regulation of undocumented workers, the situation of stateless persons and refugees as well as access to justice. Notably, the Independent Committee recommended that the Ministry of Human Resources be designated as the single lead authority responsible for formulating and implementing policies pertinent to the national foreign workers management system. This was intended to replace the current fragmented, multi-agency system that has led to inefficiencies, overlapping mandates and inconsistent enforcement. However, the absence of implementation of the Committee’s proposal reflects persistent policy and enforcement gaps.

## 2. SUHAKAM’S INITIATIVES

2.1 The Human Rights Commission of Malaysia (SUHAKAM), established under the Human Rights Commission of Malaysia Act 1999 (Act 597). SUHAKAM’s mandate includes to promote awareness and provide education on human rights; to advise and assist the government in formulating legislation; recommend the subscription or accession of treaties and international instruments in the field of human rights; and inquire into complaints regarding infringements of human rights.

2.2 Based on its mandate, SUHAKAM has consistently engaged in protecting and promoting the rights of all persons in Malaysia, including non-citizens such as transnational migrant workers. Over the past decade, SUHAKAM has conducted a range of activities, such as policy dialogues with ministries and embassies, stakeholder consultations, inquire complaints, visits, capacity buildings, press statements and monitoring activities focused on migrant labour, forced labour, access to justice and ethical recruitment.

2.3 This submission reflects SUHAKAM's findings from its eight multi-sectoral consultation sessions held between 2020 and 2024, with stakeholder across the medical supply, construction, manufacturing and plantation sectors, as well as in the states of Johor, Sabah, Penang and Sarawak. In addition, two focus group discussions were conducted specifically on domestic workers, given their distinct vulnerabilities and working conditions. These consultations involved diverse stakeholders, including migrant workers, employers, business associations, NGOs, CSOs, state officials, government agencies, embassies, academician, and international partners. The objective of the consultation was to provide a platform for stakeholders including companies to share concerns and challenges in addressing forced labour issues in their activities.

2.4 A key issue that was raised throughout various consultations is that the exploitation of migrant workers can manifest in various forms. A central tenant to the exploitation of migrant workers is the lack of enforcement. Allegations of migrant workers being subject to multiple forms of exploitation such as being deceived about the legality of their contracts or their work conditions, withholding wages, and illegal deductions by employers to pay for exorbitant recruitment fees should be addressed.

## 3. ISSUES CONCERNING MIGRANT WORKERS IN MALAYSIA

3.1 SUHAKAM's eight consultation sessions, held across four industries (medical supply, construction, manufacturing, and plantations) and four states (Johor, Penang, Sabah, and Sarawak), uncovered consistent patterns of abuse, vulnerability, and systemic governance failures in the treatment of transnational migrant workers. The issues raised by stakeholders—including migrant workers, employers, government agencies, and civil society—can be categorised into four interrelated domains:

## I. Prior to Arrival – Recruitment and Pre-departure Issues

## i. Fraudulent and Deceptive Recruitment Practices
The recruitment process for migrant workers is often complex, where they frequently face unethical and exploitative practices even before arriving in Malaysia. SUHAKAM's consultations revealed that the recruitment process is dominated by a chain of intermediaries, including licensed agencies and unauthorised sub-agents as well as informal brokers. These actors often operate in rural areas of origin countries (such as Nepal, Bangladesh, and Indonesia) and mislead workers about key job terms. Recruitment often begins in rural areas, where local sub-agents, embedded within communities, approach workers with promises of high-paying, secure jobs in Malaysia. These agents often misrepresent critical aspects of employment, whereby, the migrant workers are promised decent wages, legal contracts, specific job roles and better working conditions.

However, upon arrival in Malaysia, they discover significant discrepancies—such as different work locations, types of jobs, or wages lower than promised. In some cases, there was no contract at all, or the contract signed in the country of origin was replaced with a different one in Malaysia, and often in a language they cannot read.

## ii. Charging of Exorbitant Recruitment Fees and Resultant Debt Bondage
A central and consistently raised concern in all consultations was the imposition of excessive and unregulated recruitment fees on migrant workers. Recruitment agencies often charge high fees to the point where it is exploitative to the workers. The figures vary for each country of origin, and it can range up to RM 18,000 – RM 22,000, and in some cases, workers claimed to have paid up to RM50,000. These payments were often justified as covering documentation, medical screening, visas, airfare, and agent services. However, SUHAKAM found that many of these costs were grossly inflated, with sub-agents or brokers adding unauthorised mark-ups.

Workers commonly take high-interest loans to finance these fees, or pawning family assets or borrowing from informal moneylenders, resulting in debt bondage even before their employment begins.

Despite some employers’ committed to ethical recruitment and observing a “zero-cost” recruitment model, they still facing significant challenges in monitoring early-stage recruitment practices and workers consistently reported bearing the actual financial burden of their recruitment.

## iii. Use of Unregulated Sub-agents

The prevalence of unregulated sub-agents is one of the most persistent and damaging elements of the recruitment system. These actors operate informally, without contracts, licences, or oversight—often serving as the first point of contact for prospective migrant workers in rural communities. Workers have little to no way of verifying the legitimacy of these agents, and the absence of complaint mechanisms further entrenches their vulnerability.

Despite the recognised role of sub-agents in facilitating abuse, Malaysia’s legal and regulatory framework does not clearly define or govern their activities. Section 14B of the Private Employment Agencies Act 1981 [Act 246] allows for placement fees but does not define legal vs. illegal charges or regulate informal actors. This legal ambiguity allows agents to impose hidden costs and falsely claim legitimacy.

## iv. Transparency of Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) and Absence of Bilateral Oversight

A significant challenge in addressing recruitment of migrant workers lies in the lack of transparency in Government-to-Government (G2G) agreements between Malaysia and source countries. These Memorandum of Understandings (MoUs) are typically not made publicly available, making it difficult for stakeholders, including employers, trade unions, civil society, and even the workers themselves.

Many MoUs fail to provide a detailed breakdown of recruitment fees, cost-sharing mechanism, or enforcement obligations. This opacity allows for excessive profiteering by recruitment agencies and sub0agents in both source countries and Malaysia. It also undermines transparency and creates confusion among employers and workers regarding what cost are legitimate or lawful.

More critically, there is no effective bilateral enforcement mechanism between Malaysia and major labour-sending countries to regulate and monitor recruitment agencies and sub-agents. Complaints related to fees, deception, or contract substitution that occur in countries of origin are rarely investigated or resolved, due to jurisdictional limitations and limited cross-border cooperation. This lack of accountability at the transnational level allows for widespread abuse in the pre-arrival phase, and workers have no means to lodge complaints once they are in Malaysia.

During consultations, plantation companies reported that while many have adopted internal “zero recruitment fee” policies, they lack the tools to monitor compliance in source countries. They stressed the need for a strong, enforceable national policy and bilateral cooperation to monitor, certify, and penalise unethical recruitment practices. Without this, even well-intentioned employers are unable to ensure fair recruitment.

## v. Lack of Pre-departure Training and Legal Awareness

Consultations revealed that pre-departure orientation and legal education are either inadequate or non-existent. Many workers—especially those from rural areas with low literacy—arrive in Malaysia without knowledge of their rights, contract terms, grievance mechanisms, or basic labour protections. Some workers were unaware of their employers’ names, job scope, or work locations prior to departure. Others did not understand the content of documents they signed. This lack of informed and voluntary migration creates significant power imbalances and undermines the ability of enforcement agencies to respond to violations effectively.

## vi. Poor Migration Governance in Countries of Origin

Recruitment challenges are compounded by weak governance systems in sending countries. Many do not maintain national registries of agents or sub-agents, lack enforcement mechanisms, or have no effective oversight of recruitment practices. Workers are often recruited without formal contracts, proper documentation, or meaningful consent.

Corruption, collusion between licensed agencies and sub-agents, and lax regulatory enforcement were commonly cited during consultations as barriers to reform. As a result, Malaysia’s efforts to promote ethical recruitment are routinely undermined by governance failures in origin countries.

## II. Upon Arrival and Living Conditions – Working and Living Environment in Malaysia

## i. Contract Substitution and Job Mismatch

One of the most persistent and concerning violations reported during SUHAKAM’s consultations is the practice of contract substitution—a form of deception that directly undermines the principle of informed and voluntary employment. Upon arrival in Malaysia, many migrant workers discover that their employment contracts have been unilaterally altered or substituted with documents containing inferior terms and conditions. These include lower wages, extended working hours, denial of rest days, changes in job roles, and more precarious employment arrangements.

Workers who were initially promised jobs in sectors such as manufacturing or services reported being transferred without consent to labour-intensive and hazardous sectors like construction or plantation work. These new roles are frequently more physically demanding and are accompanied by fewer protections and benefits. In some cases, the job scope was entirely misrepresented, or no formal job description was provided at all.

The substituted contracts are often presented in a language the worker does not understand, raising serious concerns about the validity of consent. Some workers reported signing documents under duress or without the opportunity to seek clarification. This practice directly violates the principle of free, prior, and informed consent, and undermines the credibility of Malaysia’s labour governance framework.

Alarmingly, there are cases where workers were never provided with a copy of their employment contract, leaving them unaware of the specific terms governing their employment. This violates the Employment Act 1955 [Act 265], which mandates that every contract of service exceeding one month must be in writing and clearly state matters such as the rate of pay, working hours, and entitlements.

Further exacerbating the problem, several participants shared that near the end of a worker’s contract term, some employers have deliberately falsely reported the worker as having absconded to immigration authorities. This tactic is used to avoid the cost of repatriation and may result in the worker being detained, criminalized, and deported, often without being paid outstanding wages or benefits. This practice not only contravenes Malaysia’s labour laws but also highlights the imbalance of power between employers and migrant workers, particularly when the latter are undocumented or lack legal representation.

This systemic abuse points to a broader failure of enforcement and monitoring mechanisms, allowing employers to act with impunity. The absence of standardised procedures for contract verification, multilingual documentation, and periodic oversight creates a regulatory vacuum that perpetuates worker vulnerability and contractual deception.

## ii. Withholding of Passports and Restriction of Movement

A widespread and deeply concerning practices reported during SUHAKAM’s consultations is the retention of migrant workers’ passports by employers or agents. Migrant workers consistently reported being denied access to their own identification documents, effectively limiting their freedom of movement and increasing dependency on employers. This practice, though illegal under Malaysian law, is widespread and goes largely unenforced. In many cases, workers were not even allowed to access their passports for personal or legal needs unless permission was granted.

The Passports Act 1966 [Act 150] makes it an offence for any person—including an employer—to unlawfully possess another individual’s passport. Despite this clear legal prohibition, reports gathered by SUHAKAM indicate that many employers routinely confiscate passports immediately upon a worker’s arrival, often under the pretext of “safekeeping.” Workers are rarely given written confirmation or receipts, and in many cases, passports are stored in locations made deliberately inaccessible, such as locked office drawers, remote management facilities, or third-party premises.

This forced retention of identity documents has far-reaching implications. It directly restricts workers’ freedom of movement, effectively tethering them to their place of employment and preventing them from seeking alternative jobs, accessing basic services, or fleeing abusive conditions. Migrant workers are rendered invisible and powerless, unable to lodge complaints, travel independently, or verify their legal status when confronted by enforcement officers.

In some cases, workers were not even allowed to access their passports for essential needs such as medical treatment, banking, or renewing their work permits without first seeking the employer’s permission. The resulting dependency on employers deepens the unequal power dynamic and serves as a tool of control and intimidation, reinforcing conditions analogous to involuntary servitude or forced labour.

Additionally, SUHAKAM’s consultations noted that some employers deliberately use passport confiscation to prevent workers from absconding, especially in sectors where job mismatch, wage delays, or abuse are prevalent. The practice also obstructs access to justice, as workers without documentation are less likely to report violations for fear of detention or deportation by authorities.

Conversely, a limited number of companies have acknowledged and attempted to comply with legal standards under Act 150 by allowing workers to retain their passports or by adopting voluntary consent-based safekeeping policies. However, these efforts remain inconsistent and are not backed by uniform national enforcement protocols or penalties for non-compliance.

Moreover, employers have raised concerns regarding situations in which workers lose their passports or deliberately misplace them, seeking employer assistance to obtain replacements. While this is a valid operational challenge, it underscores the need for clear and balanced procedures—ones that protect both employer interests and workers' rights without resorting to unlawful retention practices.

## iii. Poor and Overcrowded Living Conditions

Substandard living conditions remain a critical concern across all consultations. Migrant workers often reside in overcrowded, unhygienic, and poorly maintained accommodations provided by employers. These inadequate living environments, especially in construction, plantations, and manufacturing sectors, fail to meet basic health standards. Dormitories were densely packed, with some rooms housing up to 30-100 individuals, often with insufficient ventilation or sanitation.

The COVID-19 pandemic laid bare the severity of these conditions, as cramped dormitories became hotspots for infection. Clusters of COVID-19 were widely reported in worker housing, highlighting the urgent need for enforcement of housing standards under the Workers’ Minimum Standards of Housing and Amenities (Amendment) Act 2019 [Act 446] and related emergency ordinances.

While Act 446 requires employers to obtain approval for accommodation designs, stakeholders reported confusion due to inconsistent local government procedures, delays in approval processes, and lack of a standardised timeline—especially when dealing with municipal councils. Employers face penalties if their Accommodation Certificate is not granted in time.

Challenges are more acute for female migrant workers, particularly in construction, where Indonesian women face discriminatory attitudes and lack gender-appropriate housing. Similarly, plantation companies—employing large numbers of workers—report difficulties in meeting legal housing standards due to the scale and remote nature of their operations.

Xenophobia and public resistance to integrating migrants into residential areas exacerbate housing issues. While the Ministry of Housing and Local Government (KPKT) introduced a Centralised Labour Quarters (CLQ) initiative in 2021, many states still lack clear, actionable policies. For small and medium enterprises (SMEs), the high cost of building dormitories remains prohibitive, resulting in unregulated, overcrowded, and unsafe housing solutions.

## iv. Unsafe Working Conditions and Denial of Rest Days
Migrant workers across sectors, particularly in construction and manufacturing, reported unsafe working environments, including lack of personal protective equipment (PPE), exposure to hazardous chemicals, and absence of medical care in case of injuries. In plantations, workers were sometimes exposed to pesticides without training or protection. Furthermore, many reported working long hours without overtime pay, and in some cases, were denied their statutory rest days or forced to work continuously without adequate breaks, contributing to fatigue, mental distress, and injury.

Health and safety risks remain a persistent concern. SUHAKAM noted that there are stark differences in employers’ compliance with occupational safety standards. While some companies have taken steps to implement workplace safety protocols, many continue to operate with minimal oversight.

According to data from the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM), the manufacturing industry recorded the highest number of occupational accidents in 2020, with 10,303 reported cases, followed by the construction industry with 3,968 cases.<sup>8</sup> Fatal accidents in construction continue to rise, with unregulated worksites significantly increasing the risks for workers, particularly those without health insurance. The situation is even more severe for undocumented workers, who are frequently denied medical attention or compensation following workplace injuries.

Alarmingly, some employers in the construction sector were reported to deliberately conceal accidents involving undocumented workers. In such instances, workers are neither reimbursed for medical costs nor permitted to file complaints or police reports, leaving them without access to remedy or legal redress.

## v. Withholding of Wages and Wage Deductions
Wage withholding is another pervasive issue. In the manufacturing industry, workers reported wages being delayed by up to ten months, while those in construction faced delays of up to five months. Employers often made deductions for recruitment costs, housing, or unidentified “administrative fees” without explanation or proper documentation. These deductions, frequently made without the worker’s consent or knowledge, not only violate their labour rights but further compound their indebtedness and dependency.

Another major concern is the deliberate refusal by some employers to provide salary slips or statements, which allows for wage manipulation and exploitation, particularly of migrant workers. This practice violates employment laws, as failing or refusing to provide salary slips is an offence. In some cases, employers merely jot down salary amounts on a piece of paper without offering a detailed breakdown or explanation.

According to data from the Ministry of Human Resources, 40% of grievances lodged through the “Working for Workers” app relate to unpaid wages or illegal deductions. Workers attempting to file complaints often lacked payslips, contracts, or proof of the agreed terms—documents often retained by employers. SUHAKAM found that undocumented workers were especially vulnerable, as they were frequently denied full wages and prevented from pursuing legal remedies due to fear of detention or deportation.

## vi. Right to Form Unions and Hold Positions

Despite legal guarantees under the Federal Constitution, the Employment Act 1955 [Act 265], the Trade Union Act 1959 [Act 262], and the Industrial Relations Act 1967 [Act 177], migrant workers in Malaysia continue to face entrenched structural and legal barriers that restrict their freedom of association and participation in trade unions. While existing legislation permits migrant workers to become members of trade unions, they are expressly prohibited from forming their own unions or holding leadership positions within existing ones. This legal restriction significantly undermines the ability of migrant workers to organise, advocate for their rights, and engage meaningfully in collective bargaining processes.

The consultations revealed that these legal limitations are compounded by practical obstacles on the ground. Workers frequently encounter language barriers, communication difficulties, and cultural misunderstandings that further inhibit their ability to participate effectively in union activities. Many employers and companies lack mechanisms to facilitate dialogue with migrant workers, which prevents grievances from being adequately addressed. To mitigate this, some stakeholders suggested the implementation of regular, multilingual workplace meetings facilitated by worker representatives who can act as translators and intermediaries between management and the workforce.

Moreover, participants reported that certain employers unlawfully obstruct workers’ attempts to join trade unions, despite the legal right to do so. In several instances, companies were said to deny recognition to unions or discourage membership through intimidation or misinformation. Such practices not only contravene domestic law but also violate international labour standards concerning freedom of association.

An additional concern raised during the consultations was the exclusion of subcontracted or outsourced workers from union participation. Workers employed through labour contractors—rather than being directly hired by companies—are often denied the right to join existing unions and are excluded from the benefits of collective agreements negotiated by directly employed workers.

## III. Access to Justice and Grievance Mechanisms

## i. Structural and Legal Barriers
Despite the existence of protective legal instruments such as the Employment Act 1955, Industrial Relations Act 1967, and the Federal Constitution, migrant workers face considerable structural hurdles in accessing justice. Many are unaware of their legal entitlements and are unfamiliar with available complaint mechanisms. This lack of awareness is compounded by the absence of multilingual legal information and culturally appropriate outreach. Workers on temporary contracts or employed through outsourcing arrangements are especially vulnerable, as they are often excluded from grievance processes due to unclear lines of responsibility. Furthermore, the withholding of passports, contracts, payslips, and other crucial documents by employers deprives workers of essential evidence when filing claims. These practices severely undermine the effectiveness of legal protections and create a climate of impunity.

## ii. Procedural Challenges and Intimidation
Migrant workers often hesitate to lodge complaints due to fear of retaliation, dismissal, or deportation. Consultations revealed that workers who attempt to file complaints are sometimes falsely accused of absconding, a tactic used by employers to avoid repatriation costs or back-pay obligations. In addition, long delays in the processing of complaints by the Labour Department and the Labour Court discourage workers from pursuing claims. Even where workers do initiate legal action, proceedings can be prolonged and are difficult to navigate without assistance.

Moreover, undocumented workers—who are often the most exploited—are particularly disadvantaged. Their irregular status places them in a precarious position, with no legal avenue for redress without risking arrest or deportation.

## iii. Language and Communication Barriers
Language remains a major obstacle to justice. Many migrant workers speak little or no Bahasa Malaysia or English and are thus unable to communicate with enforcement agencies, legal officers, or tribunal representatives. The lack of professional interpreters, especially in administrative or legal settings, undermines the principle of fair hearing and due process.

---

This challenge extends to written documentation, where workers are asked to sign forms or contracts in unfamiliar languages, including during legal proceedings. The inability to understand legal instructions or defend oneself effectively in a foreign language reinforces workers' marginalisation within the justice system.

## iv. Limited Access to Legal Aid and Support
Although legal aid is theoretically available, in practice, access is limited—particularly for migrant workers in rural or industrial areas. Civil society organisations and trade unions often fill this gap by assisting workers in lodging complaints or representing them in court. However, these organisations are overstretched and under-resourced. Workers also face practical obstacles such as lack of transportation, difficulty in taking time off work, and fear of retaliation from employers.

Additionally, not all workers have the financial means to pursue claims, especially in the absence of immediate income or while under threat of job loss. These factors contribute to widespread underreporting of rights violations.

## v. Ineffective Grievance Mechanisms
Several government initiatives, including the Working for Workers mobile application, have been introduced to facilitate reporting of complaints by local and foreign workers. However, many migrant workers are unaware of these tools or find them inaccessible due to language and digital literacy barriers. Consultations noted that nearly 40% of complaints received via the app relate to wage issues—especially withholding of wages and unlawful deductions—but enforcement following these complaints remains inconsistent.

Furthermore, mechanisms for handling complaints are often fragmented. The lack of coordination between labour authorities, immigration enforcement, and the police weakens institutional responses and can even place complainants at risk of immigration enforcement rather than protection.

## vi. Gender-Specific Barriers
Women migrant workers, particularly those in domestic work, face additional gendered barriers in accessing justice. Many live in their employers’ homes, isolated from public spaces and support services. Fear of sexual harassment, cultural stigma, and the absence of gender-sensitive complaint mechanisms further discourage them from coming forward. The lack of female officers trained in gender-responsive approaches within enforcement agencies exacerbates these challenges.

Women workers are also less likely to be aware of their rights and may face retaliation not only from employers but from recruitment agents and even members of their own communities. SUHAKAM’s consultations emphasised the need for confidential, accessible, and culturally sensitive grievance channels that specifically address the vulnerabilities of women migrant workers.

## vii. Lack of Accountability and Enforcement
One of the most consistent findings from consultations is the lack of accountability for exploitative employers. Employers who violate labour rights are rarely prosecuted, and sanctions are minimal or unenforced. This culture of impunity emboldens bad actors and deters victims from seeking redress. Additionally, the complexity of supply chains and the use of subcontractors and third-party agents make it difficult to assign liability for violations, particularly when workers are not directly employed by the company benefitting from their labour.

## viii. Absence of Cross-border Complaint Mechanisms
There is currently no coordinated cross-border system for handling complaints arising from recruitment-related abuses in countries of origin. Migrant workers have no access to redress for fraud, overcharging, or deception once they are in Malaysia, and agencies in origin countries operate with impunity. This lack of bilateral grievance mechanisms means that abuses in recruitment go unpunished and unrepaired.

## IV. Gender Dimensions – Women Migrant Workers and Domestic Work

## i. Gender-based Violence, Harassment, and Exploitation
SUHAKAM’s consultations and engagements with CSOs working with women migrants revealed serious gender-based abuses, particularly among female domestic workers. Women reported experiencing sexual harassment, verbal abuse, psychological manipulation, and gender-based discrimination, often within private and unmonitored settings such as employers’ homes.

## ii. Lack of Legal Protection for Domestic Workers
Domestic workers, most of whom are women, are excluded from core protections under the Employment Act 1955, including limitations on working hours, minimum rest days, and access to labour courts. Their working environments are highly isolated, and inspections are nearly impossible, resulting in undetected and unremedied abuse. Many work long hours without breaks or compensation, are denied leave, and are forbidden from owning mobile phones or communicating with others.

Further, the Minimum Wages Order 2024 [P.U. (A) 376] explicitly excludes domestic workers from the national minimum wage set at RM1,700.00, placing them in a precarious position without any legally guaranteed baseline salary. Although some MoUs between Malaysia and migrant-sending countries establish minimum wage standards, such as RM1,500 for

---

Indonesian domestic workers, these agreements often lack consistent enforcement and oversight. Consequently, many domestic workers receive wages that fall below the threshold of a living wage, placing them at heightened risk of exploitation, economic insecurity, and financial dependence on their employers.

## iii. Invisibility and Inaccessibility of Grievance Channels
Women migrant workers face additional barriers to justice, including stigma, lack of women-sensitive reporting procedures, and absence of female interpreters or legal officers. Fear of retaliation, deportation, or loss of income often silences victims of harassment or assault. Existing grievance systems are not designed with a gender-sensitive or trauma-informed approach, leaving women workers without a safe space to disclose abuses or seek help.

## 4. RECOMMENDATIONS BY SUHAKAM

4.1 In view of the systemic human rights violations faced by transnational migrant workers across Malaysia’s major labour-intensive sectors, the following measures to strengthen protection, enforcement, accountability, and remedy are recommended. These are structured in line with the three pillars of the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (UNGPs).

## I. State Duty to Protect

## i. Legal and Policy Reform
Integrate the ILO General Principles and Operational Guidelines on Fair Recruitment into domestic labour and migration policies to institutionalise ethical recruitment practices.

Enact legislation to prohibit the charging of recruitment fees to workers at all stages of the migration process, including pre-departure, transit, and post-arrival. Malaysia should operationalise a zero-cost recruitment model with clear liability imposed on employers and licensed agents.

Amend the Employment Act 1955 and relevant subsidiary laws to extend comprehensive labour protection to domestic workers, including rest days, limitation of hours, complaint rights, and wage protection, or introduce a specific Domestic Workers Act.

Criminalise passport retention, and introduce clear enforcement provisions including penalties, blacklisting of non-compliant employers, and automatic investigation of such cases.

---

## ii. Recruitment Governance and Bilateral Oversight
Establish bilateral agreements with sending countries (e.g., Nepal, Bangladesh, Indonesia) that include provisions for:
• Joint monitoring of recruitment agencies and sub-agents
• Verification of employment contracts prior to departure
• Cross-border grievance redressal mechanisms

Develop a centralised and transparent recruitment portal or online system that allows workers to access verified information on job offers, employers, fees, and channels for complaint—before, during, and after employment.

## iii. Regulation and Enforcement
Strengthen the enforcement capacity of the Department of Labour, Department of Occupational Safety and Health (DOSH), and Immigration Department through increased budget, specialised training, and regular unannounced inspections.

Enforce compliance with the Workers' Minimum Standards of Housing and Amenities Act 1990 (Act 446), particularly in high-risk sectors such as construction and medical supply. Publicise enforcement data and penalties issued.

Create firewalls between labour complaint mechanisms and immigration enforcement to ensure that undocumented status—particularly resulting from employer negligence—does not deter workers from seeking justice.

## iv. Data and Monitoring
Establish a national database of recruitment agents, sub-agents, and outsourcing companies linked to migrant worker recruitment and deployment, with public transparency on violations and compliance records.

Develop a migrant worker rights monitoring mechanism at the national level that includes civil society, trade unions, and employers to oversee forced labour indicators, complaints, and remediation outcomes.

## v. Accede to Key International Instruments
Malaysia should demonstrate its commitment to the protection of migrant workers by acceding to relevant international human rights and labour instruments that provide robust legal frameworks and guidance. These include the International Convention on the Protection of the Rights of All Migrant Workers and Members of Their Families (ICMW), ILO Convention on Domestic Workers Convention 2011 (No. 189) and others.

---

## II. Corporate Responsibility to Respect

## i. Mandatory Human Rights Due Diligence
Require all companies—particularly those in high-risk sectors—to conduct mandatory human rights due diligence across their supply chains, with a focus on:
• Recruitment practices
• Employment terms
• Outsourced labour arrangements
• Working and living conditions
Due diligence must include the vetting and monitoring of recruitment agencies and sub-agents, and ensure contracts are provided to workers in a language they understand before deployment.

## ii. Ethical Recruitment and Contract Transparency
Adopt and implement the Employer Pays Principle, ensuring all recruitment-related costs are borne by the employer.

Ensure that all migrant workers are provided with written contracts, in their native language, signed by both parties prior to deployment, and that workers retain their own copy.

## iii. Inclusive and Worker-Centred Grievance Mechanisms
Establish confidential, independent, and multilingual grievance mechanisms accessible to all workers, including those indirectly employed through contractors or outsourcing arrangements.

Grievance mechanisms must include:
• Anonymity and protection from retaliation
• Female personnel for gender-sensitive complaints
• Timely resolution pathways with follow-up

## iv. Responsible Supply Chain Management
Companies, particularly exporters in medical supply and manufacturing, must conduct supply chain mapping to identify forced labour risks and ensure third-party labour suppliers are compliant with both domestic law and international human rights standards.

Public reporting on labour rights audits, remediation efforts, and worker outcomes should be made part of corporate ESG disclosures.

## III. Access to Effective Remedy

## i. Strengthen Judicial and Non-Judicial Mechanisms
Expand access to labour tribunals, civil courts, and mediation services for migrant workers by:
• Providing free and qualified interpretation services

---

• Ensuring that complaint proceedings are fast-tracked for migrant cases
• Allowing complainants to pursue cases even if they return to their home country

Ensure that remedies include wage recovery, compensation for harm, cancellation of recruitment debts, and where necessary, access to shelter and psychosocial support.

## ii. Legal Aid and Worker Support Services
Institutionalise and fund a legal aid mechanism for migrant workers, in collaboration with bar associations and CSOs, for labour and criminal cases.

Support mobile complaint units and legal clinics in remote and high-risk areas (e.g., plantations, remote factories) to ensure proactive outreach.

## iii. Transfer Employment and cancellation of work permit
Allow migrant workers to transfer employment pending the resolution of any labour dispute or complaint and be permitted to continue working under new employers after the case concludes. This is crucial to preventing income insecurity, retaliation, and dependency on exploitative employers during prolonged case proceedings.

To ensure impartiality and fair treatment, the unilateral right of employers to terminate a migrant worker’s employment, particularly in the midst of an active complaint, should be removed, and oversight by a competent government authority should be mandatory. No employer shall terminate a work permit without the approval from the Labour Department. Any purported termination done without such approvals shall be ineffective.

## iv. Cross-border Access to Justice
Work with countries of origin to develop cross-border legal cooperation protocols that allow migrant workers to lodge complaints against recruitment agencies post-return.

Enable compensation and fee reimbursement for recruitment fraud or coercion, including via joint investigative bodies or labour attachés stationed in sending countries.

## v. Gender-sensitive Remedial Processes
Ensure grievance and complaint mechanisms are designed with gender responsiveness, including:
• Female officers or caseworkers for handling sensitive complaints
• Private interview spaces for survivors of gender-based violence
• Immediate referrals for medical, legal, and shelter support where needed

---

Extend protections to cover female migrant domestic workers, including redress for sexual harassment, underpayment, overwork, and physical abuse.

## 5. CONCLUSION

5.1 SUHAKAM's consultations and findings reveal that the human rights challenges faced by transnational migrant workers in Malaysia are not incidental or isolated, but instead stem from systemic deficiencies in recruitment governance, weak legal protections, poor enforcement, and the absence of accessible remedies. The forced labour indicators observed across multiple sectors—medical supply, construction, manufacturing, plantation, and domestic work—underscore the urgent need for comprehensive and coordinated reform.

5.2 Despite Malaysia's commitments under international human rights and labour standards, including the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights, ILO Conventions, and the Sustainable Development Goals, many migrant workers remain trapped in cycles of debt, deception, and vulnerability, often with little or no access to redress. These conditions are exacerbated by opaque labour supply chains, the delegation of employer responsibilities to outsourcing agents, and the lack of gender-sensitive or cross-border grievance mechanisms.

5.3 Migrant workers, especially women and those employed in informal or domestic settings, continue to face multiple and intersecting forms of discrimination and abuse—yet their voices remain largely unheard in policy and corporate decision-making.

5.4 As Malaysia moves forward with national action plans on forced labour 2.0 and national action plan on business and human rights, SUHAKAM reiterates that reform efforts must centre the dignity, agency, and rights of migrant workers. The principle of non-discrimination, the right to access justice, and the obligation to provide effective remedies must be upheld in both policy and practice. This requires not only the strengthening of state institutions but also the active accountability of private sector actors whose operations benefit from migrant labour.

5.5 SUHAKAM remains committed to working with all stakeholders—including government ministries, private sector, civil society, international partners, and the UN system—to eliminate forced labour, advance ethical recruitment, and ensure that no migrant worker is left behind.
