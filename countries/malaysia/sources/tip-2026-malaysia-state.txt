			2026 Trafficking in Persons Report: Malaysia

Malaysia (Tier 2)

The Government of Malaysia does not fully meet the minimum standards for the elimination of trafficking but is making significant efforts to do so.  The government demonstrated overall increased efforts compared with the previous reporting period; therefore, Malaysia remained on Tier 2.  These efforts included increasing trafficking investigations, prosecutions, and convictions; increasing anti-trafficking law enforcement efforts in the palm oil and rubber glove manufacturing sectors, including by initiating two prosecutions for labor trafficking-related offenses in the palm oil sector; initiating prosecutions of three allegedly complicit officials; increasing funding for anti-trafficking efforts; significantly increasing the number of victim assistance specialists; amending regulations under the anti-trafficking law to improve protections for foreign victims; approving a new government-funded shelter for male child victims; and taking steps to increase anti-trafficking capacity in East Malaysia.  However, the government did not meet the minimum standards in several key areas.  The government identified fewer confirmed victims, and inconsistent application of victim identification procedures resulted in the inappropriate penalization of some victims solely for unlawful acts committed as a direct result of being trafficked.  Corruption and official complicity in trafficking crimes remained significant concerns, inhibiting law enforcement action, and officials continued to conflate human trafficking with migrant smuggling, undermining victim identification efforts.  The government did not effectively regulate labor recruiters, including those who charged workers recruitment fees that facilitated debt-based coercion and vulnerability to forced labor.  Victims’ access to services remained contingent upon cooperation with investigations and prosecutions, and the lack of adequate trafficking shelters and law enforcement expertise outside urban areas contributed to inconsistent anti-trafficking efforts in those regions.  The government convicted the majority of traffickers under non-trafficking laws.

					Prosecution			

The government increased anti-trafficking law enforcement efforts.

The 2007 the Anti-Trafficking in Persons and Anti-Smuggling of Migrants (ATIPSOM) Act, as amended, criminalized labor trafficking and sex trafficking and prescribed punishments of three to 20 years’ imprisonment and a fine, which were sufficiently stringent and, with respect to sex trafficking, commensurate with those prescribed for other grave offenses, such as rape.  The Employment Act of 1955, as amended, criminalized forced labor offenses by employers using threats of intimidation, restriction of movement, or fraud to induce labor or services.  The law prescribed penalties of up to two years’ imprisonment, a fine of 100,000 Malaysian ringgit (RM) ($24,655), or both; the penalties available under the Employment Act were significantly lower than those available under the ATIPSOM Act.

During the reporting period, the government reported initiating 209 trafficking investigations, compared with 188 investigations in the previous reporting period.  Officials also continued 163 previously initiated investigations — 31 for sex trafficking, 106 for forced labor, and 26 for unspecified forms of trafficking.  The Attorney General’s Chambers (AGC) initiated prosecutions of 128 suspects — 24 for sex trafficking, 99 for forced labor, and five for unspecified trafficking exploitation — compared with 72 prosecutions in the previous reporting period (eight for sex trafficking and 64 for labor trafficking).  An additional 241 prosecutions initiated in previous reporting periods remained ongoing.  Courts convicted 36 traffickers — 10 for sex trafficking, 24 for forced labor, and two for unspecified trafficking exploitation — compared with 26 convictions in the previous reporting period.  Courts sentenced all convicted traffickers to penalties ranging from three to 15 years’ imprisonment under the ATIPSOM Act and from six months to 18 years’ imprisonment under non-trafficking laws.  The government prosecuted 75 alleged traffickers and convicted 12 under the ATIPSOM Act, in addition to prosecuting another 53 offenders under non-trafficking laws.  The government increased anti-trafficking law enforcement efforts in the high-risk palm oil, rubber glove, and manufacturing sectors, conducting 77 simultaneous nationwide operations in January that resulted in 67 arrests under the ATIPSOM Act and the Immigration Act; the government subsequently initiated two prosecutions in the palm oil sector under a trafficking-related provision of the Immigration Act — compared with no such prosecutions in the previous reporting period.

The government made some efforts to address official complicity in trafficking crimes; however, corruption and official complicity remained significant concerns, inhibiting law enforcement action.  During the reporting period, the government initiated prosecutions of three allegedly complicit officials, including a police officer charged under the ATIPSOM Act for alleged sex trafficking and two civil servants for alleged labor trafficking of an Indonesian domestic worker.  An investigation — initiated in the previous reporting period involving dozens of immigration officials arrested for accepting bribes to facilitate illegal entry of foreign nationals at an international airport — remained ongoing.  The government cooperated with other governments on law enforcement operations.

The government increased anti-trafficking training for law enforcement officials — including police, immigration officers, maritime enforcement officers, customs officers, labor inspectors, and prosecutors — on topics including victim identification, trauma-informed approaches, digital forensics, and cross-border cooperation, in partnership with NGOs and international organizations.  The AGC increased the number of specialized prosecutors handling trafficking cases, and the Royal Malaysia Police and the Department of Labor each maintained specialized anti-trafficking units.  The government continued to operate one dedicated trafficking court in Selangor; no courts in Sabah and Sarawak were dedicated exclusively to trafficking cases, and limited resources and law enforcement expertise outside urban areas contributed to inconsistent anti-trafficking efforts in those regions.  NGOs reported that local law enforcement often required evidence of physical coercion to identify trafficking victims, despite Malaysian law recognizing other forms of coercion — including debt-based coercion and passport withholding — as trafficking indicators.  Civil society also noted that frequent rotation of law enforcement personnel hampered the effectiveness of training efforts.

					Protection			

The government slightly increased protection efforts.

During the reporting period, the government identified 132 confirmed trafficking victims — 31 exploited in sex trafficking, 96 exploited in forced labor, and five exploited in unspecified forms of trafficking — among 563 potential victims identified, compared with 160 confirmed victims identified among 635 potential victims in the previous reporting period.  The government had victim identification SOPs for law enforcement to identify victims; however, implementation varied across the country, especially in rural areas and in the eastern states of Sabah and Sarawak.  Officials utilized the National Guidelines on Human Trafficking Indicators (NGHTI 2.0), which included a general rapid-screening checklist, sector-specific screening checklists, and a guide on evidence collection.  The government initiated efforts to develop an updated NRM, including by holding consultative workshops; however, it did not complete or launch the revised mechanism by the end of the reporting period.  The government expanded the number of “victim identification champions” (VICs) — officers trained and designated within enforcement agencies to apply NGHTI 2.0 and train colleagues on victim screening — from 23 to 122 nationwide during the reporting period.

The ATIPSOM Act required the government to place victims who were granted a court-ordered protection order — a legal instrument that granted victims access to shelter, services, and lawful status in Malaysia, issued initially for 21 days for potential victims and extended to 90 days for certified victims — at a place of refuge designated by the Minister of Home Affairs.  The government granted full protection orders to all certified victims in accordance with the law and provided them housing in government-operated or government-funded shelters.  The government provided victims with food, medical care, social and religious activities, security, and a monthly stipend of 120 RM ($29.59) for incidental expenditures.  The law required foreign victims to remain in government shelters for the duration of their protection orders, except for those with a work permit; domestic victims could stay with family members or a guardian.  The government amended two subsidiary regulations under the ATIPSOM Act to improve foreign victims’ access to lawful employment by aligning the duration of their work authorization and temporary legal status in Malaysia, which could be renewed annually for the duration of legal proceedings; additionally, the amendments created a mechanism for officers to apply for extended care orders for foreign victims without proof of citizenship to remain in Malaysia after their court-ordered protection period expired and, where necessary, to arrange repatriation.

The Ministry of Women, Family, and Community Development continued to operate eight government-run trafficking-specific shelters and continued to fund two NGO-operated shelters.  Shortages of shelters for men and children across the country created additional challenges for law enforcement seeking placement for trafficking victims and decreased access to services for these populations.  However, a new NGO-operated shelter for male child victims became operational in January; the government approved, but had not yet disbursed, funding for the shelter.  The government allocated 5.6 million RM ($1,380,177) for victim services, including trafficking-specific shelter operations, protection services, and victim assistance programs, and a disbursement of 830,600 RM ($204,786) to two established NGO-operated shelters, compared with 5.0 million RM ($1,232,297) and 846,160 RM ($208,620), respectively, in the previous reporting period.  The lack of shelters in East Malaysia continued to discourage investigative officers from filing trafficking cases in those regions, and no dedicated trafficking shelter operated in Sarawak.  Due to officials’ inconsistent use of victim identification SOPs, authorities did not effectively prevent the inappropriate penalization of some unidentified trafficking victims solely for unlawful acts committed as a direct result of being trafficked; inconsistent screening also prevented some foreign and domestic trafficking victims from receiving protection services.  NGOs reported the government often did not identify Malaysian victims identified abroad in online scam operations as trafficking victims nor provide them services.  The government previously reported allocating 10 million RM ($2,238,138) to provide assistance to Malaysian nationals overseas, including those trapped overseas in online scam operations.  During the reporting period, the government repatriated 108 Malaysian nationals from overseas online scam operations and identified four as trafficking victims.  Authorities sometimes treated Malaysian forced criminality victims as perpetrators, according to NGO sources.

Victims’ access to services remained contingent upon their cooperation with investigations and prosecutions.  The government provided victim-witness assistance to support victim participation in criminal justice proceedings, including through the victim assistance specialist (VAS) program — trained personnel who provide trauma-informed support, advocate for victims’ needs, and accompany victims through the investigation and prosecution process — and VICs.  The government increased the number of VAS, who drafted reports for investigating offices and advocated for victims’ needs, from nine to 52 during the reporting period, including eight specialists based in Sabah and Sarawak, where there were previously no specialists.  However, the government did not institutionalize the VAS program, and government and NGO representatives who served as specialists did so on a voluntary basis on top of their standard duties.  The government expanded its roster of volunteer interpreters from 51 to 97 for victims participating in investigations or prosecutions.  NGOs reported that officials sometimes assigned interpreters for the incorrect language to foreign victims, undermining the quality of victim engagement.

The government issued restitution for victims as part of criminal sentences; prosecutors requested restitution in 19 trafficking cases involving victims who participated in court proceedings, compared with 13 in the previous reporting period.  The government reported no instances in which victims were unable to obtain court-ordered restitution.

					Prevention			

The government increased prevention efforts.

The Malaysian Anti-Trafficking in Persons and Anti-Smuggling of Migrants (MAPO) Council coordinated interagency anti-trafficking efforts to implement the government’s 2021-2025 anti-trafficking NAP.  The government initiated development of a new NAP but did not finalize the plan by the end of the reporting period.  The government allocated 10.6 million RM ($2.6 million) for its anti-trafficking efforts in 2025, including 4.36 million RM ($1,074,951) for the operation of the MAPO Council, an increase compared with nine million RM ($2.2 million) allocated in 2024.  The government also approved its 2026 anti-trafficking budget in the amount of 11.1 million RM ($2,736,440).  The Human Trafficking Advisory Group, comprising government agencies and civil society, met once; civil society contacts noted this frequency was insufficient to sustain meaningful engagement on anti-trafficking policy implementation.  In March 2026, MAPO Council officials met with state officials to plan the establishment of a MAPO presence in Sabah and Sarawak.  Additionally, the federal and Sarawak state governments reached agreement on a strategic cooperation model to strengthen trafficking case management, victim identification, and law enforcement efforts; however, the model remained under discussion and had not been operationalized by the end of the reporting period.  The government conducted public awareness activities targeting vulnerable populations, as well as outreach to supervisory personnel in plantation and agri-commodity supply chains on ethical recruitment practices and forced labor indicators.  It operated an anti-trafficking hotline and identified one trafficking victim through hotline reports during the reporting period.

The government did not effectively regulate labor recruiters and did not hold them civilly or criminally liable for fraudulent recruitment.  The Private Employment Agency Act (PEAA), which required the licensing of private recruitment agencies with the Ministry of Human Resources to recruit foreign workers, capped employee-paid “placement” or recruitment fees at 25 percent of the first month’s basic wages for Malaysian workers employed inside or outside Malaysia and one month’s basic wages for non-citizens.  The law did not define what constituted a “placement fee,” and authorities did not adequately enforce this rule; most migrant workers paid fees well above permitted levels, which contributed to workers’ vulnerability to debt-based coercion.  The government also mandated employers, rather than workers, pay a levy for foreign workers, a one-time cost paid to the government for any non-Malaysian workers the company hired.  In practice, some employers deducted the levy from worker wages, often without the worker’s knowledge.  The government did not prosecute any employment agencies under PEAA during the reporting period.  In May 2025, the government introduced a policy allowing foreign workers to transfer between employers across sectors, subject to regulatory procedures, though civil society contacts noted the requirement for written consent from all parties might limit the policy’s practical effectiveness.  Malaysia’s labor court outcomes improved during the reporting period.  Labor courts resolved 28,992 labor disputes during the reporting period, ordered employers to pay 70 million RM ($17,252,218) in back wages, and levied 4.3 million RM ($1,060,158) in fines for labor law non-compliance, compared with 21,583 disputes resolved, 52 million RM ($12,815,089) in back wages ordered, and 2.3 million RM ($567,058) in fines in the previous reporting period.

The government took measures to prevent the use of forced labor or child labor, including in supply chains, in violation of international standards; in October 2025, Malaysia signed an agreement on reciprocal trade with the United States that included the obligation to prohibit the importation of goods produced with forced labor.  The government took steps to remediate forced labor indicators at FGV Holdings — a palm oil company in which it owned 70 percent of public shares — including by delivering technical and vocational education and training programs, workers’ rights programs covering ethical recruitment, employer obligations, and forced labor indicators, and trafficking awareness campaigns to FGV Holdings and its subsidiaries.  Although the government conducted labor inspections in various sectors, the lack of adequate resources, including for additional labor inspectors, hindered the government’s efforts to adequately identify labor trafficking and enforce the prohibition on employer-perpetrated passport retention, which remained widespread.  Labor officials did not refer any cases to the AGC for labor trafficking during the reporting period.  The government convicted 15 individuals for unlawful passport retention, compared with 16 in the previous reporting period; the government did not report whether it investigated these individuals for trafficking crimes.  The government made efforts to reduce the demand for commercial sex acts.  The government did not provide specific anti-trafficking training to its troops prior to their deployment as peacekeepers.

					Trafficking Profile			

This section describes the nature and scope of trafficking based on recent government and civil society reporting.  Human traffickers exploit domestic and foreign victims in Malaysia, and traffickers exploit victims from Malaysia abroad.  Traffickers exploit some Malaysian nationals, including children, in sex trafficking and forced labor within the country.  Children are vulnerable to online sexual exploitation, including some instances of child sex trafficking.  Traffickers exploit Malaysian orphans and children from refugee communities in forced begging and forced labor.  Stateless children in Sabah are especially at risk of forced labor in palm oil production, service industries, and forced begging.  The majority of victims are among the migrant worker population.  Foreign workers constitute approximately 20 percent of the Malaysian workforce and typically migrate from Bangladesh, Burma, and Indonesia, among other countries in South and Southeast Asia, and are employed primarily in manufacturing, construction, agriculture, and domestic work.  An estimated 29 percent of domestic workers in Malaysia — primarily foreign domestic workers — reportedly experience conditions consistent with forced labor.  Employers, employment agents, and illegal sub-agents exploit migrants in labor trafficking primarily through debt-based coercion, including through recruitment fees that some workers cannot repay.  Transnational crime organizations are responsible for some instances of trafficking.  Employers utilize practices indicative of forced labor — including passport retention, which remains widespread, restrictions on movement, contract violations, wage fraud, and significant debt imposition — to exploit migrant workers on palm oil and agricultural plantations, at construction sites, in the electronics, garment, and rubber-product industries, and in homes as domestic workers.  A limited pool of government-authorized recruitment agencies — particularly for Bangladeshi workers in Malaysia — foster practices that enable agencies to charge exorbitant fees, with some Bangladeshi workers reportedly paying up to approximately $7,200 before arriving in Malaysia, increasing their risk of debt-based coercion.  According to the Human Rights Commission of Malaysia, approximately eight of every 1,000 palm oil plantation workers are in situations of forced labor, with the prevalence rate considerably higher in Sarawak than in Peninsular Malaysia and Sabah.  The government owns 70 percent of public shares of one of the largest palm oil companies in the world.  Chinese nationals working for Chinese government-affiliated construction projects in Malaysia are vulnerable to forced labor.

Traffickers recruit some young foreign women and girls — mainly from Southeast Asia and other regions — ostensibly for legal work in restaurants, hotels, and beauty salons, or for brokered marriages but instead exploit them in sex trafficking.  Traffickers use false or fraudulent offers to recruit Rohingya asylum-seekers from refugee camps in the region to exploit them in Malaysia, including women and girls coerced into commercial sex.

Traffickers exploit Malaysian nationals — particularly young adults from East Malaysia — in forced criminality in online scam compounds, primarily in Southeast Asia.  Traffickers use social media platforms and online job portals to advertise false employment opportunities, targeting economically vulnerable individuals.  Once abroad, victims are confined and coerced into operating online scams or sold to criminal networks for further exploitation.  Traffickers target Malaysian nationals primarily for their Chinese and English language abilities.  Traffickers also operate online scam centers within Malaysia, where Malaysian and foreign victims are exploited in forced criminality.  Criminal syndicates also exploit Malaysian maritime routes, including the Strait of Malacca, and the Thai land border to facilitate the transit of victims, including to scam centers in Burma.

Corruption related to immigration and foreign worker recruitment processes increases migrant workers’ vulnerability to trafficking.  Corrupt immigration officials facilitate trafficking by accepting bribes from brokers and smugglers at border crossings, and some officials profit from direct involvement in the recruitment fee system that burdens workers before they arrive in Malaysia.
