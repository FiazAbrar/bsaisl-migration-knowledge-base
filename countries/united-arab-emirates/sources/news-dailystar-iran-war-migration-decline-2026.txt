US war on Iran tanks workers’ migration

35% fewer people migrated in 10 major markets this yr so far

              Published : 2 October 2026

•

                Updated : 2 October 2026, 23:11

•

					MIDDLE EAST

							Mohammad Jamil Khan

Mohammad Jamil Khan

Migrant workers waiting at Hazrat Shahjalal International Airport. Photo: Prabir Das / The Daily Star/File

The number of Bangladeshis approved to take up jobs in 10 major overseas markets fell nearly 35 percent in the first nine months of the year on the back of a slump in Saudi-bound recruitment, along with other traditional Gulf markets, due to the US-Israel war on Iran.
A total of 5.19 lakh workers received clearances for jobs in Saudi Arabia, Singapore, Qatar, the Maldives, Kuwait, the UAE, Jordan, Italy, Iraq and Cambodia between January and September, down from 7.95 lakh a year earlier, according to the Bureau of Manpower, Employment and Training’s Country Clearance portal.

Saudi Arabia, by far Bangladesh’s largest labour market, saw clearances drop 45.5 percent to 309,792.
Of the 275,115 fewer clearances from the 10 destinations, 258,291 were accounted for by the drop in Saudi Arabia alone.

 “We have been saying for years that if we depend only on the Middle East and do not create alternatives, we will face a crisis.”

Shariful Hasan, associate director of the Migration Programme and Youth Platform at BRAC

The downward trend was also pronounced in two other major Gulf markets.
Clearances for Qatar fell 37.4 percent to 50,401, while Kuwait recorded a 33.4 percent decline to 22,745.
Singapore, now the second-largest destination among the 10 markets analysed, however, gave 12 percent more clearances: 59,675.
The UAE saw an even sharper rise, with clearances more than doubling to 22,030 from 9,589. Jordan, Italy and Iraq also recorded increases, while the Maldives remained almost unchanged.
Those gains, however, were outweighed by the scale of the decline in Saudi Arabia, Qatar and Kuwait.
Bangladesh had long been vulnerable because of its heavy reliance on the Middle East, said Shariful Hasan, associate director of the Migration Programme and Youth Platform at BRAC.
“We have been saying for years that if we depend only on the Middle East and do not create alternatives, we will face a crisis.”
The US-Israel’s war on Iran had affected opportunities in the region at a time when Bangladesh had yet to develop alternative markets on a comparable scale, he added.
The war has slowed down new investment and development activities in the region, said MS Shekil Chowdhury, chairperson of the Centre for Non-Resident Bangladeshi, a non-profit organisation.
Many Bangladeshi workers in those markets are employed in construction and development projects. When new projects slow, the demand for the largely unskilled workforce Bangladesh traditionally sends to the region also falls.
“Unfortunately, we have not been able to create alternative options. Neither the state nor businesses have been able to develop new markets or sectors on a sufficient scale.”
Efforts to find new destinations need to be intensified, or else a prolonged decline in overseas employment could eventually affect remittance inflows.
The effect might not be immediate because workers already abroad continue to send money home, including from their savings, he added.
Malaysia, another important destination for Bangladeshis, has also remained closed for around two years, further limiting the options available to workers, Shariful said.
Markets such as Japan and Europe offered opportunities, but Bangladesh would have to prepare workers with the skills and language proficiency required by employers there.

Against this backdrop, Expatriates’ Welfare and Overseas Employment Minister Ariful Haque Chowdhury yesterday said simply increasing the number of workers sent overseas would bring little benefit unless they are skilled.
Around 434,000 workers had secured overseas employment over the past seven months despite the war situation in the Middle East, he said during a visit to the expatriate welfare desk at Shah Amanat International Airport in Chattogram.
The government’s Five-Year Strategic Framework, launched in July, has set a target of sending 2 million workers abroad over five years, with greater emphasis on skilled migration.
The challenges involved in making that shift were discussed at a consultation organised on September 28 by OKUP under the GS4S initiative.
At the event, Rehnuma Salam Khan, National Programme Manager at the International Labour Organisation, stressed the need for demand-driven skills development alongside better governance of labour migration.
She also highlighted Bangladesh’s high migration costs, saying less-educated workers often bear a heavier burden.
Speakers at the programme also pointed to a mismatch between skills acquired in Bangladesh and those sought by employers in destination countries, as well as problems with recognition of qualifications.
Training by itself does not guarantee an employment opportunity, while having a certificate does not necessarily mean that a worker’s qualification would be recognised abroad, said Shakirul Islam, chairperson of Ovibashi Karmi Unnayan Programme.
