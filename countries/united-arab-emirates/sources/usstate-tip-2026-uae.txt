			2026 Trafficking in Persons Report: United Arab Emirates

United Arab Emirates (Tier 2)

The Government of the United Arab Emirates (UAE) does not fully meet the minimum standards for the elimination of trafficking but is making significant efforts to do so.  The government demonstrated overall increasing efforts compared with the previous reporting period, therefore, the UAE remained on Tier 2.  These efforts included launching a new AlI-enabled NRM to streamline and increase efficiency in victim identification and referral to care; increasing employer and recruitment agency inspections; and closing unlicensed recruitment agencies.  However, the government did not meet the minimum standards in several key areas.  Officials did not effectively screen for labor trafficking and addressed labor violations administratively instead of criminally, which impeded accountability and weakened deterrence.  Due to inconsistent screening among vulnerable populations, the government did not take effective measures to prevent the inappropriate penalization of potential victims solely for unlawful acts committed as a direct result of being trafficked.  The government did not adequately implement domestic worker protections, and the majority of domestic workers remained excluded from the wage protection system (WPS), rendering them vulnerable to wage theft — a key trafficking indicator — without proper oversight.

					Prosecution			

The government maintained anti-trafficking law enforcement efforts.

Federal Law No. 24 of 2023 criminalized sex and labor trafficking, and prescribed penalties ranging from five years to life imprisonment, and fines of no less than one million Emirati dirhams (AED) ($272,260) and deportation for non-citizens.  These penalties were sufficiently stringent and, with respect to sex trafficking, commensurate with those prescribed for other grave crimes, such as rape.

During the reporting period, the government reported investigating 17 trafficking cases, prosecuting 88 suspects, and convicting 40 traffickers, slight decreases compared with 21 investigations, 118 prosecutions, and 58 convictions during the previous reporting period.  The government prosecuted 86 suspects for sex trafficking and three for forced labor; thirty-seven of the traffickers convicted were implicated in sex trafficking and three in forced labor.  Courts sentenced the majority of convicted traffickers to at least one year imprisonment, plus fines, and, in some cases, deportation.  No Emiratis were included among suspects charged or traffickers sentenced.  In August, Abu Dhabi set up a new labor prosecution office to oversee criminal investigations and claims in cases related to labor and domestic workers, consisting of a senior public prosecutor and assistant prosecutors.

The government maintained specialized anti-trafficking units at emirate-level police headquarters across the country and in public prosecution offices at the federal and local level.  The government did not report any investigations, prosecutions, or convictions of government officials allegedly complicit in trafficking crimes.  The government cooperated with other governments, including the United States, on law enforcement operations.  In September, police in Dubai acted on an INTERPOL red notice and arrested an alleged sex trafficker from Uganda.  The government conducted extensive and increased anti-trafficking training for law enforcement officials, agencies involved in victim identification and referral, labor inspectors, shelter staff, and airport officials, in partnership with NGOs and international organizations.

					Protection			

The government made mixed victim protection efforts.

The government identified and referred 30 victims to care, a decrease compared with 121 victims identified and referred during the previous reporting period.  The 30 victims included four forced labor victims and 26 sex trafficking victims, all of whom were foreign national women and girls.  The government reported providing services, including support for repatriation to their home countries, to all victims.

The government launched a new NRM in July 2025 connecting 11 government entities and with input from the interagency and civil society.  The government integrated AI tools into the NRM that created digital profiles for victims, streamlined identification, and provided government officials real-time recommendations for intake methods at shelters, procedural steps for investigations, and legal proceedings.  The government reported providing training throughout the year to entities that were part of the NRM.

The government provided specialized shelter, health care, counseling, legal services, and repatriation assistance directly and through partnerships with civil society organizations.  The government allocated 1,979,550 AED ($539,093) for victim services through NGOs, as compared to 5 million AED ($1,361,656) during the prior reporting period.  Formal certification as a victim was required to access services.  The government reported it could award restitution during criminal proceedings, while victims could file civil suits to receive compensation.  Officials did not report whether victims sought compensation via civil suits or were awarded restitution during the reporting period.

The government provided victim-witness assistance to support victim participation in criminal justice proceedings through its witness protection program, which gave judicial officials the authority to keep witness’ identities confidential during legal proceedings.  The government reported it was not necessary for victims to cooperate with law enforcement proceedings in order to access services, though sources stated police often directed victims who declined to file police reports to their embassies while directing those who moved forward with filing police reports to shelters.

The government did not report formal policies to provide foreign trafficking victims legal alternatives to their removal to countries where they might face retribution or hardship.  The government previously reported it exempted trafficking victims who overstayed their visas from fines.  The government did not provide permanent or formal temporary residency status to victims; however, it permitted victims to stay in shelters and participate in court proceedings.  The government reported instituting mandatory health insurance coverage for both private sector and domestic workers, requiring employers to provide health insurance as a mandatory prerequisite for the issuance or renewal of residence permits.

Due to inconsistent screening, the government did not effectively prevent the inappropriate penalization of potential victims solely for unlawful acts committed as a direct result of being trafficked.  Although UAE federal law required police to refer all potential trafficking victims to government shelters, in some cases, police only referred individuals whom police formally determined to be trafficking victims, or told potential victims to go to their respective embassies for help.  Some UAE officials indicated lack of understanding of how an individual could become a forced labor victim if they entered the country on a valid visa, and did not recognize forced labor as a significant issue in the country, hindering victim identification efforts.

					Prevention			

The government increased prevention efforts.

The government maintained an interagency task force and appointed five new member agencies to more effectively implement the 2025-2027 NAP.  The government did not report the amount of funding allocated for prevention efforts, including for implementation of the NAP.  The government conducted multiple public awareness activities that reportedly reached 3.3 million workers and 21,000 domestic workers, including in-person workshops attended by 900 individuals.  It operated multiple anti-trafficking hotlines to assist potential trafficking victims, including with information on accessing shelter services and legal assistance.

During 2025, the Ministry of Human Resources and Emiratization (MOHRE) reported conducting 695,000 inspection visits and issuing 40,594 violations against private sector establishments for labor law violations, though labor inspectors did not identify any forced labor victims or report referring any for criminal investigation during the reporting period.  The Ministry of Justice conducted 2,000 labor investigations, and the government shut down 307 social media pages operating unlicensed domestic worker recruitment account during the reporting period.

MOHRE continued to oversee licensing and regulation of both private sector and domestic worker recruitment agencies and held them civilly liable for fraudulent recruitment.  MOHRE could suspend or revoke licenses of recruitment agencies, including in cases of fraud, non-payment of wages, and violations of recruitment regulations and in cases where the agency committed any act that involved trafficking.  The government reported closing 11 unlicensed recruitment agencies and taking administrative action against 23 recruitment agencies including imposing fines and referrals for legal action.  MOHRE and the telecom regulatory authority also shut down 230 social media accounts for engaging in unregulated recruitment of domestic workers.  In June, MOHRE reported it took action against 1,300 licensed, but inactive, establishments for maintaining sponsorships of workers without actually employing them, banning their owners from issuing new worker permits and fining them.  MOHRE established new penalties for “business centers” — organizations that assist individuals in obtaining government services — to allow the government to suspend licenses based on evidence of involvement with human trafficking.

The government continued to require all domestic worker recruitment be done through private agencies with MOHRE licenses; licensed agencies were required to provide training to recruited workers, educate them on their legal rights, resolve employee-employer disputes, provide and prove wage payments, verify worker accommodations for compliance with domestic worker law minimum standards, and guarantee repatriation.  The labor law and domestic worker law prohibited worker-paid recruitment fees.  However, observers reported many workers paid recruitment fees in their home countries and arrived to the UAE in debt, heightening their vulnerability to trafficking.  MOHRE’s labor complaint system continued to register complaints related to illegal worker-paid recruitment fees and employers reducing worker’s salaries to refund employers for recruitment fees paid; the government reported it also used the WPS to monitor unlawful deductions from workers’ salaries, including recruitment fees.  MOHRE inspectors’ guidelines continued to include identifying worker-paid recruitment fees or workers’ salary reductions to pay employers for recruitment fees.  MOHRE mandated licensed agencies cover the cost of employer recruitment fees for domestic workers through an insurance policy with a two-year warranty; this policy aimed at ensuring recruitment fees would not be transferred to domestic workers from employers once they arrived in the UAE.  In circumstances when a domestic worker allegedly demonstrated poor performance or unlawfully terminated (“absconded”) their employment, employers were entitled to a partial or full refund of recruitment fees and any government fees, which the licensed agency would cover; this policy aimed to disincentivize employers from restricting a worker’s freedom to leave their employment, which increased risk of labor trafficking.  From January to December 2025, the government registered 10,646 complaints related to the payment of recruitment fees across the private sector and the domestic work sector.  Of these cases, MOHRE resolved 98 percent and referred two percent to courts.

The government required employers to enroll private sector workers in the WPS enabling verification of wage payment through authorized banking institutions, however domestic workers remained excluded from this requirement.  This exclusion, coupled with the lack of legal provisions requiring inspections of domestic worker accommodations, wage payment, and work hours, increased domestic workers’ vulnerability to trafficking.

The labor law granted private sector workers the right to terminate their employment contract, leave the country, or switch employers at any time without employer approval provided they give between one and three months’ notice.  The law also permitted both employers and workers to terminate contracts during the probationary period and allowed a worker 180 days to find a new job following contract termination, without incurring overstay fines or becoming undocumented, which may have prevented trafficking for such workers.  For both private sector workers and domestic workers, to be able to leave the country or switch employers, workers had to physically possess their passport.  Although the labor law explicitly prohibited employers confiscating worker’s passports, the law did not stipulate penalties specifically for passport confiscation; instead, it stipulated fines between 5,000 AED and one million AED for any violation under the labor law.  The domestic worker law also explicitly prohibited employers confiscating passports and stipulated a 500 AED fine for employers who did so, but it did not criminalize passport confiscation.  Despite the explicit prohibition of passport confiscation for all workers, the absence of specific penalties for private sector workers and low administrative fines for domestic workers did not adequately deter employers from this practice.  From January to December 2025, the government reported 793,612 private sector workers successfully transferred to new employers.  MOHRE continued mechanisms to inform workers of their rights, offering resources in 22 languages.

The government took some measures to prevent the use of forced labor or child labor, including in supply chains.  The government made efforts to reduce the demand for commercial sex acts.

					Trafficking Profile			

This section describes the nature and scope of trafficking based on recent government and civil society reporting.

Human traffickers exploit domestic and foreign victims in the UAE and traffickers exploit victims from the UAE abroad.  Foreigners comprise nearly 90 percent of the UAE’s population.  Lower wage labor, including most manual labor and a significant portion of the service sector, is provided almost entirely by migrant workers.  Employers exploit some of these workers in conditions indicative of labor trafficking, such as passport retention, non-payment of wages, unpaid overtime, restrictions on movement, contract switching, fraudulent recruitment, substandard food, inadequate housing, debt bondage, or a failure to meet other contractual agreements.  Migrants travel willingly to the UAE to work as domestic workers, security guards, drivers, gardeners, massage therapists, beauticians, hotel cleaners, or elsewhere in the service sector, but traffickers subject some of them to labor and sex trafficking after arrival.

Some recruiters hire workers with false promises of large salaries, medical benefits, and accommodations; however, upon arrival in the UAE, workers discover the companies that hired them are fraudulent, leaving them undocumented, unable to obtain legal work, and vulnerable to trafficking.  Recruiters in some source countries work as individual agents rather than for regulated companies, complicating law enforcement and monitoring efforts.  Many source-country labor recruiters charge workers fees in their home countries, causing workers to commence employment in the UAE owing debts in their respective countries of origin, increasing their vulnerability to trafficking through debt-based coercion.  Reports of employers engaging in the practice of contract-switching persist, leading to less desirable and lower paying jobs for migrant workers after arriving in the UAE.

The UAE’s employer-based visa system restricts foreign workers’ ability to leave a position without prior notice.  Despite limited legal measures allowing workers to change sponsors or terminate their employment under certain conditions, some employers continue to exercise unilateral power over foreign workers’ movements, deny laborers working illegally the ability to change employers, restrict permission for them to leave the country, seek unlawful payment to approve a job transfer, and threaten employees with abuse of immigration processes, which heightens their vulnerability to trafficking.  In some cases, employers refuse to return passports to workers until they reimburse the employer for visa or license fees (e.g., for delivery drivers), further exploiting the worker in labor trafficking through debt-based coercion, restricting their ability to seek new employers, and disincentivizing the worker from reporting exploitation.

Traffickers subject some foreign women to sex trafficking.  Most trafficking cases registered in the UAE are classified as sexual exploitation despite significant labor trafficking concerns.  Traffickers exploit foreign national victims in forced labor in online scam operations in the UAE.  Some cases of child sex trafficking involve traffickers forging ages on passports to facilitate undetected entry into the UAE.

Some migrant workers enter the UAE on tourist visas and work for an employer who does not apply to exchange their tourist visa for the required work visa in order for the worker to have legal residency; employers subsequently use the worker’s legal status as coercion and further exploit the worker.

Many UAE households continue to employ domestic workers directly or through private recruitment agencies that were not government regulated, further increasing the vulnerability of domestic workers to trafficking without protection under the law.  Some employers sponsoring domestic workers and unlicensed recruitment agents continue to use online platforms to advertise the “selling” of domestic workers.
