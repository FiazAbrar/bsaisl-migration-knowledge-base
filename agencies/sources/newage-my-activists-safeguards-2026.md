# Malaysian activists urge safeguards before fresh hiring of Bangladeshi workers

Staff Correspondent \

Malaysian rights activists have urged their government to address structural flaws in the migrant worker recruitment system before resuming fresh recruitment from Bangladesh.

They warned that a failure to introduce safeguards could repeat the abuses that have plagued the labour corridor for years.

They identified excessive recruitment fees, multiple intermediaries, fraudulent job syndicates and forced labour as some of the longstanding problems, saying any new recruitment process must ensure that workers do not bear the cost of securing jobs.

The call comes as Malaysia is preparing to resume recruitment of Bangladeshi workers more than two years after it suspended hiring from Bangladesh on June 1, 2024, amid concerns over high migration costs, syndicate-controlled recruitment and exploitation of workers.

Between 2022 and 2024, nearly 500,000 Bangladeshi workers went to Malaysia through a recruitment system involving 101 agencies selected by the Malaysian government. Workers were reportedly charged between Tk 450,000 and Tk 600,000 in recruitment-related fees.

A group of experts appointed by the UN Human Rights Council in 2024 also reported allegations of fraudulent recruitment involving networks in Bangladesh and Malaysia. Workers were allegedly charged excessive fees, pushed into debt bondage and, in some cases, arrived in Malaysia only to find that no jobs were available.

About 800,000 Bangladeshis currently work in Malaysia, making the country one of the largest destinations for Bangladeshi migrant workers.

Bangladesh has been pressing Malaysia to resume recruitment, with efforts intensifying since political changeover in 2024. 

Following a series of negotiations, Malaysia has directly selected 25 Bangladeshi recruiting agencies and allowed another 312 agencies to operate as associate recruitment agencies.

Four of the 25 Bangladeshi recruiting agencies selected for the prospective reopening of the Malaysian labour market are facing an Anti-Corruption Commission investigation over allegations of human trafficking.

Bangladesh Overseas Employment and Services Limited, the country's only state-owned overseas recruitment agency, will also be allowed to participate in the process.

However, details of the new recruitment mechanism, including recruitment fees, worker-protection measures, monitoring arrangements and minimum wages, have yet to be made public.

Against this backdrop, former Malaysian MP Charles Santiago warned that bringing in more Bangladeshi workers without reforming the existing system could perpetuate longstanding abuses, the Free Malaysia Times reported on Sunday.

Santiago, co-chair of ASEAN Parliamentarians for Human Rights, said the proposed structure, in which 25 Bangladeshi recruitment agencies would control access to the Malaysian labour market, appeared similar to arrangements that had caused problems in the past.

Malaysian NGO North-South Initiative Executive Director Adrian Pereira acknowledged Malaysia's demand for migrant workers but said recruitment must be legal, transparent and free from forced labour.

He said civil society lacked sufficient information about how migrant workers were recruited and how quotas were allocated, while there was no independent audit mechanism or ombudsman to oversee the recruitment system.

Pereira said excessive recruitment fees had contributed to debt bondage and that Malaysia still lacked a comprehensive migrant worker policy ensuring that recruitment costs were not passed on to workers.

‘All sides must make sure no worker pays for any job,’ he said.

‘Workers should not be paying for jobs as per ILO standards, supply chain standards, and the 2021 agreement between Malaysia and Bangladesh.’

Pereira also called for proper pre-departure, post-arrival and pre-employment training so that workers understand their contracts, wages and rights before entering potentially exploitative situations.

He urged greater transparency in agreements between employers and recruitment agents, saying workers must have clear information about their jobs and employment conditions before leaving Bangladesh.
