Three women stopped at Dhaka airport: When a social prejudice becomes an immigration decision

The reported barring of three women from a Nepal trip shows how deeply ideas about women, family approval and independence remain embedded in Bangladesh

              Published : 30 September 2026

•

                Updated : 30 September 2026, 13:22

•

					NEWS

							Shamima Akhter

Shamima Akhter

FILE VISUAL: SALMAN SAKIB SHAHRYAR

A High Court rule this week sent me searching for the news behind it. Media reports say three women planned a trip to Nepal — all adults, with valid passports and their documents in order, and with no case or court order barring them from travelling. On August 8, immigration officials at Dhaka airport stopped them. They were reportedly asked why they were unmarried and whether their families had consented. They were then turned back, with no reason recorded.
I read the reports several times because it sounded impossible. Then I had to be honest. Such questions are not new to me. But being stopped at immigration has never happened to me.

I first flew out of Bangladesh at 24, with my husband. Since then, work and adventure have made me a frequent traveller — 22 years with reputed and renowned companies, travelling to many countries for work; eight countries crossed by bicycle; and international climbing expeditions. I remember that in 2017, when I was flying to Indonesia for a group cycling trip, I was asked why a woman would cycle abroad. When travelling alone, I was often asked why I was alone; once, I was asked who looked after my family. Interestingly, this month, when I was travelling to Sydney with my parents, I was praised for being a good daughter. The desk approves of me when I travel as a caregiver, but questions me when I travel for myself. And if the questions still reach a woman with my travel history and experience, what do they do to a woman without them?
Like most women of my generation, I built a tolerance. I have grey hair now; I answer politely and move on. The desk is only the last checkpoint; a woman crosses several before reaching it. My husband first travelled with his friends at 19. I first travelled with my women friends in my 40s. The two decades between were not a lack of desire; they were the quiet tax women pay — convincing ourselves first, arranging the family, absorbing relatives' comments, hearing colleagues say my husband “allows” my adventures, and being told by professionals what a woman my age should not attempt. Each comment costs some peace, and we spend that energy so routinely that we forget.
So what makes August 8 different? This is not about one department or the officers at one counter. The same belief speaks in our drawing rooms and workplaces. What changed was who was holding it. A relative's remark costs peace. A colleague's “allows” costs dignity. An officer's question costs both. But a denial costs a right. At that desk, a private belief became enforcement — a judgement with a stamp in its hand. No law requires a woman to be married, accompanied or approved by her family to board a flight. Yet three women lost their trip and the joy of travelling together because a belief, not a law, stood at the gate. We have learned to ignore bias. But enforcement cannot be ignored.
The three women did not absorb it quietly. They complained formally, sent a legal notice and, when silence followed, went to court. On Tuesday, the court asked the home secretary, police leadership and immigration officials to explain within four weeks why the obstruction should not be declared illegal. The writ rests on two constitutional promises: freedom of movement and equality. The court will decide whether the action was legal. But society has to decide whether the belief behind it is acceptable.
We reassure ourselves that our daughters will not face what we faced — that education, work and women's place in the economy have settled it. On August 8, that reassurance failed. Until that belief is retired, it can meet any woman at that counter.
So I salute those three young women for refusing to stay quiet. I am honestly not sure I would have had that courage. Their courage reminds us of our part: to raise our voices every time the belief appears. My answer to “why are you travelling alone?” has been polite for 20 years. The honest answer was always: because it is my right. If we want a Bangladesh that all of us can be equally proud of, we have to start saying it out loud.
Shamima Akhter is a corporate affairs and communications leader who has served large and reputed organisations in Bangladesh. Views expressed in this article are the author's own.
