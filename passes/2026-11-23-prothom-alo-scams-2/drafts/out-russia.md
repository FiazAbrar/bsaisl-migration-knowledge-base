### When the visa, the agency and the clearance are all real

In the cases below the worker went abroad, or tried to, through a recruiting agency or a broker, in several with BMET clearance [PA0001, PA0017, PA0022, PA0031, MA0040]. Volunteers of several organisations working on migration told Prothom Alo in November 2026 that a visa or job offer through a recruiting agency is not by itself a guarantee of legal and safe migration [RU0191, RU0192].

#### How it works

1. **Malaysia, 2022 to 2024.** Workers went through recruiting agencies picked for the syndicate, and many who reached Malaysia got no job [PA0001, PA0002, PA0003]. UN experts said in a statement published on the website of the UN human rights office (OHCHR) on 19 April 2024 that Bangladeshi workers promised jobs did not get the work after entering Malaysia, and that many were forced to stay on after their visas expired, at risk of arrest, detention, ill-treatment and deportation [MA0022, MA0023]. They said criminal networks in Bangladesh and Malaysia recruited workers to companies that did not exist, and the workers fell into debt paying the networks for it [MA0024]. A survey of 357 migrants in Malaysia by Verite and four other organisations found workers paid BDT 544,000 on average, against a government-fixed cost of BDT 79,000 [PA0004, PA0005, PA0006]. Selim of Narayanganj was put on construction piling work instead of the job promised, was injured, got no treatment from the company and bought his own ticket home [PA0007, PA0008].
2. **The agency on the papers is not the one that sent the worker.** Prothom Alo reported in May 2024 that government records showed a worker going to Malaysia in the name of a syndicate agency even when that agency did not send him; in several written undertakings the paper collected, the agency actually sending the worker, as "sub-agent" or representative, promised to take responsibility if the worker got no job, although its name appeared in no government record of the sending [MA0004, MA0005]. Prothom Alo reported in September 2026 that agencies of three Awami League-era MPs, of one MP's family member, of Awami League leaders and of a city corporation councillor did business inside the 2022-24 Malaysia syndicate, and that these agencies themselves said publicly they had sent no workers: other agencies sent workers in their names [MB0078, MB0079].
3. **Saudi Arabia.** Ali Haider Chowdhury, former secretary general of BAIRA (the recruiting agencies' association), claimed in September 2026 that 70% of workers going to Saudi Arabia go for a short term and that many find no work after arriving; Prothom Alo added that there are complaints of workers not getting work according to their contracts there, and that there is not work at the rate workers are going [RU0072, RU0073].
4. **Russia, a different employer.** According to a BRAC Migration Programme press release, 35 Bangladeshi workers dismissed from their jobs in Russia returned to Dhaka on 18 January 2026, and the workers told BRAC a recruiting agency had sent them in July 2025 with BMET clearance [PA0014, PA0015, PA0016]. They complained that their clearance named the Russian firm Mars International Limited Trade Development but they were placed with a different firm on arrival, and were made to do building construction although the contract was for factory work [PA0017, PA0018, PA0019]. Six of them said going had cost each BDT 700,000 on average [PA0020, PA0021].
5. **Russia, the army.** Research by BRAC's Migration Programme in November 2025 found, as Prothom Alo summarises it, that the deception begins in Bangladesh: the men left with valid visas, job contracts and the required papers, and were sent to the war after being taken to Russia [RU0127]. According to a written complaint that the families of Ali Hasan Sohel of Goalanda, Rajbari, and three other men filed with the Expatriates' Welfare Ministry on 19 May 2026, the men were sent to Russia on 7 May 2026 with BMET clearance after contact with Jabel-e-Noor International Limited, a recruiting agency in Malibagh, Dhaka; Sohel's father said the family had been told he was going to a company job for two years [PA0022, PA0023, PA0024]. The families allege that after arrival a group of 30 was kept in a hotel for three days and then handed over to the army [PA0025]. Rajon Hossain of Jhenaidah, one of the 30, said that on 12 May they were made to sign a contract after being told it was for a bank card, SIM card and work permit, that they could not understand it because it was written in Russian, and that they later learned it was a contract to join the Russian army [PA0048, PA0049]. According to the Special Response Battalion (SRB), a police unit, the 30 went through three firms, Jabale Noor, RS International and TS Group [PA0046].
   - **The embassy had refused.** According to a letter the labour wing of the Bangladesh Embassy in Russia sent to the Expatriates' Welfare Ministry on 25 May 2026, Jabale Noor sought permission on 5 May to send 70 workers and the embassy did not attest it, and RS International was at first told it could bring 10 workers but that was suspended when suspicion arose [RU0096, RU0097]. The letter says the two agencies, unable to get attestation, sent the workers in the name of TS Overseas, and asks the Ministry to check how workers were sent without the embassy's permission [RU0098]. Prothom Alo found that TS Overseas had taken clearance in the names of 266 workers for Russia, and that the names of the other two agencies could not be found in BMET's database [RU0123].
   - Prothom Alo reported that the Expatriates' Welfare Ministry cancelled the licences of Jabale Noor, TS Overseas and RS International (see Russia: recruited into the war) [PA0047].
6. **Russia, the government's own agency.** A responsible official of BOESL, the government recruiting agency, which took the second-highest number of Russia clearances at 771, told Prothom Alo in October 2026 that it had been sending workers to three Russian companies, that after one worker ran off with a vehicle and another became involved in the war two of the companies stopped taking workers, that 14 more people were still due to go to the third on its old demand letter, and that BOESL had received no new demand letter for a year [RU0124, RU0125, RU0126].
7. **Cambodia.** Md Shafiqul Islam, sent to Cambodia in October 2025 on the promise of a computer data-entry job, said he made a BDT 450,000 agreement with the broker Masud on BDT 200 stamp paper on 1 September 2025 [PA0029, PA0030]. He said he attended three days of BMET training at Probashi Kalyan Bhaban in Dhaka and received BMET clearance (the smart card) [PA0031]. He said that on 27 October 2025 he was taken to a walled complex near the Thai border, which he described as a scam centre, and that his phone and passport were taken there [PA0032, PA0033, PA0034]. Tofayel Ahmed, another returnee from a scam centre in Cambodia, told a roundtable at the Prothom Alo office in Dhaka on 18 August 2026 that he went to Cambodia with a BMET smart card and was sold to a scam centre there [AB0089, AB0090]. Dipta Rakshit, Bangladesh country representative of Winrock International, an international development organisation, and director of its Ashshash project, told a roundtable at the Prothom Alo office on 22 July 2026 that information from people who returned from Cambodia shows 80 percent went abroad through the legal migration process [AB0073]. Anirban Survivor Voice Foundation, an organisation led by trafficking survivors, filed an application with the Expatriates' Welfare Ministry on 18 October 2026 and said in a press release that many Bangladeshis who went to Cambodia with valid passports, visas and government clearance were forced to work in online scam compounds; Prothom Alo reported more than 1,800 workers stranded there (see Cambodia, Myanmar and Laos: scam compounds) [PA0038, PA0039, PA0040].
8. **Attested demand letters and clearance.** The state minister for expatriates' welfare told Parliament in June 2026 that nominal companies abroad bring in 100 people where there is work for 10 [PA0041, PA0042, PA0043]. He said that, because the Bangladesh embassy in the destination country attests the demand letters sent from abroad, stricter surveillance, monitoring and verification are needed at embassy level [PA0044, PA0045]. Migration expert Asif Munir told Prothom Alo in September 2026 that responsibility for approving clearance lies with the government, and asked why workers are sent to countries whose appointment letters cannot be verified and how clearance is given for countries like Russia and Cambodia [RU0074]. Md Al-Amin Noyon, president of Anirban Survivors' Voice, told the July 2026 roundtable that in the past year and a half 56,000 Bangladeshis went through BMET to 28 countries where Bangladesh has no embassy or consulate, and that there is no information on how they are or whether they are safe [AB0082]. He said it is also BMET's responsibility to monitor whether workers reached the workplace and whether their pay and housing are right, and that the state could identify, if it wished, how many went on tourist visas and did not return [AB0084]. Shakirul Islam, chairperson of OKUP (Ovibashi Karmi Unnayan Program, a migrant workers' organisation), told the same roundtable that because workers go abroad on BMET's clearance, the state must also take responsibility for that process [AB0056].

#### Red flags

- an employer on arrival that is not the one named on the BMET clearance [PA0017, PA0018];
- an agency other than the one named on the clearance taking the money, with a private written undertaking instead of the government record [MA0004, MA0005];
- a paper to sign in a language the worker cannot read [PA0049, PA0214]; in one case it was described as being for a bank card, SIM card and work permit [PA0049].

#### Check before paying

Migration volunteers said in November 2026 that these all need to be verified: the real job opportunities in the destination country, the employer's identity, the job contract, the pay and allowances, the visa type and Bangladesh government approval [RU0191, RU0192].

> **Need to know**
> - How can a worker check, before paying, whether the Bangladesh embassy's labour wing attested the demand letter behind his visa, and which agency it was attested for?

#### Cases

- On 31 May 2024, the last day for approved workers to enter Malaysia, thousands of people crowded Dhaka's Hazrat Shahjalal International Airport without tickets in a last attempt to go, according to the National Human Rights Commission [MA0040]. Several thousand were turned back from the airport on 1 June 2024; they said they had paid the company BDT 500,000 to 600,000 and still could not go [PA0009, PA0010, PA0011]. Many said the agencies they had paid could no longer be reached by phone, and many who went to the agencies' offices found them shut [PA0012, PA0013]. Tanvir Mia (22) of Akhaura, Brahmanbaria, who could not fly to Malaysia by the deadline, fell from the door of the Upakul Express train into the Meghna at the Bhairab rail bridge on his way home from the airport and went missing, Prothom Alo reported [MA0043, MA0044].
- The wife of Bazlur Rahman Sardar (30) of Jashore said in February 2025 that he had gone back to Malaysia two months earlier after an earlier two-year stay, that police detained more than a hundred people together while he was at work, and that because nobody appeared for the recruiting agency he had gone through, a red seal was put in his passport and he was to be sent home within about a week; Prothom Alo reported he had been in a Malaysian jail for a month and a half after a fault was found in his papers [RU0013, RU0014].
- Md Shafiqul Islam said the Bangladeshi community in Cambodia, approached by his younger brother, paid USD 3,200 to get him released, and that after he came home the broker Masud spoke of compensation, then could not be traced and his office was closed [PA0035, PA0036, PA0037].
- Sohel's father said the agreed BDT 700,000 was paid in full with borrowed money, and Sohel's wife said not one taka of it has come back [PA0026, PA0027, PA0028].

#### Action against agencies and officials

- **April 2024, UN experts:** they said high-level officials of the Bangladesh and Malaysian governments were involved with the networks, that little action had been taken against the firms involved in either country, and that victims who spoke out faced reprisals and harassment [MA0025, MA0026].
- **June 2024, National Human Rights Commission:** taking up the Malaysia situation on its own motion, it ordered on 3 June 2024 that the allegations of extra money taken by fraud, of workers sent to name-only companies and of workers finding no work in Malaysia be investigated and action taken, called for identifying the firms and people responsible for workers with visas and clearance failing to go in time, and gave the Expatriates' Welfare Ministry secretary until 10 July 2024 to report [MA0034, MA0035, MA0036].
- **The Paltan syndicate case:** filed on 3 September (the report gives no year) at Paltan police station, Dhaka, by Altab Khan, owner of a firm named Afia Overseas, on charges of embezzlement through a syndicate and human trafficking, it names 103 accused, among them Masud Uddin Chowdhury, former expatriates' welfare minister Imran Ahmed, former secretary Ahmed Munirus Salehin, Benjir Ahmed, a former member of parliament and owner of Ahmed International, and Md Ruhul Amin Swapan, owner of Catharsis International [OA0150, OA0151].
  - The case alleges that, although there are more than two thousand recruiting agents, the accused formed a syndicate and discriminated among them, took from the complainant by intimidation and force an extra Tk 1.5 lakh per person for 841 people, Tk 12 crore 56 lakh 1 thousand in all, and collected about Tk 24 thousand crore from other businessmen and embezzled it [OA0152, OA0153, OA0154].
  - It alleges that Salehin, while in government service, used his son as a member of the syndicate under his own ministry, and that Imran Ahmed helped the ring by irregularly approving the launch of an app named "Probashi" for his wife's elder brother's son [OA0155, OA0156].
  - On 23 March 2026 Dhaka's detective police (DB) arrested Lt Gen (retd) Masud Uddin Chowdhury, a former member of parliament for Feni-3 and managing director of M International Limited, in Baridhara; on 24 March a Dhaka metropolitan magistrate granted five days' remand, and police said there are 11 cases against him in Feni and Dhaka [OA0147, OA0148, OA0149]. The public prosecutor, Omar Faruq Faruqi, told the court the accused had taken Tk 24 thousand crore in the name of sending people abroad and that remand was needed to learn who shared the money and where it was laundered; the investigating officer sought the recovery of the money and the arrest of absconding accused [OA0157, OA0158, OA0159].
  - He was remanded a second time on 29 March 2026 and a third time, for three days, on 4 April; prosecutors told the court the syndicate traded in commissions from more than two thousand recruiting agencies, which got no work unless they paid, and that every member except him was absconding [OA0160, OA0161, OA0162]. His lawyer, Touhidul Islam, told the court on 4 April that after 11 days of remand the investigating officer's application recorded no progress, that recovering money cannot be a ground for remand, and that his client is over 72 and has had brain surgery and two heart stents [OA0163, OA0164, OA0165].
- **July 2026, licences withdrawn:** on 19 July 2026 the Expatriates' Welfare Ministry withdrew the licences of 49 recruiting agencies, which Prothom Alo described as linked to the Malaysia syndicate, because charge sheets against them under sections 406, 420, 385, 386, 427 and 34 of the Penal Code 1860 had been filed and accepted in court [MB0055, MB0056, MB0057, RU0078]. Expatriates' Welfare Minister Ariful Haque Chowdhury said that month that the licences of three agencies that sent workers to Russia had also been cancelled, and that BMET had been told to be more careful before giving clearance for Cambodia [RU0048].

#### What to do

Taking money on a false job promise is an offence under section 31 of the OEM Act (see File a police case) [S0159]; complain to BMET too (see Complain to BMET) [S0043].

### Recruited while abroad or just back

- **In the Gulf.** Abdullah Al Zobayer, who came back from captivity in Libya in 2024, said the ring that took him has many members and also targets Bangladeshi expatriates in the Middle East, taking them to Libya with talk of good jobs or passage to Europe and holding them hostage to collect money from their families [LB0042]. Afaz Uddin Molla, a worker in Kuwait, said he answered a Facebook advert in August 2024 offering a cheap route to Canada and was promised he would be taken there from Kuwait; according to the case statement, the man calling himself Asadul took BDT 24 lakh from him by December 2024 [FV0088, FV0089, FV0090]. According to his family and local sources, Elias Howlader of Kalkini, Madaripur, went to Qatar about three years before March 2026, decided from there to go to Italy illegally after being lured by a Bangladeshi broker, and reached Libya from Qatar in early August 2025; his father said the family learned of it only after he had gone [PA0197, PA0198, PA0199]. According to the family, brokers took BDT 20 lakh from them in instalments while he was held and tortured in a Libyan detention camp, where he died on 23 March 2026 [PA0200, PA0201, PA0202]. In a case over a promised job in Australia reported in June 2026, the victim was a Bangladeshi working in Oman, approached in December 2025 through a person he already knew [FV0124].
- **In Libya.** One of a group of 113 returnees told the ambassador he had lived in Libya for 14 years before deciding, in old age, to try the "game", the boat crossing to Italy (see Libya to Italy by boat) [LB0202].
- **In Malaysia.** Shariful Islam Hasan, associate director of BRAC's Migration Programme and Youth Platform, told a roundtable at the Prothom Alo office in Dhaka on 22 July 2026 that many people in Malaysia are being cheated with assurances that they will be sent to Australia [AB0077].
- **On the flight home.** According to CID sources, a man from Senbag, Noakhali, who had worked in Saudi Arabia for about three years met a fellow passenger on the flight home, and through him was introduced to a member of a trafficking ring (see A flight to a different country first) [PA0203, PA0204].
- **Back home.** Mannan Soyal, who returned from a scam centre in Cambodia, told the July 2026 roundtable that he went to Saudi Arabia in 2022, stayed three years and decided to go abroad again after coming home, and that a relative from his area then sent him to Cambodia for BDT 5 lakh with talk of a restaurant job (see Cambodia, Myanmar and Laos: scam compounds) [AB0035].
- **In Russia, after the job ends.** Ali Hossain of Mymensingh said in November 2025 that his son Afzal Hossain Meraj, who went to Russia on 10 August 2024 through the travel agency Friendship and Co-operative Recruitment, worked four months at the company he was recruited for, was laid off, and then went to fight through a ring; he was wounded in a mine blast and spent 25 days in hospital [RU0026, RU0027]. An unnamed young man told Prothom Alo that the firm he worked for in Russia put 15 to 20 workers out after two to three months, and that when four or five of them went to the airport in late February 2025 to fly home, a Russian broker took them to a hotel with talk of work [PA0205, PA0206]. He said the broker took their passports and all their papers "for visa renewal", three or four Russians had them sign a contract, and they were then taken to a forest to cut trees, where guns and ammunition arrived some days later [PA0208, PA0209]. Another man, from Trishal, Mymensingh, met a Russian broker while jobless in Moscow for about a month and a half after losing his job [PA0207]. According to his family, after the construction firm employing Shahriar Sumon of Trishal, Mymensingh, stopped work six months in, a broker in Moscow first took him away with a promise of vineyard work and then, promising to send him to Italy, took BDT 5 lakh from him and a Bangladeshi companion; they were made to sign papers in Russian whose meaning they did not understand, and later learned they had been enrolled in the Russian army [PA0210, PA0211, PA0212, PA0213, PA0214]. According to the wife of Kamrul Hasan of Brahmanbaria, after he went to Moscow looking for work and was cheated by one broker, another took BDT 5 lakh from him with a promise to send him to Italy, and he was then handed over to the Russian army (see Russia: recruited into the war) [PA0215, PA0216].
- **In Russian custody.** The UN's Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine says foreigners already in Russia were also taken into the armed forces by deception or coercion: some were in police custody or in temporary detention centres for foreign nationals and some were serving prison sentences, and several were told that signing a military contract would spare them prosecution, a long sentence, solitary confinement or torture, with threats of torture or a longer sentence if they did not sign [RU0171, RU0172].

### Russia: recruited into the war

#### How it is sold

**The jobs offered:**

- construction or welding, through a recruiting agency, at about 75,000 roubles a month [I0273, I0271];
- high-paid work in oil, construction or logistics, according to "Promises Written in Blood", a November 2025 report by BRAC's Migration Programme based on survivors' accounts, family testimony and document checks [RU0015, RU0016];
- work as a cook or cleaner, according to an Associated Press (AP) investigation summarised in a Prothom Alo editorial of 4 February 2026 [RU0037]; Jahangir Alam of Lakshmipur was promised work as a cook in a cantonment in 2025 [RU0043, RU0044];
- security guard, driver, mechanic, electrician, construction worker, welder, repair or transport worker or technician, and sometimes IT, medicine, sport or agriculture, offered through social media adverts or recruiting agents and firms, according to the UN's Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine; some recruiters said the work was under the military but civilian [RU0156, RU0157, RU0158]. The commission describes a professional athlete told he could sign a contract after arriving if he liked the work, who was handed to the military on arrival, and a cook who did not want to be a soldier but ended up on the front line [RU0194, RU0195].

**The promises:** the UN commission says the offers spoke of high pay, Russian citizenship, comfortable housing, a pension after leaving the job and other benefits, and some recruits were shown photos or a catalogue of the housing [RU0159]. Survivors say brokers repeatedly promised it was not military work [I0379]. The commission describes a group of 30 given a letter of assurance that they would not be sent to the battlefield; by the time of its report 17 had been killed on the front line, 5 were missing and 1 had returned wounded; the report does not name the group's country [RU0154, RU0155]. One family was offered medical school in Moscow for their son if he first served four months in the war [I0278].

**On Facebook:** Shariful Hasan of BRAC said in November 2025 that a Bangladeshi using the name MD Sohel was campaigning on Facebook about the benefits of joining the Russian army and fighting, and that many people were being newly lured to Russia to fight because of it [RU0023].

**Going to fight on purpose:** Prothom Alo reported in October 2026 that travel to Russia had not decreased, and that because six months at the war brings a chance of Russian citizenship and pay of Tk 3 to 4 lakh a month, many go to the war of their own accord [RU0117]. Bangladesh's ambassador to Russia, Md Nazrul Islam, said in October 2026 that more than 90% of the workers go to the war of their own accord, and that Russia says the latest 30 also went voluntarily and holds written proof of it [RU0103].

**The cost:** families paid about Tk 9.5 to 10 lakh, about USD 8,000 [I0271, I0273, I0267]. BRAC's November 2025 report puts it at Tk 7 to 8 lakh [RU0017]. The UN commission found that some recruits paid in advance and others were told the money would be deducted from their pay later [RU0162, RU0163].

#### Who sends them

- **Agencies in Bangladesh:** a Prothom Alo investigation found that clearances for Russia were taken in the names of 93 recruiting agencies over four years, that only 17 took clearance for more than a hundred workers each, and that Bonna Bijoy Overseas alone took the most, about 2,500 workers or 27% [RU0118, RU0119, RU0120]. Prothom Alo reported in October 2026 that Bonna Bijoy Overseas is accused of trafficking in the name of sending workers to Algeria, has a human trafficking case against it, faces allegations over Russia too, and has had its server locked so it can take no new clearances; its managing director, Barun Debnath, did not answer the paper's call or text message [RU0121, RU0122]. BOESL, the government's recruiting agency, took the second-highest number of clearances, 771, and one of its workers became involved in the war (see When the visa, the agency and the clearance are all real) [RU0124, RU0125, RU0126]. Shariful Hasan, head of BRAC's Migration Programme, said in October 2026 that the agencies accused of trafficking are sending the most workers to Russia [RU0129, RU0130].
- **The ring:** the February 2026 Prothom Alo editorial, citing the AP investigation, said a Bangladeshi living in Moscow is the trafficking ring's main organiser, that investigators suspect it has links with the Russian government, and named "SP Global" as one of the firms that ran such trafficking openly [RU0041, RU0042]. BRAC's report says international facilitators as well as local brokers are involved [RU0017].
- **Agents abroad:** the UN commission says recruiting agents in the various countries were local, regional or Russian nationals operating as travel or tourism businesses; some held local licences, some presented themselves as legal or government-approved, and in many cases visas issued by the Russian embassy were handed over through them [RU0166, RU0167].

#### The path

1. **A fast departure.** The UN commission found many recruits were pressed to travel within a few days of accepting, with agents arranging the visa, air ticket, housing and the rest of the journey; visas were business visas, short-term tourist visas or none, varying by country [RU0162, RU0163]. It obtained copies of business visas of recruited Bangladeshis that mention an invitation from a Russian civilian company [RU0153].
2. **Dhaka to Dubai, then Moscow**, or through other third countries [I0269, I0268]. Tasneem Siddiqui, executive director of RMMRU (the Refugee and Migratory Movements Research Unit), said in November 2025 that many are first taken to Saudi Arabia on tourist or Umrah visas and later made to sign a contract and forced to the battlefield [RU0030]. Testimony and documents the UN commission obtained name Bahrain, Brazil, Ethiopia, France, Kenya, Mexico, Morocco, Oman, Panama, Spain, Turkey and the UAE as transit countries; agents sometimes travelled with the recruits or met them there, and some recruits were told to go by road to a neighbouring country and fly from there so as not to be caught at the airport [RU0164, RU0165].
3. **At Moscow airport**, brokers take more cash, in one case about USD 1,000 per person [I0270]. The UN commission found many recruits had no freedom of movement from the airport on: they were kept in temporary accommodation and handed to the armed forces [RU0168].
4. **Passports and phones taken**, and the men are handed to the military [I0264, I0134].
5. **Russian-language contracts** they can't read [I0268]. According to the UN commission, within hours or days of arriving recruits were made to sign a one-year military service contract with Russia's Defence Ministry, and could not use translation because their phones were not in their hands [RU0168, RU0169]. Those who refused were pressured, threatened or tortured; one recruit said four people were shot dead in front of him and he was told he would be next, after which he signed [RU0170].

Some go legally on six-month work visas; when the visa ends they are offered a military contract to renew it [I0276]. Workers laid off from Russian jobs have also been recruited there (see Recruited while abroad or just back) [RU0026, RU0027].

#### What happens

- **Training:** the UN commission found initial training lasted from 20 minutes to a few days, in some cases up to a month [RU0173, RU0174, RU0175]. Jahangir Alam of Lakshmipur said he was handed a weapon without training and then taught to use it for one day, that 16 men were together in his war camp, and that he lived in fear after four of them died [RU0043, RU0044]. Khadija Akter said in February 2025 that her husband, Jafar Sardar of Jashore, was kept in a barracks, given an army haircut and war training, and that his last message said "bring me home at any cost" [RU0010].
- **Work at the front:** the UN commission says recruits were made to dig trenches, guard areas, deliver food and water and collect bodies, their main tasks being to carry out assaults and advance [RU0173, RU0174, RU0175]. In the AP investigation, returned workers said they were made to collect bodies and move the wounded, those who refused were tortured, and a Russian commander told one victim he had in fact been "sold" [RU0038, RU0039]. The SRB, the detective branch and others told Prothom Alo that the men sent from Bangladesh have neither the physical fitness, the training nor the tactics for war, and that when the enemy attacks them with drones Russian soldiers identify the enemy's position [RU0131, RU0132].
- **Death:** 30 men flew out on 7 May 2026 [I0273]. In October 2026 the commander of the police's Special Response Battalion said 17 who arrived that May were killed on their first day of deployment after minimal training, 20 Bangladeshis were confirmed dead, and they had been used as bait to locate enemy positions [I0265, I0266]. On 9 July 2026 the Minister had told Parliament that four of the 30 had died [I0314]. A joint investigation by Fortify Rights and Truth Hounds, two rights groups, counted at least 104 Bangladeshis recruited and at least 34 killed [I0275]. AFP reported that Mohammad Yasin Sheikh, 22, was killed on 27 March 2025 fighting for Russian forces [RU0031, RU0032]. According to Jafar Sardar's family, the broker took 10 Bangladeshis to Russia together; one, Akram of Narsingdi, escaped and came home, and the other nine were forced to the battlefield, where one was killed and one was shot [RU0009].
- **Abuse:** survivors were sent to fight with little or no training, beaten, unpaid, kept from leaving and threatened when they tried to escape [I0272]. Recruits told the UN commission of days without food and water, beatings, detention and death threats [RU0173, RU0174, RU0175].
- **Pay:** the UN commission found recruits were taken to open bank accounts, but many were not paid and some got little; money was withdrawn from some accounts without permission, some salaries were withheld or accounts closed, and in some cases recruiting agents or military personnel took the bank cards [RU0176, RU0177, RU0178].
- **Missing:** many recruits lost contact with their families after going to the war, the UN commission found [RU0176, RU0177, RU0178]. BRAC's November 2025 report records that Ayon Mondal of Bagerhat, who went on a valid visa, said in his last message that he was being taken towards the Ukrainian border and has not been traced since, and that Amit Barua of Cumilla has been missing since a photo showed him in Russian military uniform [RU0024, RU0025]. Adnan Hossain, a wounded returnee, said Russia does not publish information on Bangladeshis' deaths and that anyone who does not return from the front line is declared missing [RU0090, RU0091].
- **Bodies not returned:** many families still cannot get their sons' bodies home [I0279]. Adnan Hossain said Russia does not bring back the bodies, and those Prothom Alo spoke to said the bodies of foreigners killed are left on the field [RU0090, RU0091]. The ambassador said the bodies cannot be sent home because the Russian authorities are not handing them over [RU0103].

#### The group of 30

The 30 men who flew from Dhaka on 7 May 2026 went through Jabale Noor International of Khilkhet and RS International of Ashulia, through brokers, according to 28 letters their families submitted to the Expatriates' Welfare Ministry in July 2026; the letters say the men were sold to the Russian army with the lure of high pay (see When the visa, the agency and the clearance are all real) [RU0115, RU0116]. Prothom Alo said nearly all the families are low-income [RU0197, RU0198, RU0199].

**What they paid:**

- Rajon Hossain (21) of Jhenaidah paid a broker Tk 10 lakh for a high-paid job [RU0086, RU0087].
- Pabitra Chandra (30), a carpenter from a very poor family in Gopalpur, Tangail, went with Tk 1.3 lakh from selling his wife's jewellery and Tk 9.5 lakh borrowed from an NGO and local moneylenders [RU0138, RU0139].
- Robin Mir, an autorickshaw driver from Siddhirganj, Narayanganj, sold his village land and cattle and borrowed, and went with three other men from Narayanganj [RU0147].
- Md Palash Sheikh's family spent Tk 10 lakh [RU0113, RU0114].

**What happened, in order:**

1. **The first days.** Robin Mir's nephew, Rahat Chowdhury, said his uncle was given good food and shown around for three or four days, and then there was no news for five or six days [RU0145, RU0146]. Pabitra Chandra told his brother-in-law, Tapan Chandra Sutradhar, that he and several others were kept in a hotel and would be given work once the papers were sorted out; contact then stopped for some time [RU0140, RU0141]. Rajon Hossain got no job and, after four days in a hotel, was sent to an army camp after his signature was taken on a paper [RU0086, RU0087].
2. **15 to 18 May.** On 15 May Robin Mir said they were being taken to work, and on 17 May he was seen in an army camp in a video [RU0145, RU0146]. On 18 May a worker's family phoned the Bangladesh Embassy in Russia to say that 30 people who had come through Jabale Noor and RS International were being sent to the battlefield; according to the embassy's 25 May letter, it was writing to Russia's Foreign Ministry asking that the workers be returned to work under their appointment letters instead [RU0099, RU0100].
3. **Training.** After 21 days of training the men were split into groups of 16, 8 and 6 and sent to the war [RU0084, RU0085].
4. **29 May.** Pabitra Chandra phoned to say "we are being taken to the war by force; the chance of dying there is 95 per cent" [RU0140, RU0141], and afterwards asked the Bangladesh embassy to arrange for him and the others to be brought home [RU0142, RU0143]. Robin Mir phoned in tears to say they had been taken through deep forest on the promise of work and sold [RU0145, RU0146].
5. **June.** Two wounded returnees and six families said the men pleaded with their families before being sent to war, that the agency promised to take responsibility when the families contacted it, and that from the first week of June the men began to go missing; the families got news of deaths but no proof [RU0104]. Many died around 12 and 13 June [RU0084, RU0085]. At its 14 October 2026 press conference the SRB said 17 of the 30 died in a drone attack on their first day on the battlefield and three more died later [RU0136, RU0137]. Rajon Hossain said he was sent to the front line with five other Bangladeshis, four were killed in a drone attack, and he was wounded [RU0086, RU0087]. Abdul Majid of Lalmonirhat said his son Abdullah Al Mamun phoned his younger brother the day before being sent to fight and was not heard from again; a man who went with him, Al Amin, later said Mamun had died [RU0092, RU0093, RU0094].

**Where they were in October 2026:** Prothom Alo reported 20 dead, two wounded and in Russian hospitals (Palash Sheikh and Ali Hasan Sohel), two back in Bangladesh (Rajon Hossain and Adnan Hossain), and six still being made to fight [RU0133, RU0134, RU0135].

- **The dead,** according to the families: Pabitra Chandra, Robin Mir, Sohel, Md Mehedi Hasan, Md Al Mamun, Md Abdullah Al Mamun, Md Nazmul Alam, Md Wasim Akram, Md Arif Hossain, Md Sayed Molla, Tarikul Islam Mintu, Sabbir Hossain, Mofiz, Md Arman Ali, Md Nazrul Islam, Md Aminur Islam, Kazi Rezaul Karim, Main Uddin, Ripon Hossain and Arif Hossain Patwari [RU0197, RU0198, RU0199]. Robin Mir's family learned of his death through the three Narayanganj men he went with [RU0147]. Pabitra Chandra's family heard of his death through his companions and, by 21 October 2026, had not had it confirmed by the embassy or any government channel [RU0142, RU0143]. In Abdullah Al Mamun's village a funeral in absentia was arranged, but because his death was not confirmed the people who came prayed instead of performing the funeral prayer; five months had passed by October 2026 [RU0092, RU0093, RU0094].
- **The six still fighting:** Roni Sheikh and Sourav Molla of Gopalganj, Al Amin Islam of Lalmonirhat, Kamal Hossain and Helal Sheikh of Madaripur and Abu Hanif of Kishoreganj, some of whom keep in contact with their families [RU0200]. Sourav's brother-in-law, Taslim Sardar, who talks to him over the messaging app imo, said Sourav was three to four kilometres behind the front line being made to dig earth for bunkers and had twice tried to escape without success [RU0148]. He said Kamal was digging bunkers with Sourav, Roni Sheikh and Al Amin were carrying food, water and weapons on foot from the camp to the front line, and Hanif and Helal were made sometimes to dig bunkers and sometimes to carry food [RU0149].
- **The wounded:** Md Palash Sheikh was wounded in his first spell at the war, sent back after recovering, and lost a hand; in October 2026 he was in a Moscow hospital, and his cousin Abu Saleh Islam said asking the embassy and the Ministry had brought no help, that he cannot escape because the hospital is under army guard, and that the family has received no money [RU0113, RU0114].
- **The debts:** Tapan Chandra Sutradhar said in October 2026 that lenders stand behind Pabitra Chandra's family for their money when they sit down to eat, and that the family is thinking of selling its remaining 3 decimals of land, which would still not clear the debt [RU0144].

> - Was the group held in the hotel for three days, as the families' complaint says [PA0025], or four, as Rajon Hossain says [RU0086]?

#### The numbers

- **Clearances:** BMET gave 7,449 clearances for Russia between 1 January 2025 and 27 October 2026 [I0274]. BMET data show 37 people took clearance for Russia in 2023, 1,395 in 2024, 4,725 in 2025 and 3,105 in 2026 up to 15 October [RU0112]. According to BMET, 4,727 Bangladeshis went to Russia in 2025, which made it the 13th largest market for Bangladeshi workers that year, ahead of Malaysia, South Korea and Lebanon [RU0061, AB0174, AB0175]. RMMRU says 4,727 went in 2025 against 993 the year before, and that 2,125 took clearance for Russia in the first six months of 2026 [RU0046, RU0047].
- **Families asking for help:** the Bangladesh Embassy in Moscow told AFP in April 2025 that about a dozen families had contacted it to get their sons back, all alleging the sons were deceived into the Russian army [RU0031, RU0032]. By November 2025, 10 families had asked BRAC to bring relatives back from Russia, men who had gone on valid visas to work; BRAC's report says there are more, but the number could not be confirmed [RU0018, RU0019].
- **Deaths:** a Prothom Alo editorial of 4 February 2026 said at least 40 Bangladeshis were believed to have died in the previous few months after being deceived by recruiting agencies into the war [RU0040]. Foreign Ministry sources said in October 2026 that over four to five months the Russian government had told the embassy of 40 Bangladeshis missing or killed, without separating the two, and had said nothing yet about the latest 20 deaths [RU0101]. Prothom Alo had news of at least 54 Bangladeshi deaths in the war over the three years to October 2026 [RU0095]. Shariful Hasan of BRAC said in October 2026 that nobody has a count of how many found work, where the rest are or how many have died [RU0129, RU0130].
- **Worldwide:** a UN commission found men from 29 countries recruited into Russia's forces through deception and false job offers [I0277]. Its draft report, published on the OHCHR website on 26 October 2026, examined recruitment of nationals of 48 countries and found evidence of deception or coercion for 29, Bangladesh among them, but gives no separate figures, names or outcomes for Bangladeshis [RU0151, RU0152]. Its findings rest on 245 testimonies from recruits detained by Ukraine or back home, their families, lawyers, local authorities, researchers and journalists, plus documents such as invitation letters, visas, air tickets and military contracts; the victims were all men aged 18 to 60, mostly from financially weak and marginal groups [RU0183, RU0184].

> **Need to know**

> - For 2024, RMMRU gives 993 Bangladeshis going to Russia and BMET 1,395 clearances [RU0046, RU0112]: does one count departures and the other clearances?

#### The law

- **Bangladesh:** at a Dhaka briefing on 9 February 2025 the Foreign Ministry spokesperson, Mohammad Rafiqul Alam, said some Bangladeshis had been forced into the war after falling into traffickers' hands, that the Embassy in Moscow had been asked for details, that the relevant ministries had been asked to act against the recruiting and travel agencies involved, and that one member of a ring trafficking people to Russia had been arrested [RU0033, RU0034]. He said the airports had been asked to take special caution over Bangladeshis going to Russia on travel visas and those with a valid Russian travel visa in their passports [RU0035]. In June 2026 the government cancelled the licences of three agencies accused of sending the 30 men, and forfeited their deposits [I0158]; on 9 July 2026 the Expatriates' Welfare Minister told Parliament the three licences had been suspended [I0314], and later that month told Prothom Alo they had been cancelled [RU0048]. In October 2026 police arrested two men accused of the trafficking [I0263]. The SRB said Mizanur Rahman, founder of Jabale Noor International and described as one of the ring's leaders, and Abu Taher alias Lentu were arrested on 13 October 2026 over the sending of the 30 men [RU0109, RU0110]. CID sources said in October 2026 that at least six cases had been filed over trafficking to Russia [RU0111]. Most published lists of the 12 countries needing Ministry approval since 4 November 2026 include Russia [I0114, I0115, VI0026].
- **What the Minister says is being done:** Expatriates' Welfare Minister Ariful Haque Chowdhury said in October 2026 that nobody is given clearance without verification and attestation of confirmed job information, that all embassies have been told to keep watch through the expatriate community, that extra caution is being taken over Russia with appointment letters checked much more thoroughly, and that action has begun against the agencies involved [RU0128, RU0185]. Selim Reza, coordinator of North South University's Centre for Migration Studies, said in October 2026 that the government went on giving clearance for Russia even after learning men were being taken to the war by force, and that demand in Russia's labour market had not been properly assessed [RU0186].
- **International:** the UN commission concluded that the Russian authorities committed human trafficking together with recruiting agents; that trafficked men, having become members of the Russian armed forces by signing the contract, cannot be called mercenaries; and that if they surrender or are captured they are entitled to prisoner-of-war protection until the war ends [RU0179, RU0180]. It recommended that Russia stop recruiting foreigners through deception or coercion, discharge and assist the trafficked men in its forces, and prosecute those involved; according to international media citing Russia's UN mission, Russia does not recognise the commission's mandate and says the report rests on false information [RU0181, RU0182].

#### Bodies and compensation

- **From Russia:** Foreign Ministry sources said in October 2026 that compensation can be claimed if someone dies in the war or is missing for more than six months, but that it means a complicated process, including establishing who the heir is and the heir going to Russia and staying at least six months, and that the bodies cannot be recovered [RU0102].
- **From Bangladesh:** the Wage Earners' Welfare Board says it pays to bring home the body of a worker who went with BMET clearance or registered as a member with the embassy abroad, on the family's application, and that a family with neither can still apply, in which case the Board sends a proposal to the Expatriates' Welfare Ministry, which pays from a yearly allocation [RU0105, RU0106]. It gives Tk 35,000 in cash for the burial of each body brought home and then a Tk 3 lakh grant, but by October 2026 no application to bring a body home from Russia had reached it, because with no proof of death nobody can apply, and so nobody is getting any compensation (see If a worker dies abroad, what does the family get?) [RU0107, RU0108].

#### Where to get help

**How men have got home:**

- Afzal Hossain Meraj's father applied to the Foreign Ministry on 22 April 2025, alleging his son had been sold to the Russian army; by November 2025 the son had come home, two months earlier, with the help of the Bangladesh Embassy in Moscow [RU0028, RU0029].
- Jahangir Alam of Lakshmipur fell ill after four men in his war camp died, spent two months in hospital, escaped from there and came home through the Bangladesh embassy, seven months after going [RU0060, RU0045].
- A wounded survivor got home in August 2026 with help from a doctor and the Bangladesh Embassy in Moscow [I0377].
- Adnan Hossain of Narayanganj, admitted to a Moscow hospital with a splinter in his abdomen, had three operations, escaped after two months in hospital, went to the embassy and came home on 15 September 2026 on a travel pass, a valid travel permit [RU0088, RU0089]. Rajon Hossain was wounded, admitted to hospital and escaped from there to come home [RU0086, RU0087].

**Where families have gone:** in February 2025 relatives of nine men sent to the battlefield had formed human chains in Dhaka several times, gone more than once to the Russian embassy and to Bangladesh's Foreign Ministry, and written to the Expatriates' Welfare Ministry, and on 16 February 2025 Jafar Sardar's family wrote to the Chief Adviser's office asking for his return and their money; the report of 17 February 2025 records no result [RU0011, RU0012]. In July 2026 the families of the group of 30 sent 28 letters to the Expatriates' Welfare Ministry asking for their quick and safe return and for financial help [RU0115, RU0116]. Taslim Sardar organised a human chain in Dhaka with the affected families in October 2026 [RU0150].

**BRAC's Migration Programme** has received many applications from families with almost the same account since late 2024; it submits applications to the Foreign Ministry for the men's rescue, gives case documents to the police's Criminal Investigation Department (CID), and gives families legal and psychological support [RU0020, RU0021, RU0022]. It has asked the government to trace the Bangladeshis still in Russia [I0378], and in October 2026 its head, Shariful Hasan, called for a hotline and a list of the missing [RU0129, RU0130].

#### What happens if I'm arrested abroad?

The cases below are from Saudi Arabia, where Bangladeshis are the largest group of foreign workers, at least 25 lakh [OB0064, OB0065, OB0066].

- **Executions:** according to ESOHR (the European Saudi Organisation for Human Rights, based in Berlin), 59 of the 108 people Saudi Arabia executed from January to June 2026 were foreigners, including 49 of the 71 executed for drug offences, and Bangladeshis were among the 108, though how many is not known [OB0059, OB0060, OB0061]. Two Bangladeshis convicted of an "immoral act" in a mosque in Mecca were executed shortly before October 2026 [OB0063]. According to Amnesty International, 597 people were executed in Saudi Arabia for drug offences alone from January 2014 to June 2025, about 75% of them foreigners, in a country whose population of about 3 crore 53 lakh is about 44% foreign [OB0062].
- **Your rights on paper:** Saudi Arabia's 2013 Law of Criminal Procedure gives an accused person the right to legal help during both investigation and trial, and bans torture [OB0067, OB0068, OB0069].
- **In practice:** ESOHR documented that death-row prisoners in Khamis Mushait, Najran and Tabuk prisons had no professional interpreter or legal help, did not understand the proceedings, and often did not know when their sentence was upheld or their plea for pardon rejected [OB0067, OB0068, OB0069]. Amnesty, documenting 25 foreigners convicted of drug offences (from Egypt, Ethiopia, Jordan, Pakistan and Somalia), found many went through the system alone, without understanding or receiving the court papers or charges, without consular help, and at risk of confessions taken without an adequate interpreter or lawyer [OB0070, OB0071]. It found that low education, poor language skills and lack of money harm these migrants twice: brokers cheat or exploit them from the start of going to Saudi Arabia, and if they face legal trouble they cannot hire a good lawyer or defend themselves properly, and in most cases have less access to consular help [OB0077, OB0078, OB0079]. In December 2024 the UN Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination found racial and ethnic discrimination in Saudi Arabia's criminal justice system, with migrant and domestic workers, including women, subjected to arbitrary detention, torture and ill-treatment, and called on Saudi Arabia to impose a moratorium on the death penalty [OB0082, OB0083].
- **What the family is told:** Amnesty says Saudi law has no clear rule on warning the prisoner or family before an execution; families said the prisoner may at most phone 24 hours before, some prisoners were told by prison officers the day before "your turn has come", and many families learned of an execution from other prisoners' calls or from the state news agency [OB0072, OB0073, OB0074].
- **The body:** Saudi law does not guarantee the body is returned after an execution; in all three of Amnesty's 25 cases executed between January and June 2025, Saudi Arabia neither returned the body nor told the family where it was buried [OB0075, OB0076].

ESOHR could learn or record nothing before the execution about 91 of the 108 people executed in early 2026, so there is no answer on how many Bangladeshis are held, on what charges, or whether they had lawyers, interpreters or consular help [OB0064, OB0065, OB0066].

> - How many Bangladeshis are on death row in Saudi Arabia, and for which charges?
