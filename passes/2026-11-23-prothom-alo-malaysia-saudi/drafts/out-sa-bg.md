### Is it open now, and who can go?

Open as of 7 November 2026, but disrupted (see Current situation), and still the largest destination [E0129]. A man can go on a visa for a named job, and usually needs a Saudi skill certificate; a woman can go as a domestic worker, and needs none [SM0036, D0161]. Ages: see Who qualifies.

#### Current situation

As of 7 November 2026: open, but two shocks hit this year, the cancelled visas and the war [E0127, E0130].

**Cancelled visas.** In February 2026 the Saudi labour ministry cancelled thousands of visa applications filed for jobs that didn't exist, and visas that had appeared on the Saudi system disappeared [E0127, B0079].

**The war.** Since the US and Israeli strikes on Iran on 28 February 2026 [SP0635, SP0636]:
- Early March 2026: a drone hit the US embassy in Saudi Arabia; Lokman Hossain, a super-shop worker in Riyadh for a decade, said panic spread and Bangladeshis kept working, but in fear [SP0629, SP0630].
- 8 March 2026: just before iftar an Iranian missile hit a labour camp of the Al Toaik Baladiya company in Al Kharj, killing Bachchu Mia of Kishoreganj and Mosharraf Hossain of Tangail and injuring several Bangladeshis; Abdullah Al Mamun (35) of Gaffargaon, Mymensingh, seriously injured there, was declared dead on life support on 17 March, his father said [SP0641, SP0642, SP0643].
- Mid-March 2026: as of 14 March two Bangladeshis had died in Saudi Arabia in the war, according to Prothom Alo [SP0639]. Two Bangladeshis in Makkah and Jeddah said the early panic had largely eased and people in Makkah, Madinah and Jeddah were going to work, while, citing acquaintances, the east, especially the Gulf coast, was tense and Riyadh on extra alert [SP0650, SP0651].
- 6 April 2026: Expatriates' Welfare Minister Ariful Haque Chowdhury said seven deaths of Bangladeshis from the Middle East war had been reported, four bodies had reached Bangladesh and one had been buried in Saudi Arabia with the family's consent [SP0648].
- 13 May 2026: RMMRU (the Refugee and Migratory Movements Research Unit, a Dhaka migration research group) said at least 9 workers had died in the Gulf and Middle East because of the war [SP0680, SP0681].
- 9 August 2026: Yemen's Houthis attacked an Aramco (Saudi state oil company) facility in the Jazan area, two days after Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan signed a joint defence pact in Makkah, international media reported [SP0757, SP0758].
- 4 October 2026: a Houthi missile or drone hit an Aramco facility at Al Khurais in the Eastern Province and killed Md Sajib Hossain (26) of Brahmanpara, Cumilla; the Foreign Ministry said the embassy had contacted his family and employer and was working with the Saudi authorities to send his body home [SP0779, SP0780, SP0781].
- 10 October 2026: at 3:20 pm a Houthi missile hit Terminal 3 of King Khalid International Airport, Riyadh, where passengers from Bangladesh and other countries were waiting, killing two Bangladeshis, the Expatriates' Welfare Ministry said [SP0794, SP0795]. They were Md Shahed Ahammed, a plumber with a valid iqama on a contractor's Jeddah project, returning via Riyadh from leave in Bangladesh, and Md Nasrullah Badarpuri of Nangalkot, Cumilla; the ministries put the Bangladeshi injured at 24 [SP0524, SP0525, SP0526]. The embassy's labour wing counted 13 dead of various nationalities, including the two Bangladeshis, and 309 injured, including 27 Bangladeshis (11 workers and 16 on Umrah visas), 15 of them in hospital [SP0810, SP0811, SP0812]; Saudi figures reported by Al Jazeera gave 12 dead and over 300 injured, and one dead and 11 injured in another attack on a Saudi airport on 11 October [SP0522, SP0523]. About 500 Bangladeshi passengers were stranded at Riyadh airport; the embassy, with the airport authority, Saudi immigration and the airlines, arranged hotel rooms, food and onward travel: a special bus to Jeddah for 67 Umrah pilgrims in ihram, flights home via King Fahd airport in Dammam for about 400 passengers of the cancelled Saudia flight SV804, and the Al Khobar land border for two Bahrain-bound workers [SP0801, SP0802, SP0803].
- 12 October 2026: the Bangladesh embassy in Riyadh asked Bangladeshis to stay alert, follow Saudi authorities' instructions and avoid risky and crowded places; flights were halted that day at two Saudi airports including King Khalid, and the US, Canada, the UK, South Korea, Japan, Australia and New Zealand issued travel advisories for Saudi Arabia [SP0807, SP0808, SP0809].
- 21 October 2026: an attack on King Fahd International Airport, Dammam, at about 9 pm killed at least two people and injured eight, including two Bangladeshis, Sajal Chandra Das of Cumilla and Mohammad Abdur Rahim of Noakhali, who lost consciousness from heat and smoke and were treated at the Saudi German Hospital in Dammam, where an embassy representative visited them, the Expatriates' Welfare Ministry said [SP0520, SP0521].
- 23 October 2026: a state minister said the bodies of 11 Bangladeshis killed in the Middle East conflict had been brought home in the previous six months, and that three Bangladeshis had been killed by missile attacks in Saudi Arabia in the past month [SP0538, SP0539, SP0540].

**Posting about the war.** In the first week of March 2026 the embassy in Riyadh asked Bangladeshis, out of respect for Saudi law, not to upload photos or videos of the war to social media, or post, share, like or comment on war news [SP0649]. Its urgent notice of 12 October 2026 said recording, exchanging or posting on social media any information, photo or video about the Middle East situation is a punishable offence under Saudi law, and asked Bangladeshis not to post such material on Facebook, YouTube, X or other platforms, nor keep it on their phones or share it [SP0804, SP0805, SP0806].

**Flights.** The Iran war and Houthi attacks on Saudi airports have cancelled over 1,000 flights since February; Biman flies Dhaka-Dammam daily from 7 November, Dhaka-Riyadh stays suspended, and Riyadh-bound workers go via Jeddah or Madinah [E0130, SH0007, SH0008, SH0009]. All flights from Riyadh have been suspended since 10 October 2026, a letter from the labour counsellor said, so the bodies of the Riyadh factory fire's victims went by road to King Abdulaziz airport in Jeddah and home on Biman (see The Riyadh factory fire) [SP0591]. On 12 October the Civil Aviation Authority of Bangladesh said restrictions at Saudi airports had hit 61 direct and transit flights between Bangladesh and Saudi Arabia, affecting 8,381 passengers due to fly from Dhaka; Biman suspended all Dhaka-Riyadh flights until 17 October, US-Bangla cancelled its Dhaka-Riyadh flights until 21 October, and some Dhaka-Jeddah flights were cancelled [SP0813, SP0814, SP0815]. On 19 October the Civil Aviation Minister said Riyadh flights were suspended by decision of the Saudi authorities and would restart once they gave permission, and that some passengers were being carried via Dammam or Madinah instead [SP0485, SP0486]. By 26 October the attacks had disrupted 144 direct and transit flights between Bangladesh and Saudi Arabia, the expatriates' ministry said, so many workers could not get back from leave on time and their iqamas could expire [SP0561].

**Stranded workers' visas.** Workers holding visas have been stranded in Bangladesh by the flight cuts, and agencies have rushed to fly them before the visas expire (see When to pay, when to fly, and Hajj-season pauses) [E0130, SH0009]. On 9 March 2026 the expatriates' ministry opened a round-the-clock hotline and control room for migrants affected by the war, and asked the foreign ministry to seek visa extensions for affected and stranded workers [SP0638]. In October State Minister Md Nurul Haque said visa validity had been extended automatically at the start of the war, and that the government would hold talks to arrange travel for stranded workers; Ali Haider Chowdhury, a former BAIRA (Bangladesh Association of International Recruiting Agencies) secretary general, said stranded workers' visas had been renewed during the Kuwait war and the Covid pandemic [SP0818, SP0819]. In July 2024, when all Saudi Airlines flights were cancelled on 19 and 20 July during Bangladesh's unrest, the ministry said it had discussed with the Saudi embassy extending by 15 days the visas of workers expiring in the meantime [SP0095, SP0096, SP0097]. In October 2026 Bangladesh was discussing with Saudi Arabia extending or renewing the stranded workers' visas, and on 23 October a state minister said the talks were under way [SG0036, SP0538, SP0539, SP0540]. On 26 October Minister Ariful Haque Chowdhury said Saudi-based workers stranded in Bangladesh whose iqama has expired will be allowed in: when they can travel, they will be included automatically in the Saudi system and given permission to enter [SP0559, SP0560].

**Recruitment.** From 1 to 10 March 2026, 21,122 people took emigration clearance, against 46,744 in the same days of 2025, BMET data show [SP0635, SP0636]. Fakhrul Islam, a former BAIRA joint secretary general, said in March that some workers were still going to Saudi Arabia because flights to Jeddah and Riyadh were running, but fewer; some were afraid to take visas, and those who had not done their medical were not taking clearance [SP0637]. In May RMMRU said March clearances were 50% below March 2025, and quoted a packaging worker at an app-based delivery company's Saudi warehouse who said it had gone from over 400 workers to 65, the rest laid off as sales fell; it said Gulf workers faced heavy wage cuts and higher living costs and airfares [SP0680, SP0681, SP0677, SP0678, SP0679]. In 2026 about 1,200 new workers a day went to Saudi Arabia and 600 to 700 a day returned after leave, BAIRA members and BMET sources said, so by that reckoning three days of flight disruption in October stranded about 5,500 workers [SP0816, SP0817]. In October Md Nurul Haque said the Saudi situation meant the target of sending 15 lakh workers in the government's first year would probably not be met, and that work on alternative labour markets continued [SP0827]. The year's departures: see Size.

**Outlook.** In a July 2026 column, a North South University migration researcher cited an ILO (International Labour Organization) 2026 forecast that if the US-Iran war drags on, working hours in the Arab region could fall by up to 3.7%, or 10.2% if things get worse, and that migrant workers would bear the biggest burden, with early signs of less new hiring and weaker remittances [SP0707, SP0708, SP0709]. In an October 2026 Prothom Alo column, Professor A K M Ahsan Ullah of Universiti Brunei Darussalam wrote that if the Houthi-Saudi conflict is prolonged, new hiring may fall, construction projects may slow and wages and overtime may become uncertain, while demand may hold in repair, health care, transport and emergency supply [SP0584, SP0585].

#### When to pay, when to fly, and Hajj-season pauses
- When to pay: pay the bulk only after the visa is issued and you hold BMET clearance; BMET's permission letters allow the agency's fee only after visa, clearance and flight are confirmed [A0016].
- When to fly: fly before the entry visa expires; an expired Saudi work visa is very hard to renew [SH0010]. The medical runs out too: in October 2025 Sarowar Hossain Rabbi (27) of Hossainpur, Kishoreganj, said he had been unable to go for three or four months for lack of Tk 1 lakh, that his visa and medical would both expire on 30 October, and that his visa had expired twice before [SP0428, SP0429]. On 23 February 2026 Mehedi Hasan (25) of Brahmanpara, Cumilla, left for Saudi Arabia without burying his 14-year-old brother, who died that morning when the microbus taking him to Dhaka airport crashed, because, his uncle said, his visa had no time left [SP0626, SP0627, SP0628].
- Hajj-season pauses: in 2025 block work visas on Qiwa paused for Bangladeshis through the Hajj season until about early July, and visit and tourist visas from 13 April [SB0043, E0123].

#### Channels

A visa reaches a worker through a recruiting agency, through a relative or friend working in Saudi Arabia, or as a free visa; across all destinations, IOM's (the International Organization for Migration's) 2019-2020 survey of returnees found 42.3% went through recruiting agencies, 33.2% on a visa relatives procured and 19.5% on a free visa [RD0021]. From Cumilla and other districts about 80% go on independent visas from relatives and friends, the rest on company visas (2024) [A0078]. In a 2022 survey of Gulf returnees by OKUP (Ovibashi Karmi Unnayan Program, a migrant workers' organisation), 554 workers (51%) had knowingly gone on a free visa: 54% of them through illegal broker networks, 7% directly through a recruiting agency, 8% through a family member and 31% through distant relatives [SP0434].

> **Need to know**
> - Of workers going to Saudi Arabia in 2025-2026, what share got their visa through an agency, through a relative or friend, or as a free visa?

The channels:
- Recruiting agencies and resold visas: small Saudi firms sell demand letters to Bangladeshi brokers, Bangladeshis working in Saudi Arabia resell them, and Bangladeshi agencies, which often can't get job orders from employers directly, buy visas from Saudi agents and resell them down the chain (2020, 2024) [RD0005, RD0004, SB0012, SI0028].
- Relatives and friends working in Saudi Arabia [A0078]. Migration experts said in February 2024 that in the Middle East it is workers already abroad who collect job demand and send it to acquaintances and relatives at home, and that about 90% of demand letters reach Bangladesh this way [SP0025, SP0026]. In June 2025 sector people said a class of brokers has grown up around this route who pass off fake appointment letters, so some workers reach Saudi Arabia, find no work and are cheated [SP0368].
- Women domestic workers: through ministry-approved agencies and Musaned, the Saudi domestic-worker platform (see Work visa: domestic worker) [D0060, D0157].
- Health workers: under a 2022 agreement between the two countries on recruiting Bangladeshi health workers, the first group of about 60 went in November 2023 [SP0016, SP0017]. The Saudi ambassador in Dhaka said in February 2024 that Saudi Arabia had started hiring Bangladeshi health workers because they now met Saudi standards, and that a Saudi health ministry team had visited Bangladesh twice in 2023 and would keep coming to recruit [SP0018, SP0019]. Saudi authorities asked for more than 150 trained nurses; in February 2024 the expatriates' ministry was examining questions such as whether they would go to government or private hospitals, and drawing up a procedure for recruiting health workers [SP0020, SP0021]. A nursing teaching assistant at Bangladesh Medical University wrote in September 2025 that nurses can get work in Saudi Arabia and the Middle East by taking the Prometric exam (the licensing test the testing company Prometric runs), and that demand for male nurses abroad is rising [SP0405, SP0406, SP0407].

Whichever channel the visa comes through, a Bangladeshi agency the Saudi embassy has approved must carry the papers [SI0007]. Under the agreement on recruiting general workers that Bangladesh and Saudi Arabia signed on 6 October 2025, that is a private agency, not BOESL (Bangladesh Overseas Employment and Services Limited, the state agency) [SB0001, SB0003]. There is no public way to check embassy approval (see The embassy's approved list); how to check the agency and get proof it holds the visa: see If a relative or friend offers you a visa, item 2.

BMET counts visas by form, not channel: a single visa (সিঙ্গেল ভিসা) is one sponsor's visa for one or a few workers, and may come from a relative or from an agency; a group visa (গ্রুপ ভিসা) is 25 or more workers for one company [SH0006]. By BMET figures, 21.36 lakh workers went on single visas against 90,000 on group visas in the five years to 2024 [RD0015]. BAIRA members said in March 2025 that 85% of workers going to Saudi Arabia go on small demand letters for 1 to 24 workers [SP0251, SP0258, SP0259].

#### Who the sponsor is

The sponsor type on your visa decides your rights: on a household visa you are a domestic worker in law, outside the Labour Law, even if you were promised a company job, and you may work only for that household [C0131, SK0001]. The Bangladesh embassy in Riyadh sorts sponsors three ways:
- Company (শারিকা, sharika) [SM0044]: on a company visa the company often provides food and lodging [SH0026].
- Establishment (মুয়াসসাসা, muassasa), such as a shop, workshop or small contracting firm [SM0044].
- Household, as private driver (সায়েক খাস, sayeq khas) or household worker (আমেল মানজিলি, amel manzil) [SM0043]. Each Saudi household can recruit eight workers as drivers, gardeners or cooks, and in 2020 these household visas were being used for free visas [SH0024].

A visa also names a profession (মিহনা, the job title on the visa): a man whose visa says farm worker but who works in a coffee shop or on a building site is fined if caught, and the kafil can deny knowing [SM0015]. Changing the profession is the employer's cost [C0020].

#### Who qualifies

Men must be 21 to 60, women going as domestic workers 25 to 45 [SH0001, D0039]. Most men going for labouring jobs need the skill test, the Skills Verification Program (SVP, run by Takamol): it is compulsory only for trades Takamol has listed, 108 by late 2026, but the list includes street, hospital, office and roof cleaners, load-and-unload workers and construction workers; domestic workers don't need it [SM0036, SM0037, SM0038, D0161]. The list started at 29 occupations when BMET and Takamol for Business Services signed the agreement to run the SVP in Bangladesh on 27 September 2022 [SP0003, SP0004]. In December 2024 tests were held in Bangladesh in 33 occupations, and the embassy's chargé d'affaires urged workers to train in a trade at a Technical Training Centre (TTC, the government's trade schools) and come with a skills certificate [SP0160, SP0161]. On 30 January 2025 Bangladesh invited Saudi authorities to inspect its TTCs and consider recognising the skills of some, and Saudi Arabia agreed to a possible evaluation [SP0216]. How to take the test: Work visa: company worker, Step 3 [A0058]. The Saudi embassy in Dhaka's 2019 rules also set higher minimum ages for some trades (industrial tailor 30, barber 35, garment shop worker 40), and drivers need a valid licence [SH0001, SH0003, SH0005]. Saudisation (reserving jobs for Saudis) closes some trades: housing supervisor became Saudi-only in 2026, and firms must give Saudis 60% of sales and marketing jobs [E0124, E0125].

In 2025, 755,194 Bangladeshis went to Saudi Arabia for work; Qatar came next with 107,598 [SB0017]. From January to 17 October 2026, 315,935 went, 45% fewer than a year before [E0129].

- Fiscal 2022-23: nearly 4.5 lakh of the 11,25,833 workers who went abroad, the most to any country, by the expatriates' welfare ministry's count [SP0068].

Saudi Arabia took 60% of the year's departures by Prothom Alo's count in December 2024, and more than 80% in October and November [SP0146, SP0147]; Ami Probashi, a digital migration platform, put it at 62% of 1,009,146 in its 2024 annual report [SP0221, SP0222].

#### Where demand is growing and shrinking

What officials and the sector have said, oldest first:
- January 2024: the Bangladesh embassy in Riyadh said Saudi Arabia's selection to host World Expo 2030 and the 2034 FIFA World Cup had opened wide job opportunities for skilled workers in construction, cleaning, maintenance, and tourism and hospitality [SP0006].
- December 2024: sector people said Saudi demand for Bangladeshi workers was coming regularly because Saudi Arabia had begun preparing football infrastructure for the 2034 World Cup [SP0148]. The embassy's chargé d'affaires named the 2027 AFC Asian Cup, the 2029 Asian Winter Games, World Expo 2030, the 2034 World Cup and the giga projects NEOM, Red Sea, Qiddiya, Green Riyadh, Amaala, Diriyah and Roshn as great prospects for Bangladeshi workers [SP0162, SP0163].
- January 2025: Saudi recruitment companies told Asif Nazrul, the interim government's expatriates' welfare adviser, that they recognise skilled Bangladeshi professionals but face a lack of information, marketing and networking, slow visa processing, language barriers and a lack of pre-boarding training [SP0207].
- February 2025: Bangladesh's ambassador to Saudi Arabia, Mohammad Delwar Hossain, said at a Jeddah consulate seminar that bringing skilled people was the mission's number one manpower priority, and that talks across Saudi Arabia showed skills were an important obstacle [SP0232, SP0233].
- June 2025: the expatriates' ministry said construction projects for the 2034 World Cup were nearing completion and cutting labour demand [SP0371, SP0372, SP0373].
- August 2025: Prothom Alo reported that Saudi demand for unskilled workers had fallen [SP0396].
- February 2026: the Saudi ambassador said ongoing development work meant more jobs for skilled and semi-skilled workers [SP0624, SP0625].
- July 2026: a North South University researcher's column said demand across the Arab world is rising for industrial automation technicians, advanced welders, electrical and mechanical technicians, health workers, renewable energy specialists, logistics operators, IT-skilled workers and digital maintenance specialists, that repetitive, easily replaced jobs will come under pressure from automation, robotics, AI and digital management, and that demand for large numbers of construction workers has fallen as big infrastructure projects mature [SP0710, SP0711, SP0712].
- 10 October 2026: State Minister for Foreign Affairs Humayun Kabir said Bangladesh was in positive talks with Saudi Arabia on sending skilled and professional workers, and had expressed interest in joining the Makkah defence agreement [SP0792, SP0793].

#### What has closed it before

The market can shut suddenly: Saudi Arabia stopped hiring Bangladeshi men from 2008 to 2016 [SB0031, E0126]. In 2025 recruitment stalled three times [SH0021, SH0016, SB0043]:
- **The embassy attestation rule:** from 22 December 2024 the government required the Bangladesh embassy's attestation of any Saudi demand letter for more than one worker (see The Bangladesh embassy's attestation, and the missing contract) [SP0247, SP0248, SP0249, SP0254, SP0255]. It was strictly enforced from late January 2025, and after agency protests single visas issued up to 28 February were cleared without it [SH0021]. Departures, more than 80,000 a month on average from October 2024, fell by half in February 2025, which BAIRA members blamed on attestation delays [SP0256, SP0257]: 44,229 in September 2024, 83,582 in October and about the same in the next two months, 76,618 in January 2025 and 44,258 in February, by BMET figures [SP0250].
- **The skill test** from mid-2025 [SH0016].
- **The Hajj season** until about early July (see When to pay, when to fly, and Hajj-season pauses) [SB0043]. In June 2025 Ali Haider Chowdhury said Saudi visas had been closed for a month, new demand had also fallen, and Saudi Arabia might add screening in an announcement expected in July [SP0367]. The expatriates' ministry said on 24 June 2025 that recruitment had slowed because of complications in the visa process, vaccine-related restrictions and changes in Saudi labour policy, and that Saudi Arabia had temporarily suspended visa processing for Bangladeshi workers in June 2024, as it has done before, as it seeks under Vision 2030 a more technology-based, localised workforce that prioritises women workers [SP0371, SP0372, SP0373].

Bangladesh itself has twice banned women from going abroad and twice lifted the ban, Syed Saiful Haque, chairperson of the WARBE Development Foundation (a returnee migrants' organisation) and a returnee from about 10 years in Saudi Arabia, said in December 2025 [SP0467].

#### Talks between the two governments

Recruitment to Saudi Arabia began informally in 1976 with no agreement on general workers; before 2025 the two countries signed only an agreement on domestic workers (2015) and one on skill verification (2022), the expatriates' ministry said in October 2025 [SP0408]. What has been raised since, oldest first:
- 1 July 2024: the two foreign ministers discussed in Riyadh forming a joint task force to bring more transparency to sending workers and stop harassment of workers by vested interests [SP0086]. On 15 July Foreign Minister Hasan Mahmud said he had discussed the task force with the Saudi ambassador in Dhaka [SP0094].
- June 2025: the expatriates' ministry said a government-level memorandum of understanding was at the final stage of signing, and that sending more skilled and unskilled workers had been discussed [SP0374, SP0375].
- August 2025: Asif Nazrul said the agreement then being signed was the first ever with Saudi Arabia on migrant workers, would increase workers' safety and protection, came after the previous government had tried many times and failed, and was one India and Pakistan do not have [SP0384, SP0385, SP0386]. Prothom Alo reported that a joint task force had been formed to resolve the problems of Bangladeshis in Saudi Arabia [SP0388].
- 6 October 2025: at the meeting before the signing in Riyadh, Asif Nazrul asked that employment contracts be correct, that employers carry out their duty to renew the iqama, and that workers wanting to go home get an exit visa quickly; Saudi Minister of Human Resources Ahmed bin Sulaiman Al-Rajhi told officials to take effective steps on these [SP0409, SP0410, SP0411].
- March 2026: Prothom Alo reported that seven agreements to expand the labour market had been signed with Italy, South Korea, Japan and Saudi Arabia under the interim government [SP0640].

The January and May 2025 talks on contracts are under The Bangladesh embassy's attestation, and the missing contract, and those on iqama fees under The iqama: the employer's bill [SP0211, SP0326, SP0208].

### How a job reaches you, and who takes a cut

A Saudi sponsor's work visa is sold on, and Bangladeshi agencies, sub-agents and brokers each add a commission until the worker pays three or four times the original price (see The root: a visa the sponsor owns) [SB0006, SB0007]. The choke point is the Saudi embassy in Dhaka, which decides which agencies may file Saudi work visas and doesn't publish the list (see The embassy's approved list) [SI0007, SI0008].

#### The root: a visa the sponsor owns

An employer issues work visas itself, instantly, on Qiwa, the Saudi labour ministry's platform, if its Nitaqat rating (the Saudisation rating) is at least "medium green" and its government account has enough credit [SI0001, SI0002]. Each visa costs it about SR 2,000 (Tk 66,000) [SB0006]. Sponsors take out two or three permits in their own name for workers they don't need and sell each for Tk 1-1.5 lakh; by the time a permit leaves Saudi Arabia it is worth about Tk 2 lakh, a Bangladeshi labour supplier in Saudi Arabia said in October 2026 [SI0036, SI0037, SB0007]. Large orders have been divided among Bangladeshi recruiters: Arifur Rahman, a physician now chairing Premier Bank, told Prothom Alo in August 2025 that years ago Al Khodari, the contractor holding the Saudi government contract to clean Dammam, needed 2,700 people from Bangladesh and later divided the job among several people, and that a later job for 4,000 people was shared among several businessmen who approached him [SP0399, SP0400, SP0401].

#### From okala to e-visa: the Saudi side, then Tasheer in Dhaka

On the Saudi visa platform the employer delegates the visa to a recruitment office [A0076]; agencies call this the okala (ওকালা, from the Arabic wakala, authorisation), and an okala visa sits in the agency's name, not a worker's [SI0004]. It becomes a named worker's visa when the agency files that worker against it; agents call this application the MOFA (মোফা, after the Saudi foreign ministry), and it shows the sponsor's name and an application number [SI0006]. So a visa is not yours until the agency files a MOFA in your name, and you can check which it is (see Work visa: company worker, Step 7) [SI0004, SI0006].

Once the MOFA is filed, the Bangladesh end starts: Tasheer, the visa centre in Dhaka, takes the fingerprints and fills in the form on Enjaz, the Saudi foreign ministry's visa platform [SI0019, SG0043]. Since May 2023 Bangladeshis get Saudi work visas as e-visas, with no sticker in the passport [SM0001].

#### The embassy's approved list

The Saudi embassy in Dhaka decides which of the 2,807 licensed agencies may file Saudi work visas, and doesn't publish the list: in September 2025 BMET wrote that it did not know which agencies those are [SI0009, SI0007, SI0008]. An agent's "embassy contact" proves nothing: in March 2023 Nazaha, Saudi Arabia's anti-corruption authority, arrested the Saudi embassy's own head of consular affairs and his deputy for taking money to issue work visas [SB0036, SB0044]. The agencies with the most Saudi job orders, and the allegations against some, are under Work visa: company worker, Step 1, item 4 [SC0001].

> **Need to know**
> - Is the Saudi embassy's approved-agency list published anywhere?
> - How many agencies are on the list in 2026?

#### The Bangladesh embassy's attestation, and the missing contract

The Bangladesh embassy's labour wing in Riyadh attests the employer's demand letter (see Work visa: company worker, Step 2) [A0087]. Group visas always needed it [A0087]. Under a rule the Bangladesh government introduced on 22 December 2024, any Saudi demand letter for more than one worker must be attested by the embassy, while a letter for one worker needs no embassy attestation; before, letters for up to 24 workers needed none, and only BMET's attestation was taken, a former BAIRA joint secretary general said [SP0254, SP0255, SP0247, SP0248, SP0249]. In a memorandum to the expatriates' welfare secretary on 5 February 2025, BAIRA leaders put the earlier exemption at up to 25 workers, and demanded that 24-person visas be treated as single visas and cleared without embassy attestation [SP0223, SP0224]. The embassy lacks the capacity to attest every worker's demand letter, so the sudden change cut departures, the same former BAIRA official said in March 2025 (see What has closed it before) [SP0252, SP0253].

> **Need to know**
> - Before 22 December 2024, were demand letters exempt from embassy attestation up to 24 workers or up to 25?

TIB (Transparency International Bangladesh) found some officials attest without checks or for bribes [SB0009]. Experts told Prothom Alo in December 2024 that embassy attestation of demand letters should end and recruiting agencies be made liable instead, that labour wings fail workers because administration-cadre officers serve there temporarily and are transferred, and that brokers should be brought under registration [SP0149, SP0150, SP0151].

In October 2025 BMET's additional director general said employment contracts are found for only 3-4% of workers going to Saudi Arabia [SI0032], and workers often first see a contract at the airport [SB0013]. At a meeting in Riyadh on 30 January 2025 the two countries discussed making sure workers receive their employment contract before leaving Bangladesh, and Saudi officials said a task force against illegal workers and recruitment irregularities would soon be formed, welcoming Bangladesh's proposal for a joint team [SP0211, SP0212]. In May 2025 Asif Nazrul proposed in Riyadh that Saudi Arabia check an employer's capacity and whether the work exists before issuing a work visa, and that contracts be signed online before the worker arrives; these were proposals, not agreed measures [SP0326].

#### The village end

Since 2023 an agency may use only registered sub-agents, but most agencies still deal with workers only through sub-agents beyond regulatory oversight [SG0005, SB0013]. A village broker in Cumilla got Tk 20,000 from the agent for each man in 2024 [SJ0042]. How to deal with a broker: see Work visa: company worker, Step 1, item 4.

Manpower businessman Motiur Rahman said seven masked young men beat him on a train on 18 May 2025 and tried to push him off until he fell at Nasratpur station, Adamdighi, Bogura; he filed an attempted-murder case at Santahar Junction railway police station naming as main accused the brother-in-law of Sajib, a man he had sent to Saudi Arabia who had found no work [SP0332, SP0333, SP0334]. Sajib's father said his son had gone 40 days earlier and still had no work and Motiur was hiding from the family, but denied any part in the attack [SP0332, SP0333, SP0334].

> **Need to know**
> - What does a village broker get per man sent to Saudi Arabia in 2026?

#### Women domestic workers: the money should run the other way

The Saudi household pays its recruitment office up to SR 11,750 for a Bangladeshi domestic worker (July 2026), and in 2020 the office paid the Bangladeshi agency about USD 2,000 per woman through Musaned, so she should pay nothing [SM0035, SI0029]; yet none of the returnee women IOM interviewed in 2020 had gone free [SI0030]. Saudi Arabia's Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development set that cap on 15 January 2024, lowering the maximum that may be paid to a recruitment service provider: SR 11,750 for a worker from Bangladesh, against SR 14,700 for the Philippines, SR 13,800 for Sri Lanka, SR 9,000 for Kenya, SR 8,300 for Uganda and SR 5,900 for Ethiopia [SP0008, SP0009, SP0010]. In OKUP's survey of returnees from six Middle East countries (December 2024), 21% of 666 women said they had spent Tk 2-3 lakh to go, and 29% of men more than Tk 6 lakh [SP0172]. Asma Begum, a returnee from Saudi Arabia, said at a December 2025 roundtable that she paid Tk 45,000 through a broker to go, was much abused while working there, was ill for six months after coming home, with high treatment costs, and has not fully repaid the loan she took to go [SP0462, SP0463, SP0464]. How it works: see Work visa: domestic worker.

Sector people said in September 2026 that women going abroad don't know the language and have no cooking or housework training, that kafils make them work in several homes and abuse them, and that Saudi Arabia is turning to workers from African countries [SP0775]. By Saudi government figures about 5 lakh workers from Kenya and Uganda, mostly women domestic workers, work in Saudi Arabia, and at least 274 Kenyan workers, mostly women, died there in the five years to early 2025, the New York Times reported [SP0277, SP0278].

#### The medical

Brokers sell guaranteed "fit" results from the Wafid medical check (the Gulf health check) at centres allegedly controlled by a few influential owners who issue fake certificates; a worker who paid Tk 25,000 for a fit certificate was found unfit in Saudi Arabia and deported [SB0040, B0031]. The check itself: Work visa: company worker, Step 4.

### What you pay, and what you earn and keep

The Saudi employer is meant to pay nearly every cost, and the legal maximum a worker may pay is Tk 1.65 lakh in all; yet a man pays two to three times that, about Tk 5 lakh on average and up to Tk 9 lakh, mostly borrowed [C0020, A0013, A0014, SJ0025]. Most of the jobs pay SR 800-1,000 a month [SJ0007]; a man on SR 800 who buys his own food and phone keeps at most SR 250 (Tk 8,250) (see What reaches home, and how long the debt takes) [SM0008]. The rule and the whole cost: see The legal maximum and what people really pay.

#### What the visa, medical and fingerprints really cost
- The visa: it costs the Saudi sponsor about SR 2,000 to issue, the sponsor sells it for Tk 1-1.5 lakh, and with each commission on the way the worker ends up paying three or four times the original price [SB0006, SI0037, SB0007]. On a genuine company visa you should pay only the Bangladesh-side fees, about Tk 19,800 in all, before the skill test, passport and ticket (see If a relative or friend offers you a visa, What it costs, official against real) [A0028, RB0021, B0052, RB0024, B0166, A0011, A0012, B0096]. A free visa (also called an azad visa, আযাদ ভিসা) is the scam visa with no real job [SM0045]; what it costs: see the same section. What men who died in the 2026 Riyadh factory fire had paid: see The Riyadh factory fire [SP0722, SP0723].
- The medical: the set fee is about Tk 10,000, but most workers pay double or more; a worker declared unfit pays Tk 30,000-80,000 for a "fit" report, and many make a "contract" (কন্ট্রাক্ট) with a broker in advance to be passed [SJ0013, SJ0014]. A bought "fit" report can get you deported (see The medical) [B0031].
- Tasheer's fingerprints: the service fee is about Tk 660-694, or about Tk 2,100-2,200 in its premium Lounge; the appointment slip is free, so an agency asking money for it is overcharging (see Who pays what) [RB0024, SJ0010, RB0026].
- The ticket and extras: in January 2025 Bangladeshis in Saudi Arabia complained to Asif Nazrul in Riyadh about high air ticket prices and recruiting agencies collecting extra money, and he promised solutions [SP0206].

#### How they pay

81% of men borrow to go [SJ0025]. The lenders:
- Family: loans mostly interest-free [SJ0034].
- NGOs: 10-15% interest [SJ0034].
- Moneylenders (মহাজন): far higher interest [SJ0034]. A dadon (দাদন, an informal loan) a Trishal construction worker took in late 2023 cost Tk 10,000 a month per lakh (see What reaches home, and how long the debt takes) [SP0237, SP0238].
- Probashi Kallyan Bank (PKB), the government bank for migrant workers: up to Tk 3 lakh at 9% without collateral once the worker has the visa (see If you need to borrow) [A0052].

Land goes too: in OKUP's 2022 Gulf survey, 72% of free-visa workers took loans, 11% mortgaged land and 6% sold land, and those who came home without work could not recover the mortgaged land [SP0433].

Workers pay intermediaries part in advance, often before the visa is confirmed, and mostly get no receipt [SB0013, SJ0040].

#### The iqama: the employer's bill

Each year a company worker's iqama costs the employer SR 9,150-10,350, plus compulsory health insurance [SM0022, SM0023]:
- iqama fee: SR 650;
- work permit fee: SR 100;
- expat levy: SR 800 a month (SR 700 for each foreign worker up to the number of Saudis in the firm);
- health insurance.

Every item is the employer's cost, and nothing may be deducted from wages for it [C0020, C0014]. If you are asked to pay any of it, complain to the labour ministry on 19911 (see If you aren't paid) [B0109]. In practice some small kafils and supply companies deduct the iqama and health-insurance cost from wages [SM0033]. The work permit and levy can be renewed for 3, 6, 9 or 12 months at a time [SM0024]: that is how a kafil hands a worker a 3-month iqama and comes back for money every quarter [A0080]. Check the expiry date yourself on Absher, the interior ministry's app [SM0040]. At a meeting in Riyadh on 30 January 2025 between Asif Nazrul and Saudi Human Resources Vice Minister Abdullah Abuthnain, the main topic was high iqama renewal fees and workers losing their jobs because of them; Saudi Arabia said its labour law was being reformed to protect both sides' rights [SP0208, SP0209].

#### On a free visa you pay it yourself

On a free visa the worker pays the whole iqama renewal himself, SR 8,000-12,000 a year (Tk 2.6-4 lakh) [SM0013]. On top of that he pays the kafil: a monthly or yearly cut called kamishon (কামিশন), and an extra SR 5,000-6,000 (Tk 1.65-2 lakh) when the iqama comes up for renewal [SM0012, SG0002]. In 2026 workers told of paying a kafil Tk 4-8 lakh for a renewal that never came [SL0028].

> **Need to know**
> - Is the extra SR 5,000-6,000 at renewal the yearly kamishon itself, or paid on top of it?

#### What the job pays

Nearly 80% of those going are cleaners, loaders and other low-skilled workers on SR 800-1,000 by recruiters' account, and cleaners promised SR 1,000-1,200 often get no more than SR 500 [SJ0008, SJ0007, SL0033]. Foreign Minister Hasan Mahmud told parliament on 1 July 2024 that West Asia is the source of 60% of Bangladesh's remittances and that about 60% of the Bangladeshi workers employed there are semi-skilled or unskilled [SP0084]. Saudi Arabia sets no minimum wage for foreign workers; the contract wage is what counts [C0092]. Monthly wages in BMET's 2025 permission letters [SJ0012, SJ0003, SJ0002]:
- kitchen workers: SR 800 plus SR 200 for food;
- construction labourers: SR 1,100 with food and housing;
- carpenters, masons, steel fixers, electricians and painters: SR 1,200 plus SR 300 for food;
- heavy equipment operators: SR 1,500.

New house drivers get about SR 1,500-1,800 [D0077]. A writer from King Saud University in Riyadh wrote in December 2024 that most Bangladeshis in Saudi Arabia are seen doing low-level work because they lack Arabic, while Indian and Pakistani workers with Arabic are hired for office jobs [SP0154, SP0155, SP0156].

#### What it costs to live

A worker living outside a camp needs about SR 1,450-2,650 a month (November 2026), more than the SR 800-1,000 most men earn [SM0008, SJ0007]. What the employer must provide: see Your rights at work, Housing, food and health care. The main items for a worker living outside [SM0008]:
- shared room: SR 500-900;
- food he cooks himself: SR 500-800;
- transport: SR 150-300;
- phone and internet: SR 50-100.

> **Need to know**
> - What does a worker in a labour camp whose contract gives no food spend a month on a shared mess in 2026?

#### What reaches home, and how long the debt takes

A man on SR 800 (Tk 26,400) who buys his own food and phone, SR 550-900 a month, keeps at most SR 250 (Tk 8,250) [SM0008]. At that rate, and assuming free housing, a Tk 4.5 lakh debt takes about 55 months, over four years, to repay before interest, against a usual 2-year contract [C0095]. Many low-skilled Gulf workers struggle to send even Tk 15,000-20,000 a month (2024) [SB0022]; a worker who does manage that still needs 23-30 months for the same debt [SB0022]. In 2020 men took 19 months or more to recover the cost [SJ0021]. By IOM figures for 2019, a Bangladeshi migrant worker sent home USD 203.33 a month on average, against USD 546.10 for a Filipino worker [SP0041]. Sending costs and channels: see Sending money home.

Cases:
- Late 2023: Md. Kawsar (30), a construction worker from Trishal, Mymensingh, went for Tk 7 lakh in all, Tk 5 lakh of it a dadon costing Tk 10,000 a month per lakh, and his family took a further Tk 2.10 lakh from two NGOs to pay the dadon, Prothom Alo reported in March 2025, about 16 months after he left [SP0237, SP0238]. The Tk 50,000-55,000 he sent home each month all went on interest, and his wife said he would come home once the loan was repaid [SP0239, SP0240].
- 2024: Shafiqul borrowed about USD 5,000 (over Tk 5 lakh), the price village brokers demanded, for an ordinary construction job in Saudi Arabia, and on Tk 30,000-40,000 a month spent almost his whole first year repaying it, the ILO's country director and IOM's chief of mission wrote in December 2025 [SP0447, SP0448, SP0449].
- 2025-2026: men killed in the August 2026 Riyadh factory fire left their families the debt (see The Riyadh factory fire) [SP0726, SP0727, SP0734, SP0597, SP0598].

### What goes wrong most, and what people wrongly believe

Nearly every Saudi complaint is one of three things: no iqama, no work, or pay that is late, short or missing, and behind them, again and again, is a visa sold for a job that didn't exist or an employer who won't pay for the permit [E0128, SL0014]. The false beliefs that go with them are about the same visa and permit: that a "free visa" lets you work for anyone, as brokers sell it [SM0011], and that the iqama is the worker's cost, when the employer should pay [SL0031].

#### Complaints

Of 114 complaints to BMET in 2023-2024 that OKUP studied, 47% got no work permit on arrival [SL0004]. Men without a permit were often kept until their entry visa ran out, then counted as absconders [SL0011]. Only 39% of complaints were resolved, usually for Tk 20,000 to just over Tk 1 lakh [SL0007]. To complain, see "If an agency, broker or relative takes your money" and If you aren't paid [A0056, C0039].

Other problems officials and organisations have named, oldest first:
- 2023: OKUP's chairman Shakirul Islam said that in the Middle East women domestic workers face physical abuse and other problems because they don't know the language, and that men, though not directly harmed by it, earn less because without the language they stay in low-skilled work (quoted in a March 2024 opinion piece) [SP0042, SP0043, SP0044].
- 29 May 2024: the Bangladesh embassy's deputy head of mission, Md. Abul Hasan Mridha, asked the Saudi Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development for help with assurance of work and iqama, sending women workers home when their contracts end, quick return of workers whose iqamas have expired, and Bangladeshi agencies' complaints on the Musaned system, and asked it to name a focal point for quick solutions to workers' problems, which the assistant deputy minister agreed to [SP0060, SP0061].
- December 2024: the UN Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination said migrant and domestic workers, including women, face arbitrary detention, torture and ill-treatment in Saudi Arabia's criminal justice system [SP0583].
- April 2025: Foreign Adviser Touhid Hossain said about 80% of the problems Bangladeshi migrant workers face arise from processes in Bangladesh before they leave and 20% in the destination country, citing Saudi Arabia as an example of a mission's workload [SP0300, SP0301].
- July 2025: Asif Nazrul said that in one place in Saudi Arabia Bangladeshi thugs attack other Bangladeshis to snatch and loot their money [SP0378].
- August 2025: Prothom Alo reported that, after Bangladeshis abroad protested in support of the 2024 uprising, 188 people were freed from the UAE and 12 from Saudi Arabia and returned home [SP0394, SP0395].

> **Need to know**
> - Who is the Saudi labour ministry's focal point for Bangladeshi workers' problems agreed in May 2024, and can a worker or the embassy reach it?

#### What people believe, and the facts
- **"A free visa lets you work for anyone."** Brokers sell it as "go and work wherever you like" [SM0011]. In fact a worker may work only for the employer and job on the permit, or for another company through an Ajeer permit, which that company must apply for [C0021, SM0007]. Many jobs also need a baladiya (বালাদিয়া, municipal) health card, which free-visa workers don't easily get [SM0014]. Of 10 men one Cumilla broker sent, three got a proper job with a contract (2024) [SL0020]. To spot one, see Avoid a "free visa" [A0079].
- **"The iqama is the worker's cost: pay the kafil and he renews it."** A Bangladesh embassy official confirmed the employer pays (see "What you pay, and what you earn and keep", The iqama: the employer's bill) [SL0031].
- **"Saudi Arabia abolished the kafala system in 2025."** No Saudi announcement was made: the story began in an 18 October 2025 article by ImpACT International, a UK think tank, citing no Saudi source [SK0008].
- **"A visit, tourist or Umrah visa can be turned into an iqama once you're there."** A visit visa can't become an iqama, and working on a tourist or Umrah visa brings a ban of 3 to 10 years (Changing visa type) [E0054, E0057].
- **"Pay extra and the medical is guaranteed fit."** This is false (see "How a job reaches you, and who takes a cut", The medical) [B0032, B0031].

#### Irregular routes and how they end

Irregular status can end in a raid: those detained usually spend one to two weeks in a deportation centre and land in Dhaka with almost nothing [SG0032, SG0033], then face a re-entry ban [E0057], and irregular returnees get no Welfare Board benefits [E0092]. Saudi Arabia tops the countries from which workers return empty-handed without a passport, Prothom Alo reported in January 2025, amid complaints of workers not getting work as contracted, becoming illegal, and being detained by Saudi police and sent home [SP0183]. There are ways out without a raid: the embassy's Special Exit Programme, grace periods, and, until the end of 2026, an employer regularising an expired permit (see Going home without valid papers) [E0026, E0087, G0014].

Workers fall out of status in four ways:
- a free visa with no real job [A0081];
- an employer who never turns the three-month entry permit into an iqama, often to avoid the fee [B0131, SL0014];
- a kafil who keeps the renewal money or files huroob [SL0028, SL0036];
- a visit or Umrah visa overstayed [E0057].

Cases:
- 16 January 2018: Abdul Jabbar Sardar, a farm labourer from Hiranandapur, Tanore, Rajshahi, borrowed Tk 7 lakh and went; he was put to work as a cleaner on landing, later realised brokers had "sold" him as an airport cleaner, and after three years of unpaid work was sent away empty-handed, his wife Adori Khatun said; the loan was still unpaid in May 2025 [SP0338, SP0339, SP0340].
- December 2024: Mosammat Hanifa of Narayanganj said she paid a broker Tk 4 lakh to go for a company job, found none, was kept in a room with 20 to 25 others for some days, made to work a month in pilgrims' houses and then turned out, worked illegally in various places, and came home with kidney problems and diabetes [SP0175, SP0176].
- 16 February 2026: Fahim Ahmed Munna (20) of Dowarabazar, Sunamganj, whose father has worked in Saudi Arabia for 18 years, was sent through a relative acting as a broker; he spent two days in Saudi Arabia, was taken to Libya, put on a small rubber boat (a "game") on 21 March, and died of hunger and thirst at sea, his uncle said [SP0660, SP0661, SP0662].
- April 2026: Azgar Bepari had paid money for his iqama twice without getting it, his sister-in-law said [SP0666].

Many free-visa men end up running or staffing a shop or workshop registered in a Saudi's name (tasattur, commercial concealment): that carries up to 5 years in prison, a fine of up to SR 5 million, and deportation with a ban on returning to work [SM0020, SM0018, SM0019, SM0021]. Anyone who shelters, transports or helps a violator faces up to 15 years in prison and a fine of up to SR 1 million [SL0040]. Bangladeshis who open a baqala (small grocery shop) or other small business have to keep a Sudanese or Egyptian employee, paid a large sum, to handle daily correspondence and legal formalities, a writer reported from field work in December 2024 [SP0157]. In July 2024 Foreign Minister Hasan Mahmud asked Saudi Arabia for a special opportunity for Bangladeshis running businesses there to register them in their own names [SP0085, SP0094]; in February 2025 the Bangladesh ambassador said, as community leaders told him, that 5,000 Bangladeshi businesspeople were already registered [SP0236].

> **Need to know**
> - Can a Bangladeshi worker register a business in his own name in Saudi Arabia in 2026, and how?

#### The Riyadh factory fire

### In one minute

Saudi Arabia takes two of every three Bangladeshis who go abroad for work, though 2026 departures are 45% down on 2025 [E0129]. In 2026 the war reached Saudi Arabia: missiles and drones hit a labour camp, Aramco oil sites and the Riyadh and Dammam airports, killing and injuring Bangladeshis, and Dhaka-Riyadh flights are suspended (see Current situation) [SP0641, SP0779, SP0794, SP0520, SH0007]. Every work visa belongs to a Saudi sponsor (kafil, কফিল), who can sell it, and it reaches the village through agents and brokers at several times its price [SB0007]. A man pays Tk 3.5-5 lakh or more, most often Tk 4-5 lakh, mostly borrowed, against a legal maximum of Tk 1.65 lakh [SJ0004, SB0024, SJ0025, A0013]. A cleaner or labourer earns SR 500-1,200 a month (Tk 16,500-40,000) [SJ0007, SL0033]. The sponsor holds the iqama (আকামা, the residence card), and a visa sold for a job that doesn't exist (a "free visa") leaves the worker without an iqama, work or pay [E0128]. A woman going as a domestic worker should pay nothing, yet none of the returnee women IOM interviewed in 2020 had gone free [D0059, SI0030].
