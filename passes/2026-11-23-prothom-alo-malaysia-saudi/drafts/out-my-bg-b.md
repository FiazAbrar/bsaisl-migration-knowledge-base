### How a job reaches you, and who takes a cut

A job reaches a village down a chain, and every link takes a cut: the employer's quota becomes calling visas; the employer's demand letter is bought in Malaysia; the visa is processed in FWCMS; then a licensed agency; then a village broker (দালাল) [W0043, A0026, YE0029, A0028, Z0148, YE0018, YE0016]. The choke point is FWCMS, built by the company Bestinet: every Bangladeshi worker's visa passes through it, and only BOESL and 25 private agencies chosen by Malaysia can process a visa in it, so every other agency pays one of them [Z0001, YA0024, Z0144, Z0148, A0029]. On the street the whole arrangement is called the সিন্ডিকেট (syndicate) [YI0019].

#### The quota

Quotas are approved on the employer's paper eligibility, not on a checked need, so a quota, and the visa sold from it, can exist with no job behind it; Malaysia's private employment agencies' association says agents apply for 100 workers when a company needs 10 [YE0013, YE0014]. The employer pays a yearly levy per worker, RM1,850 in manufacturing, construction and services and RM640 in plantation and agriculture in Peninsular Malaysia [W0043, M0227]. In October 2026 Immigration broke up an "A-to-B" ring, believed to be run by two Bangladeshi men, that got quotas on false company details and hired the workers out to other firms at RM3,800 to 5,000 profit a head [YE0007, YE0008, YE0009, D0046]. In that raid, on 8 October 2026, Immigration's Director General, Datuk Zakaria Shaban, said the team seized 265 Bangladeshi passports (with 63 Indonesian, 12 Pakistani and 1 Indian) and RM6,700 believed paid by customers, detained 9 Bangladeshi men among 12 foreign "customers", and found that the workers brought in did not work for the companies that applied for the quota but independently for other companies [MP0474, MP0475, MP0476].

#### Which employer

The employer decides whether a job and pay are waiting when you land, and whether you get your recruitment fee back. The ones to avoid are firms whose quota is far above the work they have: they have left workers with no job. The ones that have paid fees back are large export manufacturers under pressure from foreign buyers [YB0016, YA0071, YI0013].

- **Bad: ghost jobs:** services firms brought in 142,204 workers in 2023 against 20,000 needed; 161 Bangladeshis promised construction jobs had no work or pay for seven months; and a Kuala Lumpur company hired about 600 and gave them no work [YB0016, YB0018, YG0003]. In January 2024 Malaysia's human resources minister said the authorities had finished investigating five companies accused of promising hundreds of workers jobs and not giving them, that employers who hired such workers must pay their wages whether or not there is work, and that companies and people who break the law will not be allowed to hire migrant workers [MP0004, MP0005]. In a statement of 20 March 2024, BCSM (Bangladesh Civil Society for Migrants, a coalition of 23 organisations including RMMRU and BRAC) said some syndicate agencies had sent workers to Malaysian firms with no capacity to employ them, and in some cases to companies that did not exist under that name, after taking lakhs of taka from each worker [MP0025, MP0529].
- **No work on arrival, 2024-25:**
  - **February 2024:** at least 63 young men from Kazipur and Motmura unions in Gangni upazila, Meherpur, who went in November 2023, said they had been held for three months in a three-storey building in Kuala Lumpur with their passports taken, no work, visas mostly expired, not allowed out and fed only potatoes, dal and rice twice a day [MP0012, MP0013, MP0014].
  - **March 2024:** two officials of the Bangladesh mission in Malaysia, speaking anonymously, told Prothom Alo that 1.5 to 2 lakh Bangladeshi workers there were unemployed and living in inhuman conditions, that many came to the mission regularly for help, and that the mission could do little [MA0014, MA0015]. That month a member of the Facebook group "Malaysia Probashi Bangladeshi" wrote that his newly arrived brother had found no work for four months [MA0016]. From January to 24 March 2024, 453 workers came home empty-handed and without passports, on travel permits [MA0045].
  - **May 2024:** the Ministry's own count was five thousand who had found no work, while long-settled Bangladeshis said thousands were jobless, some selling goods on pavements or umbrellas in the rain to pay for food [MA0043, MA0044].
  - **2 June 2024:** the Ministry's secretary said employers come to the Malaysian airport to collect workers, that in the previous few months none had failed to, and that work was being arranged for the five thousand who had found none [MP0067].
  - **June 2024:** Md Rajib of Shibchar, Madaripur, who went about three months earlier after spending Tk 4 lakh 90 thousand, had still found no job, was living with an uncle in Kuala Lumpur for food and lodging, and had sent no money home [MP0107].
  - **November 2025:** a group of workers from Cumilla, met near Sultan Abdul Samad Jamek Mosque in Kuala Lumpur, said each had spent Tk 5 lakh through a broker and they had still found no work, and were trying for factory jobs outside the capital [MP0732].
- **Employers that avoid Bangladeshis:** three North South University researchers wrote in Prothom Alo on 10 June 2024 that some palm oil producers in Malaysia often refrain from recruiting Bangladeshi workers [MP0097, MP0098].
- **Good: repaid fees:** after US import bans for forced labour, Top Glove paid workers over USD 30 million and FGV set aside RM112 million to reimburse fees to current and former migrant workers [YA0071, YI0013].
- **Employer-paid recruitment:** BCSM said in May 2025 that before the syndicate some Malaysian companies took workers from Bangladesh at the employer's cost but could no longer do so, and that some who went earlier found no job and lived half-starved abroad or on money sent from home [MP0531, MP0532].

#### Company visa, agent visa, free visa

Workers sort jobs by who holds the visa; what separates the three is which company holds your quota and whether you work at its address [YI0015, YG0005]:

- **কোম্পানি ভিসা (company visa):** you work for the company that applied for you; it pays the visa and the yearly renewal, and keeps your passport [YI0015].
- **"Agent visa":** puts you under an agent or subcontractor who hires you out. Outsourcing companies were abolished in 2019, but some workers who go legally still land with one, are sent to work elsewhere, and can't tell until they arrive [YG0025, YG0026].
- **ফ্রি ভিসা (free visa):** sells the right to work anywhere, which no work pass gives. Every work pass (PLKS) is tied to the employer that applied, free-visa workers pay an agent about RM5,000 (Tk 1.53 lakh) a year to renew, and agents hold back weeks or months of their pay [W0052, YI0016, YI0017].

Working away from the company on your permit is an offence: Immigration's Ops Mahir raid in Ipoh in September 2026 detained 175 foreigners, mostly Bangladeshis, including people working outside the company named on their permit [W0052, YG0005, YG0006]. The calling visa (VDR, the visa approval) comes first and the e-visa after it, both through BOESL or one of the 25; check both (Step 3; Step 5) [Z0148, W0045].

#### The demand letter: the first price

The demand letter is the employer's job order for a set number of workers, and buying it is the first cost in the chain: a Bangladeshi agency buys it from Malaysia [YD0011, YE0029, G0013]. Agency owners say one now costs RM8,000 to 12,000 per worker (Tk 2.45 to 3.67 lakh), against RM2,000 to 3,000 before (2022-24 prices: see [Where a Tk 5.5 lakh deal goes](#where-a-tk-55-lakh-deal-goes)) [G0013, YE0027]. Big agencies buy the demand and pay the middlemen around it, so small agencies can't send workers cheaply [YE0029]. In 2022-24, clearances for 4,92,416 workers were taken in the names of the 100 syndicate agencies in a little over a year and a half, and at least half of those job letters are alleged to have been bought, at an average of RM6,000 (Tk 1.5 lakh) a worker, Prothom Alo reported in September 2024 [MP0181].

#### Attestation

Every demand letter must be attested by the Bangladesh High Commission in Kuala Lumpur, against the employer's notarised commitment to give the workers work and pay [U0044, W0059]. Attestation is not proof that the job was checked: in 2019 the High Commissioner said the mission lacked the staff to check every case [YE0035].

#### The processing agency: the toll gate

Every visa must go through BOESL or one of 25 private "processing" agencies, which charge other agencies a fee per worker [Z0148, A0019, A0026]. Malaysia lists 338 Bangladeshi agencies: BOESL, the 25 "processing" agencies, and 312 associates (সহযোগী এজেন্সি), 10 under each of the 25 and 62 under BOESL [Z0144, W0008, YE0024]; Prothom Alo's summary of the Ministry's 15 October 2026 announcement put the number allowed to work at 337, including BOESL and the 25 [MP0441]. An associate may find employers, collect demand and select workers, but the calling visa and e-visa go through BOESL or one of the 25, which issues a no-objection certificate so the associate can get BMET clearance under its own licence [Z0145, Z0148, Z0149]. For 2026, claims for the toll run from a Tk 6,000 processing fee to RM5,500 (about Tk 1.68 lakh), the figure BAIRA (Bangladesh Association of International Recruiting Agencies) members allege (2022-24 toll: see [Where a Tk 5.5 lakh deal goes](#where-a-tk-55-lakh-deal-goes)) [A0019, A0026]. Even the government channel buys visas: of the Tk 1,62,500 BOESL charged stranded workers, its managing director said Tk 1 lakh was for buying visas [A0056].

**How the toll worked in 2022-24.** Once a Malaysian demand letter was finalised, FWCMS distributed it among the approved agencies, not equally, and the workers had to be sent through the agency in whose name it arrived [MA0053, MA0054]. Agency owners outside the list told Prothom Alo that Malaysian companies sent demand letters to them, because of their long business there, but the letters went to a listed agency because their own names were not in FWCMS, so they paid that agency to send their workers: Tk 1.52 to 1.65 lakh a worker, three owners said in May 2024; Tk 1,52,000, four owners said in September 2026 [MA0024, MA0053, MA0054, MB0051, MB0052, MB0053]. Prothom Alo reported that the agencies inside the syndicate took at least Tk 1.5 lakh in "syndicate fee" a worker without doing anything, an allegation the ACC (Anti-Corruption Commission) is investigating [MB0051, MB0052, MB0053]. BAIRA's joint secretary general, Fakhrul Islam, said in May 2024 that with no set rules for making the list, some agencies with no experience of sending workers got on it in collusion with the ringleaders and then earned Tk 1,52,000 a worker sitting at home [MA0060]. In September 2024 he, also managing director of the agency Human Resource Development Centre, said the agency he sent workers through took Tk 1,52,000 a worker just for arranging the BMET clearance, which in fact costs Tk 6,000, and that the airfare and all the worker's other costs had to be paid separately [MP0164, MP0165]. BAIRA members' memoranda to the Ministry put the ring's take two ways: Tk 1,07,000 a person, extorted by the syndicate built by Ruhul Amin and Aminul Islam (5 February 2025) [MP0487, MP0488]; and Tk 1,52,000 a worker, of which RM5,000 (Tk 1,25,000) went to Bestinet, collected in Bangladesh by Amin's local representative Ruhul Amin, with the worker's whole cost at Tk 4 to 5 lakh (21 April 2025, signed by the owners of 453 agencies) [MP0497, MP0498, MP0499]. Who these two men are: see Who controls it.

- **Five M International:** agencies inside the syndicate blamed the workers' extra cost on agencies outside it, which they said bought demand letters from Malaysia at high prices in unhealthy competition; Five M's managing director, Masud Uddin Chowdhury, said on 30 May 2024 that he had not bought even 100 jobs, that all came automatically through the software, that those whose jobs came sent workers through Five M, and that it only did visa processing and took only the government-fixed fee [MA0057, MA0058, MA0059].

**2026: the same position, agency owners say.** The four agency owners told Prothom Alo in September 2026 that an associate will be in the same position, since it cannot send workers directly, and that listed agencies approved without experience are unknown to Malaysian employers, so they will send workers through experienced agencies and take the syndicate fee in return [MB0054, MB0055]. Riazul Islam, BAIRA's immediate past senior vice-president, said no agency applied to be an associate, they were simply made associates; that jobs will come in the names of the 25, not of the associates; and that at least 817 agencies sent workers in the earlier round by paying the syndicate fee [MB0056, MB0057]. BAIRA members said about 1,400 Bangladeshi agencies were once listed on FWCMS, but only 10 were approved in 2016, and in 2022, 25 at first and finally 100 [MB0058]. In June 2026, The Edge, a Malaysian business paper, reported (as carried by Prothom Alo) that 102 Bangladeshi firms were then approved under FWCMS and that Bangladesh was considering including 432 recruiting agencies instead; a manpower-sector executive told it that Bangladesh wanted recruitment opened to more Bangladeshi companies rather than a few chosen by some influential people [MP0353, MP0354, MP0355].

#### How the toll was collected

A Prothom Alo investigation published on 17 September 2024 set out how the 2022-24 toll was collected, from agency owners, syndicate members, company records and Ministry letters [MP0157, MP0159, MP0161, MP0166, MP0172].

**The e-wallet.** Every worker going to Malaysia had to be registered in Migram, the software formally called FWCMS, whose registration fee was RM100 (about Tk 2,700); agency owners said about Tk 1,07,000 was taken per worker instead, of which the local collecting firm kept Tk 7,000 and sent Tk 1 lakh to Bestinet in Malaysia, the transactions being illegal [MP0157, MP0158]. Only syndicate members' names were in Migram, and they paid the RM100 per worker through an e-wallet; two agency owners said Bestinet let the fee be paid from the e-wallet only after Tk 1,07,000 had been deposited for that worker [MP0159, MP0160]. On 9 May 2023 the Ministry had written to the High Commission in Kuala Lumpur asking it to take up with Malaysia complaints from several agencies that Bestinet was not allocating e-wallet top-ups, a problem several Malaysian employers had also reported to Malaysia's home ministry [MP0176, MP0177, MP0178].

**The cash.** The money had to be deposited at the Madani Avenue, Dhaka, office of Catharsis International, the agency of Ruhul Amin (Swapan), whose staff took in crores of taka in cash every day, two syndicate members said [MP0161, MP0162]. S M Rafiq, managing director of United Export Limited, itself a syndicate member, said every syndicate agency had to deliver Tk 1,07,000 a worker to the Catharsis office [MP0163].

**The company records.** Records of the RJSC (Registrar of Joint Stock Companies and Firms, Bangladesh's company registry) show Bestinet registered in Bangladesh on 24 March 2014 as Bestinet Bangladesh Limited, with Aminul Islam bin Amin Nur as chairman and Mohammad Ruhul Amin as managing director [MP0166]. Prothom Alo also reported that Amin held 10,000 of its 12,000 shares and Ruhul Amin 2,000, that on 28 March 2019 Amin's shares were transferred to Ruhul Amin and his brother Saiful Islam became a director, and that in 2022 the company was renamed Catharsis Solutions Limited [MP0167, MP0168]. Ruhul Amin told Prothom Alo that Bestinet Bangladesh had no connection with Malaysia's Bestinet and that Amin gave up his share because he got no business in Bangladesh; on 20 June 2024 he called the allegations of taking and laundering money false, and said both governments gave all approvals and agencies with good contacts in Malaysia got approval after heavy lobbying [MP0169, MP0170, MP0171].

**The visa office.** On 2 January 2023, acting on a BAIRA letter, the Ministry wrote to the High Commission that most agencies selected through FWCMS lacked the facilities and capacity to recruit and send workers, that automatic selection let them charge high migration costs, and that although 12 visa centres were approved, only FWCMS's Dhaka office, MEFC (Malaysia Employment Facilitation Centre), issued workers' e-visas; it asked for visas to be issued from several centres [MP0172, MP0173, MP0174]. MEFC's office is in Catharsis Tower in Banani, Dhaka; Malaysia-bound workers' passports were submitted there for the visa and agencies collected them there, sources told Prothom Alo [MP0175].

#### Who controls it

**Bestinet and Amin.** Bestinet was founded by Aminul Islam Abdul Nor ("Amin"), a Bangladesh-born Malaysian; Malaysia's Auditor-General found it collected RM100 per worker without a formal government contract [Z0001, YE0021, D0024]. Bestinet owns Migram, the software of FWCMS; the Bangla press calls Amin "Amin Nor" or "Amin Nur" [MA0051, MB0039, MB0040]. People in the sector told Prothom Alo in June 2024 that Bestinet's contract with the Malaysian government ended on 31 May 2024, that Malaysia stopped taking workers so as not to renew it with this controversial company, but that the contract might now be extended by three years [MP0075, MP0076]. Bestinet is supposed to receive RM100 (Tk 2,500) per worker, but is alleged to have done illegal business by controlling the whole process in the name of selecting agencies, Prothom Alo reported in April 2025 [MP0489, MP0490]. In October 2025 it reported that Amin had stepped down as Bestinet's chairman but still holds a stake, that at least Tk 8,750 crore is alleged to have been laundered out of Bangladesh by a ring in the Malaysia labour market under the Awami League government, and that manpower exporters claim Amin and his Bangladeshi agent Ruhul Amin led it [MP0703, MP0704]. Asked on 20 August 2025 about Amin Nur, the expatriates' welfare adviser, Asif Nazrul, said he works with the Malaysian government, that Bangladesh has nothing to discuss with him, and that talks are held directly with Malaysia's government [MP0639].

**Amin and Ruhul Amin Swapan, owner of Catharsis International.** The Daily Star reports that the two led the 2022-24 syndicate; Bangladesh police sought their extradition in 2024, both deny wrongdoing, and Amin has not been charged [YA0054, YA0042, YA0045, YA0046, YA0055]. Prothom Alo reported in May 2024 the allegation that Amin controls the whole worker-sending trade from Malaysia, that Swapan, a former BAIRA secretary general, acts as his representative in Bangladesh, and that the two decided who would be in the syndicate [MA0051, MA0052]. BAIRA members allege that Swapan led the forming of the 2022 syndicate, fixed the names of 69 of its 100 agencies and was the channel for money transacted in Malaysia; he did not respond to Prothom Alo [MA0034, MA0035]. RMMRU, a migration research body, said in its report "Trends in labour migration from Bangladesh 2024", presented on 28 December 2024, that Amin, owner of Malaysia's foreign-worker recruitment software, and Swapan jointly built the syndicate, which sent five and a half lakh workers and embezzled USD 200 crore (Tk 24,000 crore) from migrants [MA0070, MA0071]. Prothom Alo reported in September 2026 that Swapan has been in hiding abroad since the fall of the Awami League government in the 2024 uprising, that his agency's licence has been withdrawn, a case against him is under way, and the ACC is also investigating [MB0037, MB0038].

**Bringing them back.** Malaysia's Home Ministry confirmed in November 2024 that it had received Bangladesh's arrest request, which accused Amin and Ruhul Amin of money laundering, extortion and trafficking foreign workers; the minister said then that Malaysia wanted Dhaka to clarify whether the purpose was extradition or further investigation [MP0700, MP0701, MP0702]. Malaysia's Home Minister, Saifuddin Nasution Ismail, said in October 2025 that the two countries' police were working on Bangladesh's request to bring the two back, through government-to-government and police-to-police coordination agreed with Chief Adviser Muhammad Yunus during his visit [MP0697, MP0698, MP0699]. In June 2026 the matter was still under discussion between the two governments, The Edge reported [MP0358].

#### How the 2022-24 syndicate was formed

When Malaysia reopened in July 2022 after nearly four years, the choice of agencies was left to the Malaysian government; the Ministry sent it a list of 1,520 recruiting agencies and only 25 were chosen [MA0021, MA0022, MB0011, MB0012]. Prothom Alo reported that there was no policy for the selection and many allegations of influence and bribery in it [MA0021, MA0022]. BAIRA members said the MoU (memorandum of understanding) between the two countries gave Malaysia the job of finalising the agencies, which let one group trade illegally, and Prothom Alo reported that a ring of private agencies in Malaysia assisted the ring [MA0007, MA0008, MA0009]. BCSM said on 14 May 2025 that the 2021 MoU gave Malaysia the job of selecting agencies, which let a Malaysian company and some Bangladeshi agencies form a syndicate that pushed the cost of sending a worker to Tk 5.5 lakh [MP0529, MP0530]. The expatriates' welfare adviser, Asif Nazrul, said on 20 August 2025 that the agreement the previous government signed itself says Malaysia will select the agencies, that it cannot be changed by force, and that Bangladesh had told Malaysia in every meeting that all recruiting agencies should get the chance, as in other countries [MP0637, MP0638].

The list grew in three steps to 100, and it is alleged that the ring's leaders took a large sum from each agency to put its name in [MA0019, MA0020, MB0011, MB0012]; in a memorandum of 5 May 2025, BAIRA members put that price at Tk 5 crore an agency [MA0091, MA0092]. The agencies inside received at least Tk 1.5 lakh "syndicate fee" per worker while sitting idle, and part of it went to the ring's controllers in Malaysia, Prothom Alo reported (how it was charged: see The processing agency: the toll gate) [MA0019, MA0020]. Prothom Alo reported that no other country sending workers to Malaysia (14, by its count in 2024 and July 2026; 11, by its count in September 2026) had such a syndicate arrangement [MA0023, MB0015, MB0016, MB0049, MB0050].

Officials named on both sides: several UN experts said in a statement of 19 April 2024 that Bangladeshi migrant workers were being cheated in Malaysia and alleged that government officials of both countries were involved with the ring, and Bangladesh's expatriates' welfare secretary, Md Ruhul Amin, said on 21 April that the Ministry was reviewing it [MP0035, MP0036]. The ACC's summary of the allegations, reported in August 2024, said high-ranking officials of both the Bangladeshi and Malaysian governments were involved with the syndicate, and did not separately name Swapan, known as its ringleader [MP0150, MP0151]. Malaysia's High Commissioner in Dhaka, Haznah Md Hashim, said on 12 March 2024 that syndicates active in both countries were one cause of the problem, and on 29 May 2024 that there may be syndicates beyond the control of both governments [MA0010, MA0067]. A Prothom Alo editorial of 22 July 2026 noted that the UN human rights office (OHCHR) had issued a statement on the plight of Bangladeshi workers in Malaysia [MB0015, MB0016].

#### How the 2026 list was made

A Malaysian committee chose the 25 without the Bangladesh government, 17 of them had never sent a worker to Malaysia, and Prothom Alo reported allegations that Amin and Swapan again decided who joined [A0029, A0027, YB0028]. How it was made:

- **28 October 2025:** after the third Bangladesh-Malaysia Joint Working Group meeting, in Dhaka on 21 and 22 May, Malaysia sent Bangladesh its "recruiting agent selection criteria", and the Ministry said the same criteria would apply in future to Bangladesh, India, Nepal, Pakistan and Myanmar [MP0689, MP0690, MP0691]. The ten conditions for a private agency, as the Ministry published them [MP0692, MP0693, MP0694]:
  - at least five years of satisfactory operation since licensing;
  - proof of sending at least 3,000 workers abroad in the last five years;
  - experience in at least three destination countries in that time;
  - valid licences from all relevant Bangladeshi authorities;
  - a good-conduct certificate from the sending country's authority;
  - no record of forced labour, trafficking, labour-law breaches, extortion, money laundering or other financial crime;
  - its own training and assessment centre with accommodation;
  - testimonials from five different foreign employers;
  - a permanent office of at least 10,000 square feet, run for at least three years;
  - proof of lawfully following destination countries' recruitment conditions, Malaysia's included.

  The Ministry asked licensed agencies able to meet them to apply with supporting papers by 7 November 2025, and said Bangladesh would ask Malaysia to list every agency that met them without exception [MP0695, MP0696].
- **19 November 2025:** Asif Nazrul said Bangladesh had objected to several of the conditions and asked for the deadline to be extended, telling Malaysia that if they were kept there would again be a syndicate with only a few agencies able to send workers, and that Malaysia had promised to relax them [MP0713, MP0714].
- Malaysia set 10 conditions; 163 agencies first qualified, and after Malaysia relaxed three conditions and then one more at Bangladesh's request, the Ministry under the interim government sent a final list of 423, Prothom Alo reported [MB0044, MB0045].
- **9 April 2026:** the two governments' joint statement said Malaysia had begun introducing an AI-based, technology-driven recruitment system for all source countries, meant to cut middlemen and migration cost and bring the cost to workers to zero in line with the ILO (International Labour Organization) "employer pays" principle; Bangladesh said it fully supported such a digital platform [MP0317, MP0318].
- **30 July 2026:** Mahdi Amin, the Prime Minister's spokesperson, said Bangladesh had asked Malaysia to run the first phase of recruitment through BOESL, and that under the MoU valid until December 2026 Malaysia would set the final number of Bangladeshi agencies on its own vetting, from the list of 423 [MP0381, MP0414, MP0415].
- **21 August 2026:** Malaysia published the 25 on FWCMS [MB0018].
- **28 August 2026:** the Ministry's notice said a high-level joint committee of Malaysia's human resources, foreign and home ministries, its anti-corruption commission and law-enforcement agencies vetted the agencies, with not the slightest involvement of the Bangladesh government [MB0042, MB0043]. It said that because the MoU signed in 2022 gives Malaysia's government alone the job of choosing Bangladeshi agencies and runs until 31 December 2026, it is not easy for Bangladesh to change Malaysia's conditions, and that it hopes a new MoU after that date will protect Bangladesh's interest in approving agencies [MP0425, MP0426].
- **September 2026:** Malaysia approved 25 agencies each for Nepal and Bangladesh and 10 each for India, Pakistan and Myanmar, while everyone on the lists sent by countries such as Sri Lanka and the Philippines was approved; the agency owners' associations of four countries protested in a joint statement, and Nepal has begun investigating its list of 25, Prothom Alo reported [MB0046, MB0047, MB0048].

#### Objections and allegations

In order:

- **28 May 2024:** S M Rafiq, managing director of United Export Limited, itself a syndicate member, wrote to the Prime Minister's Office that the main figures in sending money from Bangladesh to Malaysia were Swapan and his Malaysian partner Amin [MP0183, MP0184].
- **22 August 2024:** in a letter to the Chief Adviser, BAIRA member Mostafa Mahmud alleged that Amin and Ruhul Amin had also built a Malaysia syndicate in 2017 and that over the two rounds the ring looted Tk 17,544 crore [MP0182].
- **September 2024:** Iftekharuzzaman, executive director of TIB (Transparency International Bangladesh), said workers from neighbouring countries go to Malaysia at the employer's cost while Bangladesh's rules are different, and that a ring in Malaysia acts as the representative of the Bangladeshi ring [MP0185, MP0186].
- **3 October 2024:** at a BAIRA press conference Fakhrul Islam read a statement naming Swapan as the syndicate's main ringleader, built with former MPs and ministers including Benzir Ahmed, Nizam Hazari, Masud Uddin Chowdhury and Awami League leader Mohiuddin Mohi, and saying no action had been taken against them [MP0191, MP0192, MP0193]. BAIRA's eight demands included ending Malaysia's power to select Bangladeshi agencies, letting the employer choose the agency, a central online system from the Ministry so all agencies could send workers at low or no cost, and sending the workers stranded by the syndicate again at low cost and free of it [MP0194, MP0195, MP0196].
- **5 February 2025:** at a human chain outside the Ministry, BAIRA leaders said the Amin-Swapan ring was manoeuvring to form a syndicate again around the reopening; a memorandum signed by 12 members, including Riazul Islam, Noman Chowdhury, Khandaker Abu Ashfaq and Md Fakhrul Islam, alleged it had extorted Tk 1,07,000 from each worker and demanded strict legal action against the two [MP0288, MP0289, MP0290].

#### The broker and the village

Most workers (78% in a 2023 survey) reach the agency through a sub-agent, sometimes through five or six levels of brokers [YE0033, YE0016]. The broker is usually a friend, neighbour, relative or returnee, and by October 2026 brokers were already collecting passports, and the owner of one associate agency said he had already collected over 1,000 [RF0030, YE0030, YE0028]. Across Bangladesh's visa trade, when a visa comes through relatives, BAIRA's secretary general says, the money changes hands mainly among them and the agency only does the paperwork [YI0020]. Only a sub-agent registered with BMET may now recruit (Step 1, "Before paying") [YB0001, YB0002].

Cases:

- **November 2023, Meherpur:** the Gangni men held in Kuala Lumpur (see Which employer) said three cousins, Suruj Ali and Awal of Sahebnagar and Abdullah of Mohammadpur, took Tk 4 to 5 lakh from each, promising construction work at Tk 45,000 to 50,000 a month within 40 days of arrival; they alleged that 54 of them were taken on 6 November 2023 to an agency office on Road 4, Banani, Dhaka, kept there some days, flown out on 11 November, and had their passports taken by agency people after landing [MP0015, MP0016, MP0017]. Abdullah told Prothom Alo he was a computer operator at the Dhaka travel agency involved, said everyone was expected to have work by 28 February and those who did not would be sent home, and did not answer whether money would be refunded; a Kazipur union member said more than a hundred people from the area went through a Dhaka travel agency and none got work [MP0018, MP0019, MP0020].
- **June 2024, Akhaura:** Tanvir Mia, 22, of Debgram, Brahmanbaria, for whom Tk 6.5 lakh had been paid to a broker and his brother in Malaysia for a supermarket job, had two flights cancelled, and on 31 May 2024 fell from the train taking him home from Dhaka into the Meghna and went missing [MP0069]. His family said they had trusted the broker because he had sent 30 people to Malaysia three days earlier, and his father had sold 130 decimals of land for the trip and his own business [MP0072, MP0073]. After Tanvir's death his father said the brokers had not contacted the family and had fled their homes [MP0074].
- **July 2024, Jashore:** in Bankra, on the Kopotakkho river where Monirampur and Jhikargachha upazilas meet, some local men who tried to go to Japan illegally through brokers were taken through Thailand and the jungle into Malaysia, came home after three years and then began sending people to Malaysia themselves; the bazar committee's general secretary said at least 10 of the area's multi-storey buildings are known as "Malaysia buildings" [MP0144, MP0145, MP0146].

### What you pay, and what you earn and keep

No cap had been set for 2026 [YD0007] until the minister told parliament on 15 November 2026 that the cost had been fixed at Tk 1,54,000 [MP0459]. Both governments say the employer pays, yet people are told to keep Tk 6 to 7.5 lakh ready, and an MP told parliament the same day that it costs Tk 7 to 8 lakh [A0097, G0011, YD0010, MP0460]. Much less than the headline wage reaches home: a 2024 permission letter leaves food to the worker, and over 40% of income goes on the mess (shared lodging), transport and food [W0035, YF0002].

> **Need to know**
> - What does a plantation worker take home after food in 2026?

#### The cap

No cap is set for 2026 private-agency recruitment: the agencies proposed at most Tk 2.5 to 3 lakh, the figure the minister expects, and the state minister says nobody followed the old cap [YD0007, YA0066, G0012]. On 15 November 2026 the expatriates' welfare minister, Ariful Haque Chowdhury, told parliament that the cost of sending a worker to Malaysia had been cut and fixed at Tk 1,54,000, and that strict action would be taken against anyone who collects more or breaks the government's arrangements [MP0459]. In April 2026 both governments said employers will bear the full cost [A0097]. Malaysia officially follows an "employer pays" policy for recruitment costs, but under the current system it is still hard to implement, and the money changing hands in the Bangladeshi recruitment market runs to several hundred crore ringgit while the real cost of recruitment is far lower, The Edge reported in June 2026 [MP0362, MP0363]. The old cap, in the Ministry's July 2022 order, was Tk 78,990 for the worker's costs inside Bangladesh, with the employer paying everything in Malaysia, airfare both ways included (Step 1, "Before paying") [R0008, R0009, W0027]. Some 2024 permission letters set less, Tk 41,240 or Tk 38,710 (Step 1, item 6) [W0028, W0034]. For the BOESL zero-cost route, see Status.

> **Need to know**
> - Is the Tk 1,54,000 the minister announced on 15 November 2026 [MP0459] a cap on what a private agency may take, in which order or notice is it set, and what does it cover?

#### What people really pay

For 2026, brokers collecting passports tell people to keep Tk 7 to 7.5 lakh ready, and a listed agency's owner says no less than Tk 6 lakh [G0011, YD0010]; members of BAIRA (Bangladesh Association of International Recruiting Agencies) said in September 2026 that workers may have to spend Tk 6 to 7 lakh, and the opposition party Jamaat-e-Islami said on 23 August 2026 it feared the cost could reach that (see Who controls it) [MB0033, MB0034, MB0022, MB0023]. An MP, Atikur Rahman Mujahid, told parliament on 15 November 2026 that sending a worker to Malaysia currently costs Tk 7 to 8 lakh, and asked whether a separate parliamentary committee could check that workers go at the fixed Tk 1,54,000 [MP0460, MP0461].

In 2022-24 the average was Tk 5.44 lakh, with press and workers' groups giving Tk 3.5 to 6 lakh [A0001, YF0021, B0124]. BAIRA's joint secretary general, Fakhrul Islam, said in March 2024 that though a worker was supposed to go for Tk 80,000, nobody could go for less than Tk 4.5 lakh [MA0006]. Other figures for that round:

- **May 2024:** the managing director of PKB (Probashi Kallyan Bank, the government's bank for migrants), Mojibur Rahman, said people going through recruiting agencies were spending Tk 4 to 5 lakh [MP0045].
- **31 May 2024:** at least 10 Malaysia-bound passengers at Dhaka airport told Prothom Alo they had paid Tk 5 to 7 lakh each to the firms sending them [MP0059].
- **June 2024:** Prothom Alo put the average at Tk 5.5 lakh against the Tk 78,990 cap [MP0077].
- **August 2025:** BCSM said that, by the government's white paper on the economy, press reports and ACC allegations, each worker sent in 2022, 2023 and 2024 was charged five to six times the government-fixed cost, and that a syndicate of ten operated in 2017 and 2018 without anyone ever being tried [MP0614, MP0615].
- **June 2026:** The Edge reported that the problem peaked in 2023, when many workers spent up to USD 6,600 each (about Tk 8 lakh, as Prothom Alo printed it) to reach Malaysia and then found that the jobs promised did not exist [MP0359].

Workers call the price the চুক্তি (deal) struck with the broker: "went on a Tk 5 lakh deal" [YF0034, YF0038].

#### Where a Tk 5.5 lakh deal goes

Official fees are about Tk 15,000; the rest pays for the visa trade (the demand letter, the syndicate toll and agents) and usually the airfare, the visa and the medical [A0002, A0028, R0008, YI0025, YI0022]. The 2022-24 pieces reported per worker [A0002, A0028, YE0020, YE0018, Z0164, R0008, YE0019]:

- the demand letter: Tk 1.6 to 2 lakh to middlemen in Malaysia;
- the syndicate toll: Tk 1.07 to 1.52 lakh;
- agents in Bangladesh: about Tk 1.42 lakh, of which an unauthorised middleman takes Tk 50,000 to 1 lakh;
- official fees: about Tk 15,000
  - medical Tk 8,000
  - welfare fee Tk 5,500
  - insurance Tk 1,000
  - clearance Tk 250
  - source tax Tk 800 to 1,200

Together these come to about Tk 4.2 to 5.1 lakh [A0002, A0028, YE0020, Z0164, R0008]. The deal usually also covers the airfare (Tk 45,000 in BOESL's own 2025 costing), the visa and the medical, though the MoU puts all three on the employer [YI0025, YI0022, YI0023, W0027]. Even official items get padded, such as the medical (Step 2) [D0021]; Prothom Alo reported in March 2024 that a business in medical tests ran under the same ring of 100 agencies that took Tk 1.5 lakh a worker, and that many who paid Tk 4 to 5 lakh on average found no job on arrival [MA0004]. When each part is paid: see How it is paid.

**The syndicate's share.** In the August 2025 money-laundering case filed by the CID (the police Criminal Investigation Department), Five M International is alleged to have taken Tk 1.5 lakh a worker, of which Tk 1,07,510 was laundered (see The ACC and CID cases) [MA0117, MA0118]. How the toll was taken in: see How the toll was collected. In total:

- BAIRA members said in March 2024 that the ring alone took Tk 1.5 lakh a worker, more than Tk 6,000 crore on over four lakh workers, and that the corridor made Tk 18,000 crore of business in the previous year and a half [MA0005].
- People in the sector told Prothom Alo in May 2024 that sending about 4.5 lakh people in a year and a half made Tk 24,000 crore of business, about Tk 20,000 crore of it above the government-fixed fee, including more than Tk 6,500 crore of syndicate fee at Tk 1.5 lakh a head [MA0042]; Prothom Alo repeated the Tk 24,000 crore and the Tk 5,44,000 average against the Tk 78,990 cap in July 2026 [MB0013, MB0014].
- Prothom Alo reported in September 2024 the allegation that about Tk 5,000 crore was laundered to Malaysia as syndicate fee at Tk 1 lakh a worker and another Tk 3,750 crore through the visa trade, at least Tk 8,750 crore in all, a large share of the USD 1.12 billion (about Tk 11,000 crore) Bangladesh Bank recorded as remittance from Malaysia in the 2023-24 fiscal year [MP0179, MP0180].

For 2026, BAIRA members said that on top of a syndicate fee of RM5,500 (Tk 1,68,000), buying each demand letter, that is each visa, will cost RM8,000 to 9,000 (Tk 2,65,000 to 2,75,000), so more than Tk 4,30,000 a worker will go to Malaysia [MB0035, MB0036, A0026].

#### How it is paid

Stages: the deal is paid in stages, for visa processing, the medical and other costs, much of it to a broker [YF0031, YI0022]. A delay raises the price: a Cumilla man's Tk 3.6 lakh became Tk 5.7 lakh [YF0036].

> **Need to know**
> - In 2026, how much of the চুক্তি is paid with the passport, how much when the calling visa comes, and how much before the flight or the BMET card?

Receipts: few are given. Down payments for the passport and medical carry none, and workers get receipts for little of the rest [YF0028, YF0031, YF0032]. Before flying, some workers are made to sign, on video, that they paid only the official fee; in 2025 police cited those signatures to close a trafficking case (see Done to workers) [A0061, YA0029].

#### How they borrow

Nearly all borrow (96% in a 2024 survey of Malaysia workers), most (82%) with two or more loans [A0064]. A typical package is a land mortgage (জমি বন্ধক), cattle sold and loans from relatives: a Natore farmer mortgaged his 16 decimals for Tk 1 lakh and sold four cows for Tk 4.5 lakh to pay a Paltan agency Tk 5.5 lakh, then could not fly [YF0026, YF0027]. Informal loans are usually paid straight to the broker, so nothing records them [A0066, YF0030]. Workers in Malaysia borrow at 10% a month to renew their permits; a microcredit NGO in Bangladesh may charge at most 24% a year, the cap set by the MRA (Microcredit Regulatory Authority) [YG0001, YI0021]. The government's PKB (Probashi Kallyan Bank) loan comes only after the visa, and many can't find its guarantors (Step 1, "Before paying") [B0017, G0039, G0040].

Cases:

- **November 2023, Meherpur:** the wife of one of the Gangni men held in Kuala Lumpur (see Which employer) said he went after selling one decimal of their homestead land and borrowing Tk 3 lakh at high interest [MP0021].
- **About 2023, Abdus Salam:** his mother, Taslima Begum, said in October 2024 that she borrowed Tk 3.5 lakh at interest from various people to send him to Malaysia about a year and a half earlier through her brother Abu Taher; he could not work for his first six months and sent some money within a year, and she had repaid Tk 1.5 lakh including interest and still owed lenders Tk 2 lakh [MP0222].

> **Need to know**
> - What monthly rate does a village moneylender (মহাজন) charge on a Malaysia loan in 2026?
> - What are the usual terms of a land mortgage taken for one: the amount per decimal, who works the land, and when it is returned?

#### What the job pays: a correct payslip

A correct payslip shows basic pay of at least RM1,700 a month (about Tk 52,000), plus overtime at least 1.5 times the hourly rate, 2 times on a rest day and 3 times on a public holiday, up to 104 hours a month (Your rights at work) [Z0137, M0001, M0013, M0020, YI0014]. The deductions allowed [I0014, I0015, M0170, J0090, M0050]:

- 2% for EPF (Employees Provident Fund, Malaysia's retirement fund), about RM34 on the basic, compulsory since October 2025 wages and matched by the employer's 2%;
- 0.5% for SOCSO (Malaysia's social security);
- rent of at most RM100 a month;
- no more than half the wage in total.

Both EPF shares are paid back in full when you leave for good (Leaving at the end of the job) [I0018, I0037]. The levy, the pass renewal and the FOMEMA medical are the employer's to pay [L0068, M0144]. At a Pahang plywood factory in 2026, workers on 8-hour contracts got RM65 a day for 12-hour days: the minimum day rate for normal hours alone, so four hours a day went unpaid [F0031, Z0137]. What workers are made to pay instead: see What goes unpaid.

#### What goes unpaid

43% got less than promised in the 2024 survey [A0064]. The usual losses:

- Overtime unpaid or paid at a flat rate (the Pahang case, see What the job pays: a correct payslip) [F0031].
- Wages held for months, often before the company closes: up to eight months at Kawaguchi, nearly three at Aerofoam in 2026 [F0046, F0038].
- The employer's costs taken from pay: the levy, the yearly renewal, hostel charges, uniforms (RM500 in one case) and "flight-ticket deposits" [F0082, YG0001, J0059, F0039].
- Time with no job, earning nothing while the loan runs [YF0034].

Workers who ask for the promised pay have been fired or told to do more overtime [YF0012]. How to claim: If you aren't paid.

#### Money sent home

Most send through banks, and legal transfers earn the government's 2.5% incentive (how: see Sending money home); over 16% use hundi (an unlicensed money agent), which is an offence in Bangladesh [YF0014, B0104, H0045]. Hundi survives because it converts at the kerb rate, which in 2022 was well above the bank rate, because the hundi man comes to the mess, and because an employer holding your passport shuts you out of banks [YI0006, YI0007, YF0024, YF0016]. The risk: families could not cash money received in agent and MFS (mobile money) accounts frozen for hundi, and Malaysian Immigration arrests hundi operators working behind restaurants and shops [B0103, H0045, H0044, T0019].

Remittances from Malaysia were USD 102 crore in 2021-22, 112 crore in 2022-23, 160 crore in 2023-24 and 280 crore in 2024-25, then USD 26 crore in July 2025 and 27 crore each in August and September, Prothom Alo reported [MP0741]. By Bangladesh Bank data they grew 35% in 2023-24, which people in the sector put down to more workers going; by BMET data, 2023, with more than three and a half lakh workers, was the highest year for departures to Malaysia between 2004 and 2023 [MP0130, MP0131, MP0132].

> **Need to know**
> - Was the 2023-24 remittance from Malaysia USD 1.12 billion [MP0180] or USD 160 crore (1.6 billion) [MP0741]?

#### How long the debt takes

At the 2022-24 price of about Tk 5.44 lakh the cost equals at least a year's wages; counting living costs, repayment takes from 21-22 months to three years [YF0047, YF0003, YF0025]. The 2026 asking price of Tk 6 to 7.5 lakh is higher (see What people really pay) [G0011, YD0010]. Meanwhile 73% spend half or more of their monthly pay on repayments [A0064]. Every month without work or pay, and every renewal the worker is made to pay, adds to it [YF0034, YG0001]. Two cases:

- Farhad Hossain, killed on a Seremban construction site in July 2025 two and a half years after going to Malaysia on a Tk 5 lakh loan: his father said the loan had still not been fully repaid [MP0597, MP0598, MP0599].
- Mominul Islam of Chuadanga, a shop worker at Times Square, Kuala Lumpur, said in December 2025 that he borrowed Tk 5 lakh to go, earns RM2,200 (Tk 66,000) a month, and has already repaid the loan [MP0742, MP0743].

### What ties you to the employer

Your work pass ties you to one employer for everything. The PLKS (Pas Lawatan Kerja Sementara, the work pass; the "permit", পারমিট) names one employer; only that employer can bring you in, renew the pass and send you home, and work for anyone else breaks it [W0052, L0079, L0071, K0025, YG0001]. The standard contract is 3 years, renewable, with 90 days' probation, and the air ticket home is due only after the contract is completed [W0035, W0003].

> **Need to know**
> - Do outsourcing or shell companies still sponsor Bangladeshis on PLKS to work anywhere in 2026?
> - What does such a PLKS "visa" sell for?

- **Enter:** only your employer can bring you in. The employer holds the quota and applies for your VDR (Visa Dengan Rujukan, the calling visa approval); you wait in Bangladesh until it is approved, and can check its status yourself on Immigration's MyIMMs site (Step 3, item 2) [W0043, W0045]. The employer must collect you at the airport within 6 hours of landing ([After you arrive, item 1](#after-you-arrive-item-1)) [L0041].
- **Stay:** the FOMEMA medical in Malaysia decides whether you can stay. The employer has 30 days after arrival to take you to a FOMEMA clinic, and the PLKS is issued only if you pass; if you fail, you are sent home on a Check-Out Memo with the debt unpaid [L0042, L0043]. Since December 2023 the check is yearly [D0026]. FOMEMA has rejected workers for diabetes, high blood pressure and high cholesterol, and neither worker nor employer gets the report ([After you arrive, item 2](#after-you-arrive-item-2)) [D0027, D0028, D0029].

  > **Need to know**
  > - Does any agency refund part of the package when a worker fails FOMEMA, and is this ever written into the চুক্তি?

- **Renew:** only your employer can renew the pass, once a year, up to 10 years (see How long you can stay and renewing) [L0079, L0044, L0050]. Workers call it ভিসা রিনিউ (visa renewal) [YI0016]. The official cost in manufacturing, construction or services is about RM2,055 (Tk 63,000): the RM1,850 levy, RM60 for the pass, RM125 processing and RM20 visa, all payable by the employer [M0227, M0226, M0228, L0068]. Workers report paying RM2,500 to 3,200 a year (Tk 77,000 to 98,000) [YG0001].
- **Quit:** to quit, you give 2 months' notice or pay 2 months' wages, and pay your own fare home; Immigration normally cancels the pass at once [L0145, M0066, J0035].

  > **Need to know**
  > - Once Immigration cancels a quitting worker's pass, how many days does he have to leave?

- **Change employer:** you can't change employer on your own; only the employer can apply, and only if it closes, goes bankrupt, is restructured or dies (If you want to change employer) [L0055, M0123, M0124]. A Kuala Lumpur migrant lawyer calls a legal job change almost impossible and a main reason so many end up undocumented [YG0015].
- **Leave:** only your employer can send you home. The employer applies for the Check-Out Memo and since 2025 must be present at the exit point (Leaving at the end of the job) [L0071, H0029]. Its RM500 security bond for you is refunded only if you leave on that memo and forfeited if you run [M0228, M0229, M0216]. That bond is why employers hold passports and demand deposits: at a Pahang plywood factory in 2026, workers had to pay RM2,500 before going home on annual leave [F0033]. Missing the agreed return date counts as running away [L0058].

  > **Need to know**
  > - Can a worker get the Check-Out Memo without the employer?

#### Which law covers you

The pass falls under the Immigration Act, so any breach of its conditions voids it at once [K0025]. The work falls under the Employment Act 1955 in Peninsular Malaysia and Labuan, and separate Labour Ordinances in Sabah and Sarawak [M0060]. In Sarawak a foreign worker is bound by a Labour Department licence to the job, workplace and licence holder named on it [L0168, L0173].

#### Your passport in practice

The law forbids the employer to keep it ([After you arrive, item 4](#after-you-arrive-item-4)) [L0149, K0045], but employers routinely take passports on arrival "for safekeeping" without a receipt [J0069, F0084]. In an IOM (International Organization for Migration) study of 40 workers who became undocumented, every one had their passport held by the employer or agency [YG0011]. Held passports are ransomed: the Kuala Lumpur company that gave about 600 Bangladeshis no work asked Tk 1.7 to 1.8 lakh each for theirs in 2024 [YG0003]. Some have got passports back by complaining to the authorities [F0042].

#### What running away or an expired pass does to you

- **Running away (lari, Malay for "run").** The employer reports you, you are blacklisted, the bond is forfeited and the pass cancelled; some employers file false reports near the contract's end to avoid the fare home, or after a complaint (If your employer reports you absent (lari)) [M0216, J0035, J0065, J0066]. You become what Malaysia calls PATI (Pendatang Asing Tanpa Izin, foreigner without permission) and Bangladeshis call অবৈধ (illegal) [YG0008, YG0004].
- **An expired pass** makes you an overstayer: fine, jail or a compound, then removal and a blacklist (see What can get you jailed or deported) [K0015, R0004, K0018, H0015]. Most undocumented workers got there this way: all 40 in the IOM study arrived legally and left their jobs because of exploitation, and recruitment debt kept them in Malaysia afterwards [YG0010, YG0013]. Selim Reza of North South University wrote in June 2026 that most undocumented Bangladeshis in Malaysia are not involved in crime but lost legal status through employer fraud, company closure, visa-renewal complications or administrative weakness [MP0348].
- **Buying your way back to legal.** No legalisation programme is open; the last, RTK (the Labour Recalibration Programme), ended on 31 December 2023 [YB0014]. Past rounds were a market: agents charged RM6,000 to 10,000 against an official maximum of RM3,900, and rings sold fake renewals and PLKS stickers at RM2,500 to 6,000 [YG0018, YG0019, YG0023, YG0024]. The only official way out is home under PRM 2.0, with a compound of RM300 for breaking pass conditions or RM500 for overstaying (Going home without valid papers) [L0120]. Some can't use it (see Going home without valid papers, Step 3, Who can't use PRM 2.0) [H0008, T0002, T0003]. What the governments have said since:
  - **15 May 2025:** asked to legalise undocumented Bangladeshis, Malaysia replied that it does so from time to time, last in the previous year, but cannot for those whose visas have expired; Bangladesh asked it to consider them too, since workers often cannot renew because of the employer, and Malaysia said it would consider it [MP0551, MP0552].
  - **2 August 2025:** the adviser Asif Nazrul said some Bangladeshis in Malaysia had messaged him complaining that undocumented workers there could not become legal while more people were being brought from Bangladesh [MP0604, MP0605].
  - **December 2025:** Tasneem Siddiqui, founding chair of RMMRU, said that instead of showcase manpower export the government should push to regularise undocumented Bangladeshis, as Malaysia has done before, and that if the market is again handed to a syndicate, workers will not in practice be able to join jobs [MP0745, MP0746].
  - **22 June 2026:** at the two Prime Ministers' talks, according to the Prime Minister's spokesperson Mahdi Amin, the two sides discussed humanely giving legal status to Bangladeshis in irregular status or in prison in Malaysia, or returning them safely home; no programme was announced [MP0381].

> **Need to know**

> - Did Malaysia run any legalisation in 2024, as it told Bangladesh in May 2025 [MP0551], or was RTK, which ended on 31 December 2023, the last [YB0014]?

### What goes wrong most, and what people wrongly believe

Money owed is the commonest complaint: unpaid, late or cut wages, then jobs that don't exist or don't match the promise [J0067, F0022, YH0005, A0014]. Before departure the loss is money taken and no departure, and most complainants get back less than half of it (see If an agency or broker takes your money) [G0021, A0074]. Employers sent 20,331 Bangladeshi workers home in 2022 and 23,065 in 2023, by Malaysia's Home Ministry's count [MP0646]. On 22 June 2026 Malaysia's Prime Minister, Anwar Ibrahim, referring to allegations of exploitation and abuse of Bangladeshi workers in Malaysia, said exploiting workers, treating them badly and using them only for self-interest could not be accepted, and that both countries must lead in making recruitment transparent [MP0391, MP0392].

Most workers go on what they were told rather than on a contract, so a false belief goes unchecked until it costs them: OKUP (Ovibashi Karmi Unnayan Program, a Bangladeshi migrants' organisation) finds 75% leave without having seen a contract [A0082].

#### What people believe, and the facts

- **"The talks mean the market is open."** Selim Reza of North South University's Centre for Migration Studies wrote on 30 June 2026 that before the Prime Minister's June visit much of the media and some government officials gave the impression that reopening was decided and only a formal announcement remained, but that the visit's main result was agreement in principle on transparent, fair, lower-cost recruitment, not an immediate opening [MP0398, MP0399]. Where it stands now: see Status.
- **"I've paid the full price, so a job is waiting."** Not guaranteed: quotas are issued beyond real need, so many who paid found no job (see The quota; see Which employer) [YE0013, YE0014]; in the case of the 161 promised construction jobs, the employer also asked RM6,000 to return their passports [YB0018]. Activists and researchers estimate 100,000 to 200,000 of about 800,000 Bangladeshis in Malaysia are jobless, unpaid and in debt [L0191]. Before paying the bulk, the employer can be checked on SSM (Malaysia's company registry) and the calling visa on MyIMMs (Step 1, item 7; Step 3, item 2) [W0067, G0055, W0045].
- **"Passing the medical in Dhaka means I'm in."** FOMEMA in Malaysia decides, every year, and failing it means going home with the debt (see What ties you to the employer) [L0043, D0026].
- **"Go on a tourist visa; the work permit gets done there."** Travel agents sell exactly this: a Paltan travel agency owner promised work permits, sent two people on visit visas, held them and demanded Tk 5 lakh from their families [O0032]. A visit pass allows no work and can't become a work pass inside Malaysia; the worker must be outside Malaysia while the employer applies (Changing visa type) [V0104, S0090]. In one July 2025 operation 123 Bangladeshis holding tourist visas were refused entry at KLIA (Kuala Lumpur International Airport) [N0013].
- **"Malaysia will legalise us again."** When a rumour of an "RTK 3.0" spread in January 2025, Immigration's Director General said no decision had been taken, and rings sold fake places in past rounds [YH0014, YH0015, YG0021]. What the governments have said since: see What running away or an expired pass does to you.
- **"A student visa lets you work."** Prospective students are routinely told this [S0135]. Students may work only in breaks of over 7 days, up to 20 hours a week, in a few listed jobs, and those breaking the rules are deported (see [Student visa (Student Pass), Can you work?](#can-you-work)) [S0012, S0013, S0039, O0019]. In August 2025 a 2-minute-19-second TikTok video by Haim Hilman Abdullah, chairman of Kedah state's committee on industry, investment, higher education, science, technology and innovation, claimed that 10,000 Bangladeshi students in Malaysia would get jobs [MP0622, MP0623]. On 23 August 2025 Malaysia's Higher Education Minister, Zambry Abd Kadir, said reports that a "Graduate Pass" would be given to 10,000 Bangladeshi students to let them work were untrue and baseless, that no such agreement was made during Chief Adviser Muhammad Yunus's visit, and that Malaysia had given no consent; he called Haim Hilman's claim irresponsible [MP0619, MP0620, MP0621, MP0624].
- **"The approved agency list is on Facebook."** Posts in late 2025 that Malaysia had approved 25 Bangladeshi agencies were false; Malaysia had told neither the High Commission nor the Ministry (see Done to workers) [W0021].

#### Irregular routes, and where an irregular stay ends

- **Overland through Thailand: it ends in arrest, in Thailand or in Malaysia.** Brokers fly people to Cambodia; they cross into Thailand on foot, are moved in stages to the south, and are brought into Malaysia across the Kelantan border [YH0010, YH0019, YH0007]. The price is Tk 7 lakh (April 2026) or up to RM15,000 (July 2026), more than the legal route [YH0009, YH0006, A0001]. Caught on the way, they are charged in Thailand: in April 2026, 21 Bangladeshis found hidden in a resort in southern Thailand were charged there with illegal entry [YH0009, YH0020]. Caught after crossing, they go to an immigration depot: in July 2026, 15 caught in a Cheras transit house on the day they crossed were taken to the Putrajaya depot [YH0021, YH0008].
- **By sea from Teknaf and Cox's Bazar.** A Prothom Alo editorial of 7 September 2025 said Cox's Bazar has become a regular sea route for human trafficking, used from 21 points in Teknaf, Ukhia, Moheshkhali, Cox's Bazar Sadar and other upazilas, and that besides Rohingya from the camps, traffickers lure a section of young Bangladeshis onto it [MP0648, MP0649, MP0650]. The gangs become active after the monsoon and before winter, when the sea is calm, and such incidents rise in winter [MP0651, MP0682]. The editorial said deaths in the Thai jungle on the way to Malaysia, torture by traffickers and deaths when food and water ran out on trawlers have been reported, and that more than 657 Rohingya died on sea voyages in 2024 [MP0652]. Rohingya pay syndicates Tk 3,52,000 to 3,85,000 (USD 3,200 to 3,500) each, AFP reported in November 2025, citing Malaysian media, and the boats are often weak with no life jackets, or many passengers don't wear them [MP0708, MP0709]. UNHCR (the UN refugee agency) and IOM said on 16 July 2026 that about 300 Rohingya and Bangladeshis had been reported dead or missing in the Andaman Sea and Bay of Bengal so far in 2026, on a route to Malaysia, Indonesia and Thailand that has become the world's deadliest sea route for refugees and migrants, after about 900 Rohingya dead or missing in 2025 [MP0406, MP0407, MP0408, MP0410]. Cases:
  - **June 2023 to April 2025:** teenagers and young men from Teknaf and Ukhiya, some aged 16, lured by brokers with promises of well-paid jobs in Malaysia, set off by sea without telling their families and were detained in Myanmar for illegal entry, serving 22 months in prison [MP0477, MP0478, MP0479]. Families learned of the arrest only about a month after it; 20 were brought back on the navy ship BNS Samudra Avijan from Yangon, arriving at Chattogram on 15 April 2025, a year after the families submitted papers to the government [MP0480, MP0481, MP0482].
  - **10 May 2025:** the BGB (Border Guard Bangladesh) rescued 14 teenagers and young men from a house in Uttar Lambori, Teknaf, where, it said, they had been locked up to be trafficked to Malaysia by sea and held hostage to extort money from their families; no one was arrested [MP0523, MP0524, MP0525]. Two of them, Mohammad Kasem and Mohin Uddin of Cox's Bazar town, said a CNG auto-rickshaw driver tricked them into the house while they were visiting Inani; they were beaten, Tk 1 lakh each was extorted from their families, and they were held for 18 days while the captors tried to sell them to brokers for the sea route [MP0526, MP0527, MP0528].
  - **September 2025:** two young men from Munshiganj Sadar, Amit Hasan and Manik Mia, said a Cox's Bazar resident, Md Nur, invited them to visit, took them to his house in Marichya, Ukhia, on 2 September, then put them in an auto-rickshaw for a "trip to Teknaf" and handed them to traffickers, who held them in a hill den to be taken to Thailand by sea and beat them if they tried to phone home; they were rescued 20 days later [MP0667, MP0668, MP0669]. On 15 September the BGB's Ramu sector commander, Colonel Md Mohiuddin Ahmed, said 47 traffickers had been arrested during trafficking to Malaysia by sea and 21 more who fled were being sought [MP0661, MP0662]. Operations in Teknaf that month: the BGB detained 12 traffickers and rescued 11 people on 16 September; the Coast Guard rescued 66 on 18 September; RAB (Rapid Action Battalion) and the BGB arrested three armed traffickers and rescued 84 on 22 September; five kidnapped people were rescued from the Kachchhapia hills on 23 September [MP0679, MP0680, MP0681].
    - The 66 of 18 September, 44 of them women and children, were held in a den deep in the hills of Kachchhapia, Baharchhara union; the Coast Guard said most were Rohingya, some lured with promises of a better life in Malaysia and others kidnapped and held for ransom [MP0663, MP0664, MP0665].
    - The 84 were rescued in a roughly 12-hour operation dated 21 September in the first report, 66 of them from the Rohingya camps; officials said two had been taken there by force and the rest brought by lures, many with promises of jobs in Malaysia and Thailand and some women with promises of marriage, and the Teknaf-2 BGB commander said the traffickers fired at the forces and tried to use some victims as shields [MP0670, MP0671, MP0672]. One young woman rescued said she had been married by mobile phone on 15 September to a Rohingya man in Malaysia, who told her to meet some people to go by sea; no money was asked of her, and her "husband" was to pay something once she reached Malaysia [MP0673, MP0674]. By 22 September, 65 traffickers had been arrested and 161 people rescued in Teknaf that year, law-enforcement sources said [MP0675].
    - On 25 September the BGB rescued three men from Khuniapalong Marichya, Ramu, Robiul Alam (18), Md Tazim (18) and Md Ismail (30), from a hill in Baraitali, Teknaf; it said they had been lured with the promise of being taken to Malaysia by sea via Thailand, that ransom was being sought from their families, and that they were threatened with being sold to a trafficking group in Malaysia if it was not paid [MP0676, MP0677, MP0678].
  - **October 2025:** the Coast Guard rescued eight women and children near Jummapara hill in Baharchhara on 1 October, and at midnight that day a Coast Guard and Navy team rescued 21 people, mostly Rohingya, held for trafficking to Malaysia by sea near Karachipara ghat [MP0679, MP0680, MP0681]. On 3 October the Coast Guard and Navy rescued 38 more (18 women, 12 men, 8 children, mostly Rohingya) from the hills next to Kachchhapia government primary school; by then law-enforcement agencies had arrested 70 gang members and rescued 239 people in Teknaf in 2025 [MP0682, MP0683, MP0684].
  - **November 2025:** over 100 migrants went missing when a boat sank near the Thailand-Malaysia sea border; by 9 November Malaysia's maritime enforcement agency (MMEA) had rescued 12 alive, one of them Bangladeshi, and recovered one body near Langkawi, and Kedah police said the boat had left Buthidaung in Myanmar with about 300 aboard, who split into three boats of about 100 near the coast, as the organisers had taught them, to evade the Malaysian authorities [MP0705, MP0706, MP0707, MP0411].
  - **7 December 2025:** the Coast Guard rescued seven men and women from Jaliapalong, Ukhia, from a den in the Baharchhara hills and detained three men with a homemade firearm; an officer said they had been brought to be trafficked to Malaysia by sea with promises of jobs for the men and marriage for the women, and were held while the traffickers tried to extract money [MP0728, MP0729, MP0730].
  - **3 January 2026:** the Navy ship BNS Swadhinata chased and stopped a wooden boat about 30 miles south-west of St Martin's Island carrying people illegally to Malaysia, rescued 263 including women and children, and detained 10 gang members; the boat had no basic life-saving equipment and not enough food or water [MP0751, MP0752, MP0753].
  - **16 January 2026:** Sumaiya Akter, 18, was shot dead in Noakhaliapara, Baharchhara, when armed men from the hills attacked a den where a gang had gathered people to send to Malaysia by sea, a local union member said; the district police spokesman said coastal Baharchhara has become the main base of trafficking gangs [MP0761, MP0762, MP0763].
  - **8 April 2026:** a Malaysia-bound trawler that left the Teknaf coast with at least 250 Bangladeshi and Rohingya men and women sank in the Andaman Sea in strong wind, rough sea and overloading; nine were rescued, and the Coast Guard said no trace of the missing or the wreck had been found [MP0324, MP0325].
  - **Late June 2026:** two boats carrying more than 500 people, mostly Rohingya including some from camps in Bangladesh, left Rakhine and are feared lost, UNHCR and IOM said [MP0409].

- **Through the airport, on a tourist visa: if spotted, it ends in refusal at the airport and a flight back.** Travellers refused entry are sent back within 24 hours [N0010]. A "counter setting" syndicate sold entry packages on Facebook and Telegram: agents booked dates with immigration officers by WhatsApp, and the group, arriving as tourists, was sent to a "special counter", with officers taking RM200 to RM2,500 per worker; the MACC (Malaysian Anti-Corruption Commission) arrested 60 people over it in 2024, 49 of them immigration officers [YH0011, YH0023, YH0012, YH0013]. On 17 March 2025 Malaysia's Border Control and Protection Agency (AKPS) detained 15 Bangladeshis at KLIA who wore cricket uniforms and showed an organiser's letter for a 21 to 23 March tournament; AKPS found the letter fake, no such event, and a named "sponsor" who knew nothing of it, and said it believed they were part of a syndicate entering for another purpose [MP0296, MP0297, MP0298].

- **Overstaying, or staying on after running away: it ends in arrest, the depot and removal.** Undocumented work is there to be had: Prothom Alo reported in December 2025 that because Malaysia sets a minimum wage, many businesses hire undocumented workers to save costs, Bangladeshis the largest group among them, mostly in construction, services and manufacturing [MP0735, MP0736]. Detention is in a crowded immigration depot, where 465 detainees died in 2021 to 2025, and removal comes with no appeal (see What can get you jailed or deported) [K0019, F0056, F0054]. Deportees pay their own tickets or their families or embassy do, and they are blacklisted [H0020, H0021]. Forged papers bought to stay carry jail and caning (see Tried by workers) [D0045, L0088]. Crackdowns follow crises: a 21 May 2020 OHCHR statement, cited in a Prothom Alo column of 3 April 2026 by Mohammad Jalal Uddin Sikder of North South University, said that during COVID-19 migrant workers in Malaysia were blamed for spreading the virus, detained, and many sent back [MP0311].
